## Overview

NeuroQuery is a model-based quantitative meta-analysis tool for the neuroscience literature that uses smooth ridge regression on term-document matrices with word embeddings to predict patterns of brain activation from cognitive or behavioral queries. Developed by Jérôme Dockès, Bertrand Thirion, and the Parietal team at INRIA (with contributions from Stanford University's Poldrack Lab), NeuroQuery serves as a web-based search engine and analytical framework that maps free-text queries about cognitive processes onto estimated probability distributions over brain locations [[Dockès et al. 2020]](https://elifesciences.org/articles/53385). Unlike traditional keyword-based literature searches, NeuroQuery employs a trained statistical model that captures the relationship between over 13,000 published fMRI experiments from PubMed Central and the cognitive/behavioral terms used to describe them, enabling researchers to generate data-driven hypotheses about which brain regions are likely to be involved in specific mental functions.

## Key Features

The core innovation of NeuroQuery lies in its statistical model, which learns the association strength between cognitive terms (such as "working memory," "emotion regulation," or "visual perception") and brain coordinates from a large corpus of published neuroimaging experiments [[Dockès et al. 2020]](https://elifesciences.org/articles/53385). When a user enters a query, the model computes a probabilistic activation map by aggregating information from all experiments containing semantically related terms, using word2vec-style embeddings trained on neuroscience literature to capture semantic relationships between terms. This means that queries for related but non-identical terms (e.g., "moving" versus "grasping") will return spatially correlated but distinct activation maps, capturing the graded and overlapping nature of cognitive representations in the brain. This semantic similarity capability allows NeuroQuery to generalize to novel terms not explicitly seen in the training corpus—a key differentiator from earlier coordinate-based meta-analysis tools.

NeuroQuery primarily supports forward inference, taking a cognitive or behavioral query and returning a spatial probability map indicating which brain regions are most consistently activated in studies involving that concept. The tool provides several outputs: a three-dimensional brain map showing the probability of activation in each voxel, a list of published studies that contributed to the map, and confidence intervals that reflect the heterogeneity across studies. While the system can perform inverse inference (submitting activation maps to receive cognitive interpretations), this capability is more limited than the forward inference direction and is a capability more fully developed in the related Neurosynth tool [[Yarkoni et al. 2011]](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnins.2011.00009/full).

## Relationship to TVB

NeuroQuery provides a complementary approach to [[the-virtual-brain]] by offering empirical constraints derived from the aggregate neuroimaging literature. While [[TVB]] simulates the dynamics of large-scale brain networks using biophysically principled [[neural-mass-models]] and [[structural-connectivity]] matrices derived from [[diffusion-imaging]] data, NeuroQuery synthesizes functional activation patterns from thousands of fMRI studies to indicate which brain regions are commonly involved in specific cognitive domains. Researchers can use NeuroQuery maps to inform the selection of brain regions or cognitive states to include in TVB simulations, particularly when building [[personalized-brain-modeling]] pipelines that aim to link individual connectivity patterns to cognitive phenotypes. The combination of NeuroQuery's meta-analytic functional maps with TVB's dynamic modeling framework represents a powerful approach for bridging descriptive neuroimaging findings with mechanistic models of brain function.

## Technical Background

The underlying model in NeuroQuery employs smooth ridge regression on term-document matrices, deliberately avoiding topic models like Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) in its production implementation [[Dockès et al. 2020]](https://elifesciences.org/articles/53385). The model learns a matrix of associations between cognitive terms and brain locations from a training corpus of over 13,000 neuroimaging experiments drawn from PubMed Central, capturing the fact that certain terms (e.g., "pain") are consistently associated with particular regions (e.g., anterior cingulate cortex, insula) while other terms show more distributed or variable patterns. The mathematical formulation treats the probability of activation at location r given query q as a weighted sum over all cognitive terms w in the query, where the weights are determined by both the term's learned association with location r and its semantic similarity to the query (as captured by word embeddings trained on the neuroscience literature). This approach naturally handles synonymy (different words mapping to similar brain patterns) and polysemy (words with multiple meanings showing complex activation patterns). Earlier research explored LDA-style topic modeling for neuroimaging meta-analysis [[Poldrack et al. 2012]](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnins.2012.00152/full), but the final NeuroQuery system adopted a different approach based on ridge regression for improved predictive accuracy.

## Related Software and Approaches

NeuroQuery belongs to a family of meta-analysis tools for neuroimaging that share the goal of synthesizing the rapidly growing fMRI literature. [[neurosynth]] is perhaps the most closely related tool, using a similar coordinate-based approach with forward and reverse inference statistics to generate activation maps [[Yarkoni et al. 2011]](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnins.2011.00009/full). Both tools have been used extensively for network neuroscience analyses, parcellation validation, and hypothesis generation, though they differ in their treatment of cognitive synonyms and the statistical framework used for map generation. A key differentiating feature of NeuroQuery is its ability to handle arbitrary-length text queries and its use of word embeddings to generalize to novel terms not seen during training.

Other notable related tools include Activation Likelihood Estimation (ALE), which uses a coordinate-based meta-analysis method that accounts for spatial uncertainty in reported activation peaks, and [[brainmap]], which provides the curated taxonomy of cognitive terms that supports structured queries. For downstream analysis, NeuroQuery outputs are often processed using [[nilearn]] (a Python library for statistical analysis of neuroimaging data) or visualized in [[fsleyes]] (the FSL viewing tool). The maps can also be compared with [[structural-connectivity]] matrices from [[diffusion-imaging]] tractography to identify relationships between functional co-activation patterns and anatomical connectivity.

## Key Papers

The foundational NeuroQuery methodology was introduced in Dockès, Poldrack, Primet, Gözükan, Yarkoni, Suchanek, Thirion, and Varoquaux (2020), published in eLife, which demonstrated that smooth ridge regression with word embeddings could successfully predict brain activation patterns from cognitive queries and established the theoretical framework for model-based meta-analysis [[Dockès et al. 2020]](https://elifesciences.org/articles/53385). This work built upon earlier efforts to apply statistical models to the neuroimaging literature, including a 2012 paper by Poldrack et al. that explored latent Dirichlet allocation for extracting cognitive topics from full-text publications—a precursor that informed the semantic modeling approach later employed in NeuroQuery [[Poldrack et al. 2012]](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnins.2012.00152/full). The tool has been validated through numerous applications, including studies showing that model predictions agree with established neuroscience knowledge (e.g., primary visual cortex activation for visual stimuli) and discoveries of novel functional associations later confirmed by targeted experiments.

## Open Questions and Limitations

Despite its utility, NeuroQuery has notable limitations that researchers must consider. The model is trained on experiments from PubMed Central through approximately 2020, meaning it may not reflect even more recent findings or evolving methodological standards in fMRI acquisition and analysis. The coordinate-based approach discards the full spatial information available in univariate statistical maps, potentially missing important patterns of distributed activation. Additionally, the model cannot distinguish between activation patterns associated with different task contexts or cognitive states within broad categories, limiting its ability to capture nuanced cognitive subprocesses. While NeuroQuery excels at forward inference (text to brain maps), its inverse inference capability (brain map to cognition) is more limited compared to dedicated reverse inference tools like Neurosynth.

Current developments aim to address these limitations by incorporating larger and more recent corpora of neuroimaging data, integrating multivariate pattern analysis approaches, and connecting NeuroQuery maps with dynamic brain modeling frameworks like TVB to create closed-loop pipelines from literature synthesis to computational prediction.
