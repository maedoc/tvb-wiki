# BrainCoGS

## Overview

BrainCoGS (Circuits of Cognitive Systems) is a multi-institutional, NIH-funded research consortium that represents one of the most ambitious attempts to understand the neural basis of working memory and decision-making through coordinated experimental and computational approaches. Founded with funding from the BRAIN Initiative (Brain Research through Advancing Innovative Neurotechnologies), the project brings together seven laboratory groups from Princeton University and the University of California, Davis, working in concert to dissect the complete circuit mechanisms underlying evidence accumulation and choice behavior. The fundamental scientific question driving BrainCoGS is deceptively simple yet profound: how does the brain—as a distributed network of billions of neurons—temporarily hold information in mind and use it to guide decisions? This question sits at the intersection of cognitive neuroscience, systems neuroscience, and computational modeling, making BrainCoGS a uniquely integrative enterprise.

The consortium's approach distinguishes itself through its explicit integration of multiple scales of investigation, from single-neuron electrophysiology to whole-cortex functional imaging, combined with large-scale anatomical reconstruction via electron microscopy. Unlike more focused experimental programs that typically study one brain region or one cell type in isolation, BrainCoGS adopts a truly systems-level perspective, treating working memory and decision-making as emergent properties of interactions across dozens of cortical and subcortical areas. This ambition requires not only sophisticated experimental techniques but also novel computational frameworks for analyzing high-dimensional neural data and building biophysically realistic multi-region models that can explain and predict behavior.

## Key Features

BrainCoGS is organized into five interconnected project areas, each addressing a distinct aspect of the working memory problem through complementary methodologies. The **Perturbations & Behavior** component, led by Carlos Brody, Ilana Witten, Sam Wang, and David Tank, employs optogenetic inactivation combined with quantitative behavioral tasks to establish causal relationships between specific brain regions and cognitive function. Using tasks where rodents must accumulate sensory evidence over time to guide decisions, these experiments can dissociate the contributions of different brain areas to evidence loading, maintenance, and retrieval—phases that unfold on sub-second timescales and require precisely timed perturbations to distinguish.

The **Cortical Neural Coding & Dynamics** project, also led by David Tank with Carlos Brody, characterizes neural activity across the entire dorsal cortex during evidence accumulation using cellular-resolution two-photon calcium imaging with custom-built mesoscopes. This component has produced unprecedented datasets documenting how dozens of cortical regions represent task-relevant variables during decision-making, revealing both distributed coding schemes and specialized sub-populations. Crucially, the project also employs widefield fluorescence imaging to capture simultaneous activity across all cortical areas, providing the first complete spatiotemporal map of neocortical dynamics during a cognitive task.

The **Subcortical Neural Coding & Dynamics** component, led by Ilana Witten and Sam Wang, extends the investigation to dopamine neurons in the ventral tegmental area, cerebellar Purkinje cells, striatal medium spiny neurons, and hippocampal pyramidal cells. This focus on subcortical structures reflects the consortium's recognition that working memory cannot be explained by cortex alone—the basal ganglia, cerebellum, and hippocampus all contribute to memory maintenance and decision formation in ways that remain incompletely understood.

The **Neuronal Interactions** project, led by Sebastian Seung, David Tank, and Jonathan Pillow, represents perhaps the most technically ambitious aspect of BrainCoGS: combining in-vivo functional imaging with subsequent serial-section electron microscopy to reconstruct complete neural circuits at synaptic resolution. This "circuitomics" approach promises to provide the anatomical wiring diagrams that can constrain computational models, turning correlation into mechanism.

Finally, the **Analysis & Modeling** component, led by Jonathan Pillow and Mark Goldman, develops the statistical and computational frameworks needed to make sense of the consortium's massive datasets. This includes advanced latent variable methods for dimensionality reduction, generalized linear models for linking neural activity to behavior, and biophysically realistic network models constrained by both the experimental data and anatomical connectomics.

## Relationship to TVB

Both approaches treat brain dynamics as emerging from recurrent interactions between regions, and both rely on computational models to bridge the gap between neural activity and behavior. The constraint-based modeling philosophy at the heart of BrainCoGS, where models must simultaneously explain neural recordings, perturbation results, and anatomical connectivity, parallels [[the-virtual-brain]]'s approach to personalized brain modeling where individual [[structural-connectivity]] data constrains simulations. The consortium's emphasis on deriving [[effective-connectivity]] from perturbation experiments rather than relying solely on correlational [[functional-connectivity]] mirrors [[dynamic-causal-modeling]] frameworks that use Bayesian model comparison to infer directed interactions between brain areas.

There are also direct technical connections: the latent variable methods and dynamical systems analysis techniques pioneered in BrainCoGS for interpreting large-scale neural recordings find direct application in TVB's [[parameter-estimation]] pipelines. Conversely, TVB's frameworks for modeling white-matter transmission delays and region-to-region coupling could inform the connectivity analyses in BrainCoGS. Researchers working at the intersection of these traditions increasingly employ the experimental paradigms and analysis tools developed in BRAIN Initiative projects like BrainCoGS to validate and constrain whole-brain [[computational-neuroscience]] models, creating a natural bridge between the mesoscale circuit understanding and the macroscale connectome-based simulations that [[whole-brain-modeling]] enables.

## Relationship to Other Projects

BrainCoGS occupies a unique position in the landscape of large-scale neuroscience initiatives, distinguishing itself from other BRAIN Initiative efforts through its explicit focus on working memory rather than sensation, motor control, or innate behaviors. The project has significant overlap with [[nest]]-based modeling efforts and [[brian]] [[spiking-neural-networks]] in terms of the biophysical realism pursued, though BrainCoGS emphasizes direct integration with experimental data over exploratory simulation. The consortium's emphasis on combining multiple modalities—optogenetics, imaging, electrophysiology, anatomy—mirrors the multi-modal philosophy of projects like the [[human-connectome-project]], but with a cognitive rather than developmental focus.

The [[brain-connectivity-toolbox]] provides complementary graph-theoretic tools for analyzing the structural and functional networks that BrainCoGS maps at unprecedented resolution. The consortium's emphasis on systematic exploration of analytical choices in network neuroscience has influenced tool development in the broader community, as exemplified by the **Comet Toolbox** which implements comprehensive multiverse analysis for dynamic functional connectivity from fMRI data, directly addressing the robustness concerns that BrainCoGS researchers have identified in their own multi-modal datasets. Data management standards like [[bids]] and [[datalad]], along with neurodata formats like nwb, enable the large-scale datasets generated by BrainCoGS to be shared and reused across the neuroscience community, creating interoperability with downstream analysis frameworks including [[the-virtual-brain]], [[brain-dynamics-toolbox]], and other whole-brain simulators.

## Key Papers

The theoretical foundation for BrainCoGS draws on decades of computational neuroscience research on accumulator models of decision-making, including classic work from the participating Princeton laboratories on the bounds of evidence accumulation and related frameworks that have become standard for fitting behavioral data in [[computational-neuroscience]]. The Brody lab's foundational papers on rodent decision-making and evidence accumulation (e.g., Brunton et al., 2013; Erlich et al., 2011) establish the behavioral paradigm that the consortium employs. The Tank lab's work on neural representations of sensory evidence and choice variables in parietal cortex provides key benchmarks for the coding schemes the consortium seeks to characterize across the cortex.

The Seung laboratory's pioneering applications of machine learning to electron microscopy reconstruction (e.g., Seung & Sompolinsky, 2013) establish the theoretical framework for the circuitomics approach. The Pillow lab's extensive body of work on statistical methods for neural population coding—including spike train decoding, latent variable models, and optimal stimulus estimation (e.g., Pillow et al., 2008; Park et al., 2014)—provides the analytical toolkit for interpreting the consortium's large-scale recordings. The Goldman lab's studies of prefrontal working memory circuits (e.g., Goldman-Rakic, 1995; Goldman et al., 2009) provide the conceptual foundation for understanding how persistent activity emerges from recurrent network dynamics.

Major empirical papers documenting the consortium's cortical and subcortical imaging results are expected as the project reaches maturity, along with papers describing the complete electron microscopy reconstruction of decision circuits in cortical areas. More broadly, the field has seen significant methodological advancement in brain network analysis, with tools like **BRAPH 2.0 Genesis** providing open-source capabilities for brain connectivity analysis that complement the proprietary and custom pipelines developed within BrainCoGS. Similarly, physics-informed approaches to whole-brain dynamics that embed observed activity into higher-dimensional representations—such as complex-valued models augmenting neural signals with latent "dark signals" representing conjugate momenta—represent emerging theoretical frameworks that bridge the mesoscale circuit understanding and the macroscale connectome-based simulations that [[whole-brain-modeling]] enables.

## Related Software

The BrainCoGS ecosystem encompasses several software tools developed specifically for the project's goals. The consortium maintains repositories on GitHub including the **brain_atlas_toolkit** for navigating hierarchical brain atlases, **Microscope** for two-photon microscope design documentation, and **HPC_manifolds** for analysis of high-dimensional neural activity manifolds. These tools complement rather than compete with established whole-brain simulation platforms like [[the-virtual-brain]] or [[nest]], serving instead as data analysis infrastructure that could ultimately feed into larger-scale modeling frameworks. The project's emphasis on reproducible, open-source analysis pipelines reflects the broader trend in computational neuroscience toward transparent, community-available methods that can be applied beyond the original experimental context.
