The Calamity Atlas is a brain [[parcellation]] scheme oriented toward [[whole-brain-modeling]] of pathological network states. It belongs to a growing class of atlases that aim to move beyond healthy-population anatomical boundaries to provide spatial references better suited for computational studies of neurological and psychiatric disorders.

## Motivation and Context

Digital brain atlases serve as indispensable infrastructure for [[connectomics]] research, providing the stereotactic references that enable reproducible mapping of [[structural-connectivity|structural]] and [[functional-connectivity|functional]] data into simulation environments. [[raw/papers/semanticscholar-9538aa9a62c5.md|Kohnen et al. (2025)]] describe how modern atlas frameworks support advanced visualization, connectivity overlays, and direct interfacing with whole-brain simulation pipelines. Their digitization of 1722 anatomical contours spanning cortical, subcortical, and non-neuronal regions illustrates the spatial detail required for reliable connectome simulation, while their validation against stereological data confirms the reliability of such delineations. [[raw/papers/semanticscholar-9538aa9a62c5.md|Kohnen et al. (2025)]] further emphasize that atlas choice fundamentally shapes how connectivity is represented in computational models, a consideration that extends directly to human whole-brain modeling workflows.

For disease modeling, the selection of spatial references carries additional weight. [[raw/papers/semanticscholar-565d9037ee06.md|Goh and Liu (2025)]] propose the Integrative Neuroclinical Mapping Framework (INMF), which processes high-dimensional [[fmri]] and structural MRI data alongside dynamic symptom measurements to map relationships between neural circuit dysfunctions and psychopathology. Core to their approach is graph-based network analysis that models the dynamic interplay between brain networks and symptom domains. [[raw/papers/semanticscholar-565d9037ee06.md|Goh and Liu (2025)]] demonstrate in a proof-of-concept application spanning [[schizophrenia-models|schizophrenia]] and major depressive disorder that capturing pathological network organization requires spatial references reflecting disease-related connectivity alterations. [[raw/papers/semanticscholar-565d9037ee06.md|Goh and Liu (2025)]] further show how their framework identifies transdiagnostic biotypes that are more homogeneous than traditional diagnoses, underscoring why an atlas oriented toward disease modeling can provide node definitions aligned with patient cohort network perturbations.

## Key Features

Disease-oriented atlases aim to bridge the gap between healthy-population [[brain-parcellations]] and the network reorganization observed in patient cohorts. [[raw/papers/semanticscholar-565d9037ee06.md|Goh and Liu (2025)]] emphasize that frameworks mapping disordered brain states require multi-stage analytical pipelines combining data harmonization, manifold learning for low-dimensional latent structure discovery, and graph-based network analysis. [[raw/papers/semanticscholar-9538aa9a62c5.md|Kohnen et al. (2025)]] illustrate that rigorous atlas construction demands bilateral completeness and volumetric validation across regions spanning several orders of magnitude. [[raw/papers/semanticscholar-9538aa9a62c5.md|Kohnen et al. (2025)]] also note that integration with simulation environments enables hierarchical ontologies and quantitative analyses, properties that support whole-brain modeling when coupled with platforms such as [[the-virtual-brain|TVB]] to enable [[personalized-brain-modeling]] informed by disease-specific connectivity patterns rather than generic neuroanatomy.

## Relationship to TVB

[[raw/papers/sanz-leon-2013.md|Sanz Leon et al. (2013)]] introduce The Virtual Brain (TVB) as an open-source platform that combines empirical structural connectivity from [[diffusion-imaging|diffusion MRI]] [[tractography]] with [[neural-mass-models]] to simulate large-scale brain network dynamics. [[raw/papers/sanz-leon-2013.md|Sanz Leon et al. (2013)]] emphasize that TVB supports forward models for [[eeg]], [[meg]], and [[fmri]], allowing simulated signals to be compared directly against empirical recordings. [[raw/papers/sanz-leon-2013.md|Sanz Leon et al. (2013)]] describe how TVB's region-based modeling framework expects a set of regions with associated coordinates and labels, which any imported atlas must provide.

[[raw/papers/sanz-leon-2013.md|Sanz Leon et al. (2013)]] further describe how the platform enables personalized whole-brain models from neuroimaging data, meaning that disease-specific atlases can be integrated into TVB's structural connectivity pipeline. [[raw/papers/semanticscholar-565d9037ee06.md|Goh and Liu (2025)]] demonstrate that disease-specific spatial references are essential for capturing pathological network organization in psychiatric conditions, while [[raw/papers/semanticscholar-9538aa9a62c5.md|Kohnen et al. (2025)]] show that atlas frameworks must interface directly with simulation environments to be useful for connectome research. Together, these considerations highlight how atlas selection is not merely a preprocessing decision but a fundamental modeling choice that influences the biological interpretation of simulated dynamics.
