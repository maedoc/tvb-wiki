                                      S1. FUNDAMENTAL FIELD THEORY

                               S1.1. Physical Motivation of the Lagrangian Density

   The Lagrangian density L is defined as: Its structure follows the Ginzburg-Landau paradigm [1], in which a
macroscopic order parameter is governed by competing gradient and potential terms. The assignment of a metabolic
cost to each term reflects the fundamental link between information processing and thermodynamic dissipation [2].
The variational form of the resulting free energy is formally analogous to the free-energy functionals used in biological
self-organization theory [3], though the present framework does not implement the variational Bayesian inference of
the free-energy principle; the analogy is structural rather than mechanistic.

                        S8. LIFE-CYCLE DYNAMICS AND PHASE TRAJECTORIES

                              S8.1. Mapping Human Development onto Phase Space


  The human cognitive life cycle can be represented as a trajectory through the (α, K) phase space, with each
developmental stage corresponding to a distinct region characterized by the control parameter Γ = K/α. This
mapping provides a physical framework for understanding cognitive development, aging, and pathological decline.


                                    Developmental Annealing (Infancy → Adulthood)


   During early development, the system begins in the fluid phase with high cognitive temperature α and low structural
stiffness K, corresponding to Γ ≪ 1. This regime is characterized by:

   • Extreme Plasticity: Susceptibility χ ∼ K −3/2 is maximal, allowing rapid structural reconfiguration in response
     to environmental input.

   • Low Capacity: Information capacity C ∼

   As learning proceeds, structural stiffness K increases through synaptic consolidation and myelination, while cogni-
tive temperature α decreases through developmental regulation of neural noise. The trajectory moves along a nearly
diagonal path toward the critical line Γ ≈ 1, representing the transition from fluid exploration to structured cognition.


                                             Metabolic Pinning (Adulthood)


  Upon reaching adulthood, the brain actively maintains Γ ≈ 1 through coordinated regulation of both α and K.
This metabolic pinning occurs along a ridge in the (α, K) plane where information capacity C remains nearly constant
despite ongoing structural changes. The pinning mechanism has several key features:

   1. Active Regulation: Metabolic flux J continuously adjusts α to compensate for gradual increases in K due to
      accumulated experience and ongoing structural consolidation.

   The long-term fate of the cognitive system depends critically on whether metabolic support can keep pace with

   Pathway 1: Normal Aging (Crystallization)

but gradually drifts toward higher K as α can no longer be fully compensated. This leads to:

                                             S8.3. Clinical Implications

  This framework provides a physical basis for several clinical observations:
   • Functional Reserve: The plateau region near Γ ≈ 1 allows the brain to compensate for substantial structural
     loss (decreasing K) through metabolic upregulation (maintaining or increasing α), explaining why cognitive
     function can remain intact despite significant neuropathology.
   • Precipitous Decline: Once K < Kc , the divergence of χ implies that arbitrarily small perturbations trigger
     large-scale reorganization. The system rapidly transitions from compensated function to global delocalization.
   • Irreversibility: The delocalized state corresponds to a topological change in the cognitive field configuration.
     Without the structural scaffolding (K) to support recoherence, recovery is thermodynamically disfavored within
     the present framework.
