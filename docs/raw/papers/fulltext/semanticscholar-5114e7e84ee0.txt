                                                 Introduction
According to the World Health Organization, mental health is a state of well-being in which
individuals can cope with normal life stresses, work productively, and contribute to their
communities. Depression in adolescence often presents with a unique set of symptoms,
including excessive anxiety, emotional instability, and difficulty expressing feelings.
Additionally, depressed adolescents may exhibit behaviors such as academic decline, self-
harm, and suicidal tendencies, which can further exacerbate the condition. The causes of
depression in adolescence are multifaceted, stemming from a combination of genetic,

environmental, and psycho-social factors, including stress, major life events, and the presence
of other mental health disorders.

The negative impact of anxiety and depression on adolescent development is well-
documented. These disorders can significantly impair academic performance, social
functioning, and overall quality of life, ultimately affecting the transition into adulthood.
Furthermore, evidence suggests that individuals who experience depression or anxiety during
adolescence are at an increased risk of experiencing these conditions in adulthood,
highlighting the importance of early intervention and prevention efforts (Dobson et al., 2021).

Mental health of adolescents is a growing global concern, with approximately 20% affected by
depression or anxiety, including in low- and middle-income countries (Lin et al., 2014). It is a
significant but often overlooked issue globally. The transition from childhood to adolescence
brings about numerous physiological, psychological, and social changes, increasing
vulnerability to mental health disorders. Adolescent depression is a significant public health
concern, with far-reaching consequences for individuals, families, and communities
(Anderson et al., 2014).

Early intervention can be crucial in reducing the long-term burden of the disease. By
recognizing the signs and symptoms of depression in secondary school students, educators
and mental health professionals can play a pivotal role in providing appropriate support and
initiating timely treatment. The effectiveness of various treatment approaches, such as
pharmacotherapy, cognitive-behavioral therapy, and family-based interventions, has been the
subject of ongoing research, with the goal of identifying the most efficacious and tailored
strategies for this population (Cai et al., 2021).

In sub-Saharan Africa, adolescents are particularly vulnerable to mental health issues such as
depression and anxiety (Sodi et al., 2022). A systematic review of the literature reveals that
adolescents in this region face an elevated risk of mental disorders, yet mental healthcare
systems are often poorly resourced and lack the necessary policies to address these challenges.
In Kenya, this issue is even more critical, as up to 30% of adolescents may be experiencing
depression, anxiety, or substance use, driven largely by adverse social determinants such as
poor living conditions, limited economic opportunities, and exposure to violence (Myers et al.,
2021). Despite the urgent need, mental health services for Kenyan adolescents are scarce, with
few schools having dedicated professionals to provide the necessary support (Vreeman et al.,
2017).

Given the high prevalence and profound impact of depression among Kenyan adolescents,
there is a critical need for comprehensive research and targeted interventions. Current efforts
are hindered by limited data and resources, resulting in underdiagnosis and inadequate care.
Advanced predictive methods, such as Bayesian neural networks, hold promise in this context
by managing uncertainty and modeling complex nonlinear relationships in biopsychosocial
risk factors. Using these techniques could improve the identification and understanding of
depression patterns, informing more effective strategies to support adolescent mental health
in Kenya (Hankin, 2015).

                                          Method

Data
This study used secondary data obtained from Open Science Framework
(https://osf.io/download/nqhtw/). The data consists of depressive symptoms (PHQ), anxiety
symptoms (GAD), socio-demographic information, and psychosocial wellbeing indices which
include measures of gratitude, multidimensional scale of perceived social support (MSSS),
happiness and optimism (EPOCH) and perceived academic control (PAC). The size of raw
data was 2192.

Univariate Analysis
To determine statistically significant variables, this study adopted Chi-Square tests for
categorical variables and point biserial correlation for continuous variables, all against
depression status, which is a dichotomous dependent variable. Variables declared statistically
significant had their individual p-value < 0.05.

Bayesian Neural Network

Architectural Framework of the Bayesian Neural Network Model
The architectural framework summarizes the process of Bayesian Neural Network. It begins
with model setup, which involved defining the stochastic and functional models, and the
training data. The functional model is the predictive function. The next step, inference,
detailing Bayes by Backrop. The final section will provide outputs, that are posterior
distribution, marginal predictions, and summary that includes estimates and uncertainty
quantification. The architectural framework is illustrated in Figure 1.

Functional Model of the Bayesian Neural Network Model
The functional model was a feed forward neural network with both Rectified Linear Unit
(ReLU) as the preferred nonlinear activation function within the hidden layers, while the
sigmoid activation for the output layer. ReLU is less computational and powerful than
sigmoid and tanh functions. It can easily backpropagate the errors and easily activate multi-
layer hidden layer.

Stochastic Model of the Bayesian Neural Network Model

and Andermann (2020) there is no theoretical argument that makes any approach better than

stochasticity (Buntine, 1994).

Model Assessment
Bayesian Neural Networks’ challenge in evaluating their performance is, they do not directly
output a point estimate prediction but a conditional probability distribution p(y|x,D), from
which an optimal estimate can later be extracted (Jospin et al., 2022). This implied the need to
assess the model’s predictive performance and calibration.
To evaluate the effectiveness of the predictive model, the study focused on the following
metrics; Accuracy, Recall, Precision and F1 score.
Random Forest was selected as the comparator model because it is a well-established and high
performing baseline for classification tasks, particularly on tabular and small and moderate
datasets, where it demonstrates strong robustness and limited sensitivity to hyperparameter
tuning. Their performances were compared against each other.

Model Diagnostic
To diagnose the predictive model, calibration curve was plotted to assess how well the model’s
predicted probabilities align with actual observed outcomes. Also, Brier score was calculated
to measure the model’s capacity of neither underfitting nor overfitting.

Bayesian Neural Network Predictive Model
This study developed a classification Bayesian Neural Network model where the dependent
variable was depression status. Components of our model are; ReLU as the activation function
within hidden layers while sigmoid function at the output layer, Gaussian prior with
parameters (µ = 0, σ = 0.05) and a feed forward neural network with three hidden layers.

                                         Discussion
This study found that 27.3% of the adolescents were depressed. This is in aligned with the
study done by (Myers et al., 2021).
The univariate analysis indicated that father’s and mother’s education are statistically
significant risk factors. This aligned with the study done by Evans et al. (2018), where they
found that parental factors played significant roles in the development of depression and
anxiety in adolescents.
Rates of depression were found to be higher in female adolescents, consistent with
international epidemiology due to hormonal factors, social stresses, and psychosocial
expectations based on gender (Thapar et al., 2022). The vulnerability revealed in higher forms
might be due to constant academic pressures, stress of exams, and uncertainty about future
prospects, a finding consistent with recent longitudinal studies (Barendse et al., 2023).
Continuous psychosocial variables of anxiety (GAD), loneliness (UCLA), perceived social
support (MSSS), gratitude, academic self-concept (PCS), and positive youth development
(EPOCH) all emerged as strong correlates of depression. Anxiety and feelings of loneliness
had the highest effect size, consistent with research-based models of depression as mutually
reinforcing conditions in adolescence (McElroy et al., 2018).
                                                                FRONTIERS IN RESEARCH        11


Protective factors like gratitude, perceived social support, and positive youth development
were negatively related to the risk of depression. These results support current
conceptualizations of resilience that propose mental health to be promoted from a position of
strength (Bandeira et al., 2023). Perceived academic competence showed one of the strongest
associations among the categorical variables, indicating the central role of self-efficacy and
school related identity in adolescent mental health. This aligns with a finding in educational
psychology literature (Putwain et al., 2023).
The Bayesian Neural Network generally outperforms the Random Forest across most
performance metrics. Bayesian Neural Network achieves higher accuracy (0.8013 vs 0.7529),
F1 score (0.8761 vs 0.8218), recall (0.9654 vs 0.7830), ROC-AUC (0.813 vs 0.800), and PR-AUC
(0.913 vs 0.911), indicating stronger overall predictive ability and a better balance between
sensitivity and specificity. The Random Forest only surpasses Bayesian Neural Network in
precision (0.8646 vs 0.8021), suggesting it makes fewer false positives but at the expense of
recall. Overall, the Bayesian Neural Network provides more robust performance, particularly
in capturing true positives, making it the better model in this comparison. The Bayesian
Neural Network’s capacity to effectively handle uncertainty and model complex nonlinear
relationships inherent in mental health data likely contributes to this superior performance.
This aligns with a study conducted by (Shridhar et al., 2018). This suggests that Bayesian
Neural Networks are highly suitable for predictive modeling in adolescent depression
contexts where the uncertainty of the data is considerable. This study aligns with study done
by Fara et al. (2022) where they demonstrated their Bayesian Neural Network predictive
model’s performance as robust depression linked speech-based MDD prediction tool.
Furthermore, the Bayesian Neural Network’s uncertainty diagnostics indicate that the model
appropriately represents predictive uncertainty, with confidence higher at extreme prediction
probabilities and uncertainty peaking near ambiguous cases. This is consistent with the study
conducted by Heek and Kalchbrenner (2019), where they identified Bayesian Neural
Network’s advantage ability is to quantify model uncertainty, crucial in medical diagnosis.
This feature is valuable in practical deployment, as it can inform clinicians and stakeholders
about cases needing further evaluation beyond model predictions.
Overall, this study provides compelling evidence that advanced probabilistic machine
learning models can meaningfully enhance early identification of adolescent depression,
complementing traditional assessment approaches and supporting more targeted, timely
interventions.

                             Conclusion and Recommendation
This study successfully developed and validated a Bayesian Neural Network model that can
predict depression risk among Kenyan adolescents with high accuracy and sensitivity.
Statistically significant categorical variables are; gender, form, co-curricular, perceived
academic abilities, financial status and both parents’ education. Significant continuous
variables are; GAD, MSSS, EPOCH, age, PCS academic, Gratitude and UCLA. Psychosocial
factors are strongest predictors; Anxiety (GAD) and loneliness (UCLA) show the largest
effects. This implies that emotional and social experiences strongly influence depression risk.
Academic self-perception matters; perceived academic abilities have the strongest categorical
impact. This implies that low self-evaluation of performance significantly increases
12    E. KAGEREKI ET AL.


vulnerability. Socio-economic factors play a modest role; financial status and parental
education show small but significant effects. Protective psychological factors reduce risk.
Gratitude, social support (MSSS), and positive youth development (EPOCH) buffer against
depression.
The model’s superior performance compared to a Random Forest classifier demonstrates the
strength of Bayesian approaches in handling uncertainty and complex data relationships,
making them particularly suitable for mental health risk prediction in resource-constrained
settings. The findings underscore the urgent need for enhanced mental health screening,
support systems, and resources.
This study recommends interventions targeting emotional regulation, social support, and
academic self-perception may be particularly effective in mitigating depression among
adolescents. Strategies that reduce anxiety and loneliness while promoting supportive social
environments and positive youth development appear especially promising. This study
recommends developing a dynamic Bayesian Neural Network predictive model that
continuously updates its predictions over time as new data becomes available.
