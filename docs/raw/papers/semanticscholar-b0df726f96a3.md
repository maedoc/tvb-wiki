## Abstract

Attention Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) is associated with a range of neurocognitive impairments, including diminished working memory and difficulties in suppressing behaviors, which negatively affect academic and social outcomes in children. Previous studies have primarily focused on either structural or functional brain differences using uni-modal MRI, often neglecting the interplay between brain structure and function and its implications for the neurological mechanisms underlying ADHD. Additionally, most research to date has emphasized identifying associations between brain regions and ADHD rather than exploring causal relationships. Understanding the causal mechanisms underlying the neurological behaviors affecting ADHD is crucial for identifying potential neural targets for intervention. This study aims to address gaps in current research by investigating the causal relationship between brain connectivity and ADHD using multi-modal imaging and genetic techniques. We leverage data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study, including 6,381 subjects aged 9–10, and Structure-Function Coupling (SFC), a novel brain profile metric that measures how anatomical connections support or influence cognitive processes and neural interactions, utilizing both diffusion and functional MRI. A one-sample Mendelian Randomization (MR) analysis is implemented using genetic variants as instrumental variables to examine the causal effects of the couplings on ADHD symptomatology. Notable functional networks heavily involved in the identified brain regions include the Default Mode Network (DMN) and the Cingulo-Opercular Network (CON). These networks play critical roles in cognitive control, self-referential thinking, and task-related cognitive processes, deficiencies of which contribute to the core symptoms of ADHD. Leveraging the largest adolescent cohort in the literature, this study is, to the best of our knowledge, the first to investigate the causal relationship between the brain structure-function interplay and ADHD by utilizing multi-modal neuroimaging and genetic information. Our results are consistent with previous ADHD findings and further reveal potential causal pathways that could help identify neural targets for ADHD intervention.
