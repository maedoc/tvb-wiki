# Structural Core

A set of highly interconnected hub regions that form a central backbone for brain communication.

## Definition

The structural core refers to a group of brain regions that are densely interconnected and occupy a central position in the brain's structural network. These regions form a "rich club" of high-degree nodes that facilitate global information integration.

## Discovery

patric hagmann|Hagmann et al. (2008) identified the structural core using diffusion spectrum imaging and graph analysis:
- Located in posterior medial and parietal cortex
- Includes precuneus, posterior cingulate, and superior parietal regions
- Forms the most densely interconnected part of the brain

## Characteristics

### Anatomical Location
- **Precuneus**: Central hub region
- **Posterior cingulate cortex**: Default mode network hub
- **Superior parietal cortex**: Association cortex
- **Isthmus cingulate**: Connects parietal and temporal regions

### Network Properties
- **High Degree**: Many connections to other regions
- **High Betweenness**: Central to network paths
- **Rich-Club**: Dense interconnections among core regions
- **High Efficiency**: Facilitates rapid information transfer

## Functional Significance

### Information Integration
- Serves as a central "backbone" for brain communication
- Enables efficient information transfer between modules
- Supports global workspace function

### Resting-State Connectivity
- Core regions show strong [[functional-connectivity]] at rest
- Part of the default mode network
- Active during internally-directed cognition

### Metabolism
- High baseline metabolic activity
- High vascular density
- Part of the "default mode" of brain function

## Relationship to Other Concepts

### Rich-Club
The structural core is a manifestation of rich-club organization – high-degree nodes that are densely interconnected.

### Default Mode Network
Core regions overlap substantially with the default mode network, suggesting a structural basis for the DMN.

### Connector Hubs
Core regions function as connector hubs, linking different functional modules.

## Clinical Relevance

### Disease
- **Alzheimer's**: Core regions show early amyloid deposition
- **Disorders of Consciousness**: Core damage impairs awareness
- **Psychiatric Disorders**: Altered core connectivity

### Neuroimaging
- Used as seed regions for [[resting-state]] analysis
- Important for connectivity mapping
- Target for brain stimulation studies

# Mapping the Structural Core of Human Cerebral Cortex

**Authors:** Patric Hagmann, Leila Cammoun, Xavier Gigandet, Reto Meuli, Christopher J. Honey, Van J. Wedeen, Olaf Sporns  
**Year:** 2008  
**Venue:** PLoS Biology

## Key Contributions

- Identified a structural core of highly interconnected regions in posterior medial and parietal cortex
- Demonstrated dense interconnectivity between core regions using diffusion MRI and graph analysis
- Showed the core's central role in information integration
- Established relationship between structural connectivity and resting-state functional connectivity

## Abstract Summary

Using diffusion spectrum imaging and graph theory analysis, the authors identified a structural core of highly interconnected regions in the human cerebral cortex. This core, located in posterior medial and parietal cortex, contains the majority of connector hubs and shows high efficiency for information transfer. Core regions are also strongly functionally coupled.

## Significance

This study provided early evidence for a "rich club" organization in the human brain - a set of highly interconnected hub regions that form a structural backbone. This concept has become central to understanding brain network organization.

## Abstract

Abstract Impaired olfactory function in older adults is associated with memory decline and is a biomarker of Alzheimer’s disease (AD). However, the structural brain foundation underlying olfactory impairment and its link to memory function remains largely unknown. We address this gap by reconstructing the structural olfactory network, that is, white-matter connections between the primary olfactory cortex (POC) and the whole brain. Through applying multivariate analyses in a population-based sample (n = 137), we investigate the relationships among age, the olfactory network, and olfactory and cognitive function. Our findings reveal that the POC subregions have distinct structural connectivity profiles with the entire brain. Older age was associated with weaker connectivity strength between the POC and nearby regions, suggesting a reorganization of the olfactory network in older adults. Structural connectivity of the olfactory network was associated with behavioral performance in odor identification, episodic memory, and odor threshold, but not processing speed or working memory. Notably, connections including the olfactory tubercle (TUB)-caudate, TUB-amygdala, and olfactory nucleus (AON)-hippocampus were important for both olfaction and episodic memory function, suggesting a common neural basis across cognitive domains. Our study expands on previous research of single brain regions or individual white-matter tracts, uncovering the structural underpinnings of olfactory function at the network level.
