Volume conduction refers to the passive, instantaneous spread of electrical current through biological tissue without signal propagation or delay. In [[neuroimaging]] and [[computational-neuroscience]], it is the physical process by which primary currents generated by neuronal activity—primarily from postsynaptic potentials in pyramidal cells—spread through the head volume and manifest as measurable potentials on the scalp ([[eeg]]) or magnetic fields outside the head ([[meg]]). Unlike propagation along axons, volume conduction involves no neural transmission delay; the electromagnetic field generated by any given set of current sources propagates instantaneously through the conductive medium according to the quasi-static approximation of Maxwell's equations [Nunez & Srinivasan, 2006].

## Physical Basis and Mathematical Formulation

The foundations of volume conduction rest on electromagnetic field theory applied to biological tissue. When postsynaptic currents flow across neuronal membranes, they generate electric fields that obey the Poisson equation for the electric potential φ in a conductor:

<tex>∇·(σ∇φ) = -I_v</tex>

where σ denotes the conductivity tensor of the tissue (which varies across skull, scalp, and brain compartments) and I_v represents the volume current source density [Nunez & Srinivasan, 2006]. This equation captures how current spreads through the heterogeneous head volume—the different conductivities of skull, cerebrospinal fluid, and brain tissue act as filters that shape the spatial distribution of potentials recorded at the scalp.

The forward problem in [[source-localization]] asks: given a known current distribution in the brain, what potentials (EEG) or magnetic fields (MEG) are observed at the sensors? The inverse problem asks the complementary question: given observed EEG or MEG data, what current configuration in the brain produced it? Volume conduction is the physical basis for both problems—the forward model implements the physics of how currents spread through tissue, while the inverse problem must somehow "unmix" the volume-conducted signals to infer their origins.

## Relationship to EEG and MEG

Volume conduction has profound implications for the interpretation of electrophysiological measurements. Because electrical potentials spread instantaneously through the head volume, any scalp electrode records a weighted mixture of activity from many brain regions simultaneously. This mixing means that the spatial resolution of EEG is fundamentally limited—the potentials observed at the scalp represent the summed contribution of all current sources, filtered by the volume conduction properties of the intervening tissue.

Magnetic fields measured by [[meg]] are less affected by volume conduction effects because magnetic fields are less distorted by the skull and scalp [Hämäläinen et al., 1993]. However, the interplay between neural currents and the conductive head volume still shapes the MEG signal, though in a mathematically distinct manner described by the Biot-Savart law. The contrast between EEG and MEG sensitivity to volume conduction is a key motivation for combined EEG-MEG analysis in [[dynamic-causal-modeling]] frameworks and source imaging studies.

## Implications for Whole-Brain Modeling

In [[whole-brain-modeling]] frameworks such as [[the-virtual-brain]], volume conduction is essential for linking neural mass model activity to observable neuroimaging data. Neural mass models such as the [[jansen-rit-model]], [[wong-wang-model]], or [[epileptor]] generate simulated local field potentials representing aggregate synaptic activity in brain regions [Breakspear et al., 2017]. The forward model then applies volume conduction physics to transform these regional signals into simulated scalp EEG or MEG data that can be directly compared with empirical recordings.

This linking is not merely methodological—it has substantive implications for model fitting and validation. When fitting whole-brain models to empirical EEG or resting-state [[functional-connectivity]] data, researchers must account for volume conduction to correctly relate model variables to observations. Conversely, ignoring volume conduction can lead to spurious estimates of functional connectivity, as volume-conducted signals from a single active region can appear as correlated activity in electrodes overlying unrelated brain areas [Schaworonkow & Voytek, 2021].

## Historical Context and Open Questions

The neurophysics of volume conduction was systematically formalized in the classic text *Electric Fields of the Brain: The Neurophysics of EEG* by Paul Nunez and Ramesh Srinivasan, which established the theoretical framework for understanding how brain activity generates scalp potentials [Nunez & Srinivasan, 2006]. This work remains foundational for contemporary source localization algorithms and forward modeling approaches.

Several open questions in the field relate to volume conduction. The accuracy of head models—in particular, the segmentation of different tissue compartments and assignment of conductivity values—remains an active area of research. Individualized head models derived from magnetic resonance imaging can substantially improve source localization accuracy compared to simplified spherical models, but the practical tradeoffs between model complexity and computational tractability continue to be investigated. Additionally, the relationship between [[local-field-potentials]] (LFPs) generated at the cellular level and the scalp potentials resulting from volume conduction connects cellular-scale neuroscience to whole-brain modeling frameworks.
