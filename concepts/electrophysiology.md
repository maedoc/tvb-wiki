Electrophysiology is the study of the electrical properties of biological cells, tissues, and organs. In the context of neuroscience and [[whole-brain modeling]], electrophysiology encompasses the measurement, analysis, and computational modeling of electrical activity generated by neural tissue — from single neurons to large-scale brain networks. This electrical activity manifests across multiple spatial scales: intracellular potentials, [[local-field-potentials]] (LFP) measured by electrodes within brain tissue, scalp-recorded [[eeg]], and the magnetic fields detected by [[meg]]. Understanding electrophysiological signals is essential for linking [[neural-mass-model]] outputs to empirically observable brain dynamics.

## Neurophysiological Basis

The electrical activity measured in electrophysiology originates from ionic currents flowing across neuronal membranes. When postsynaptic potentials summate in the dendrites and cell body of a neuron, they generate transmembrane currents that flow through the extracellular space. These volume currents produce measurable electric fields at the scalp (EEG) and magnetic fields outside the head (MEG). The relationship between underlying neural activity and these far-field measurements is governed by the physics of volume conduction, which is treated in detail by Nunez and Srinivasan in their comprehensive treatise on the neurophysics of EEG. Critically, the forward problem — predicting scalp potentials from neural activity — requires solving the Poisson equation in a volume conductor (the head), with boundary conditions determined by tissue conductivities. This mathematical framework bridges microscopic neural dynamics and macroscopic observable signals.

## Measurement Modalities

Electrophysiological measurements in the human brain are acquired through several complementary modalities. Scalp electroencephalography (EEG) records electric potential differences between electrodes placed on the scalp, primarily reflecting synchronized postsynaptic activity from cortical pyramidal cells oriented perpendicularly to the cortical surface. Magnetoencephalography (MEG) detects the weak magnetic fields (~10–100 fT) generated by intracellular currents flowing tangentially to the skull, offering superior spatial resolution for superficial tangential cortical sources compared to EEG. Intracranial recordings, including stereo-EEG (sEEG) and electrocorticography (ECoG), provide direct measurements from within the brain tissue or on the cortical surface, respectively, with high temporal resolution and signal-to-noise ratio but limited spatial coverage due to their invasive nature.

Recent methodological advances have expanded the toolkit for cerebellar investigations, which have historically been challenging due to the cerebellum's depth and complex folding. The work by Matsubara et al. (2026) demonstrates the application of anatomically informed source modeling to quantify cerebellar signal detectability in both MEG and EEG, systematically investigating how source orientation, sensor-source distance, and head size affect signal-to-noise ratio across patient populations. Their findings reveal that while cerebellar signals remain challenging to detect with conventional clinical systems, optically pumped magnetometer (OPM) configurations optimized for posterior fossa coverage substantially improve sensitivity.

Analysis of electrophysiological data often relies on specialized software toolkits. The [[erplab]] package provides comprehensive workflows for event-related potential (ERP) analysis, enabling preprocessing, baseline correction, artifact rejection, and statistical comparison of experimental conditions. Tools such as [[autoreject]] offer automated rejection and repair of bad trials and sensors. Reproducible research pipelines benefit from unit testing frameworks such as [[sciunit]] [[pyriemann]], which allow researchers to validate electrophysiology processing steps against ground-truth datasets and ensure consistency across analysis versions.

## Relationship to Whole-Brain Modeling

In [[whole-brain modeling]], electrophysiology serves two critical functions: as a source of empirical constraints and as a target for model predictions. Large-scale connectome-based models — such as those implemented in [[the-virtual-brain]] — often incorporate [[neural-mass-model]] or [[neural-field-theory]] formulations that capture the collective dynamics of cortical populations. The outputs of these models, such as mean membrane potentials or firing rates, must be mapped to observable electrophysiological signals through forward modeling. The volume conduction model then predicts the scalp potentials that would be recorded given the simulated neural activity, enabling direct comparison with empirical EEG or MEG data.

This integration is particularly relevant for studying pathological dynamics such as slow waves following brain lesions. Gaglioti et al. (2026) extended a neural mass model of EEG to capture transitions between wake-like and sleep-like dynamics, embedding it in connectome-based networks with virtual lesions. Their work demonstrates how mechanisms of local disfacilitation, topology-dependent propagation, and synchrony-dependent amplification throughout the connectome can reproduce the spatial gradients of post-lesional slow waves observed in patient studies. This approach provides a computational framework for identifying actionable targets for neuromodulation and rehabilitation.

The relationship between [[functional-connectivity]] measured via fMRI and [[functional-connectivity]] derived from electrophysiological signals remains an important open question, as these modalities capture fundamentally different aspects of neural dynamics — the former reflecting hemodynamic responses integrated over seconds, the latter capturing millisecond-scale electrical activity.

## Methodological Considerations

Source localization — the inverse problem of estimating the spatial distribution of neural activity from scalp recordings — relies on biophysical models of volume conduction. Techniques range from simple spherical head models to sophisticated boundary element method (BEM) or finite element method (FEM) models that incorporate individual head geometry from structural MRI. The choice of forward model significantly impacts localization accuracy, particularly for deep sources or in the presence of pathological skull lesions. Additionally, the non-invasive nature of EEG and MEG means that these methods capture weighted averages of activity across large neural populations, requiring careful interpretation regarding the spatial resolution and the distinction between primary current sources and volume currents.
