    Source Localization

    Source localization (also termed source imaging or the electromagnetic inverse problem) is a computational technique in electrophysiology that estimates the locations and sometimes the time courses of intracranial neural generators from scalp recordings of eeg or meg signals. In a whole-brain modeling context, source localization provides the empirical constraints that inform dynamic causal modeling or neural mass models, linking observed macroscopic brain dynamics to underlying mesoscopic network activity.

    Why Source Localization Exists: The Inverse Problem¶

    The fundamental challenge in source localization stems from the physics of volume conduction. Electrical currents generated by post-synaptic potentials in cortical pyramidal neurons propagate through the head's heterogeneous tissues (brain, cerebrospinal fluid, skull, scalp) to produce measurable potentials on the scalp. This is the forward problem: given a known source configuration, compute the scalp potentials. Mathematically, the forward problem is linear and can be expressed as:

    $$\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{G} \mathbf{q} + \mathbf{n}$$

    where $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times T}$ represents the recorded EEG or MEG data at $N$ sensors over $T$ time points, $\mathbf{q} \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times T}$ represents the current source density at $M$ locations (vertices or voxels) in the brain, $\mathbf{G} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times M}$ is the gain matrix (or leadfield) encoding the forward model, and $\mathbf{n}$ represents measurement noise.

    The inverse problem—reconstructing $\mathbf{q}$ from observed $\mathbf{y}$—is fundamentally ill-posed: infinitely many source configurations can generate the same scalp potentials. This non-uniqueness arises because the number of sensors ($N$) is typically much smaller than the number of candidate source locations ($M$), and the columns of $\mathbf{G}$ may be nearly linearly dependent. Source localization methods resolve this indeterminacy by imposing constraints that reflect biophysical or mathematical assumptions about the nature of neural sources.

    Methods for Solving the Inverse Problem¶

    Various solution strategies have been developed, each embodying different assumptions and trade-offs between spatial resolution, temporal resolution, and robustness to noise.

    Dipole scanning (or dipole fitting) assumes that scalp potentials arise from a small number (typically 1–5) of equivalent current dipoles. The method searches for dipole locations and orientations that minimize the residual variance between observed and predicted potentials. Dipole fitting is particularly useful for modeling transient discrete events such as epileptic spikes or event-related potentials, but becomes unstable when multiple distributed sources are active simultaneously.

    Beamformers (e.g., synthetic aperture magnetometry for MEG, or linear constraint minimum variance beamformers for both EEG and MEG) construct spatial filters that pass activity from a specific location while attenuating contributions from elsewhere. By scanning across a lattice of candidate source locations, beamformers produce tomographic images of neural activity. They are computationally efficient and can accommodate noise characteristics, but assume source activity is temporally uncorrelated across different locations—violation of this assumption can lead to filter interference.

    Minimum norm estimation (MNE) and its regularized variants (depth-weighted MNE, sloreta, eloreta) formulate the inverse problem as an optimization: find the source distribution $\mathbf{q}$ that minimizes the mismatch with observations, subject to a regularization term that penalizes implausible solutions. The classical MNE solution is:

    $$\hat{\mathbf{q}} = \mathbf{G}^{\mathsf{T}} (\mathbf{G} \mathbf{G}^{\mathsf{T}} + \lambda \mathbf{I})^{-1} \mathbf{y}$$

    where $\lambda$ is a regularization parameter controlling the trade-off between data fidelity and source smoothness. sLORETA and eLORETA add additional constraints to achieve unbiased localization under certain conditions, and have been widely applied in clinical and cognitive neuroscience studies.

    Low-resolution electromagnetic tomography (LORETA) constrains solutions to have non-negative source strength and employs spatial Laplacian smoothing, producing spatially blurred but temporally accurate estimates. sLORETA (standardized LORETA) achieves zero localization error under ideal conditions, while eLORETA incorporates tissue conductivity information for improved accuracy.

    Relationship to Whole-Brain Modeling¶

    In whole-brain modeling, source localization serves as a critical bridge between empirical data and computational models. Observed resting-state or task-related EEG/MEG signals are first processed to estimate source activity, which then informs the parameterization or validation of neural mass models (such as the Jansen-Rit model or Wong-Wang model) or mean-field approximations. Conversely, forward models computed from simulated source activity can be compared against empirical recordings to assess model fit.

    The combination of structural connectivity data (from DTI or tractography) with source localization enables anatomically constrained connectivity analyses, where the temporal dynamics of estimated sources are related via white-matter pathways. This approach underlies frameworks such as DCM for electrophysiological data, which estimates effective connectivity among brain regions while respecting the observed source structure.

    Challenges and Open Questions¶

    Several factors complicate source localization in practice. The accuracy of the forward model depends critically on the head model—simplified spherical models are computationally convenient but less accurate than realistic boundary element method (BEM) or finite element method (FEM) models that incorporate individual anatomical information from structural MRI. Tissue conductivity uncertainties, especially for the skull, can introduce systematic localization errors. Furthermore, the assumption of stationary noise statistics may not hold in clinical contexts (e.g., epileptic recordings with intermittent artifacts).

    Current research addresses these challenges through adaptive head modeling, Bayesian formulations that incorporate prior information from functional neuroimaging (particularly fmri), and hybrid approaches that fuse eeg and meg data to exploit their complementary sensitivities to radial and tangential sources. Open questions include how to best incorporate prior information from parcellations or whole-brain network structure, and how to achieve robust localization in the presence of aggressive noise or limited sensor coverage.

    Related Concepts¶

    Source localization is closely related to several other concepts in the wiki. It can be understood as the inverse of the forward model, and relies fundamentally on principles of volume conduction to relate scalp potentials to neural sources. The technique is directly supported by software packages such as MNE-Python and FieldTrip. When combined with brain dynamics analyses, source localization enables investigation of brain oscillations and their interactions across distributed networks. The comparison between fMRI and EEG highlights the superior temporal resolution of source-localized electrophysiology, complementing fMRI's spatial precision.
