# HNN

## Overview

The Human Neocortical Neurosolver (HNN) is an open-source neural modeling software package designed to help researchers and clinicians interpret human electroencephalography (EEG) and magnetoencephalography (MEG) data by linking these macroscopic signals to their microscopic cellular and circuit-level generators. Developed primarily at Brown University under the leadership of Professor Stephanie Jones, HNN addresses a fundamental challenge in human neuroscience: the difficulty of connecting non-invasively recorded [[brain-oscillations]] and other [[neuroimaging-eeg|EEG]]/[[neuroimaging-meg|MEG]] signals to the biophysical mechanisms that produce them. The software provides a biophysically detailed model of a neocortical column that simulates primary current dipoles—the neural sources of EEG/MEG signals—enabling users to generate and test mechanistic hypotheses about the circuit origins of their experimental data. Unlike simpler signal processing approaches that treat EEG/MEG as abstract time series, HNN grounds the analysis in the actual biology of neocortical circuitry, linking [[local-field-potentials]] and cellular activity to [[source-localization]] outputs, making it particularly valuable for researchers seeking mechanistic insight rather than merely descriptive patterns.

## Key Features

HNN's core architecture centers on a pre-constructed biophysically-principled neocortical column model that simulates the electrical activity of layer-specific pyramidal neurons and interneurons that generate the primary electrical currents underlying EEG and MEG recordings. The model contains the minimal circuit elements necessary for producing primary current dipoles and can generate predictions across multiple spatial scales: from macroscopic current dipole signals comparable to source-localized MEG/EEG data, to mesoscopic local field potentials (LFP) and current-source density (CSD) profiles, to microscopic spike rasters and compartmental voltage traces. Users can activate the neocortical model through two primary exogenous pathways: proximal drive representing lemniscal thalamic input arriving in granular layers, and distal drive representing higher-order cortical or non-lemniscal input to supragranular layers. This architecture mirrors known anatomy of thalamocortical circuitry and enables precise investigation of how different input patterns produce observed signal features.

The software supports simulation of the most commonly recorded human EEG/MEG signals, including event-related potentials (ERPs) and low-frequency brain rhythms. For ERPs, HNN provides workflows for simulating evoked responses through sequences of proximal and distal spike train drive, enabling investigation of [[excitation-inhibition-balance]] in sensory processing, allowing users to investigate how timing and strength of excitatory inputs shape the morphology of sensory-evoked potentials. For brain rhythms, HNN includes models for alpha (7–14 Hz), beta (15–29 Hz), and gamma (30–80 Hz) oscillations, with default parameter sets derived from published studies investigating these rhythms in primary somatosensory cortex. The software provides both a graphical user interface (GUI) accessible to researchers without computational modeling experience, and a programmatic API (hnn-core) for advanced users seeking custom simulation workflows.

HNN includes sophisticated parameter optimization capabilities for fitting model outputs to experimental data. Users can perform manual parameter exploration through the GUI to develop intuition about how specific biological changes—such as modifications to excitatory/inhibitory balance, between-layer communication, or input timing—affect network dynamics. For more systematic optimization, HNN supports both Bayesian optimization and COBYLA (Constrained Optimization BY Linear Approximation) algorithms to minimize the root mean square error (RMSE) between user-provided experimental dipole signals and simulated dipole outputs. This fitting capability enables data-driven refinement of mechanistic hypotheses.

## Relationship to TVB

HNN and [[the-virtual-brain]] (TVB) address complementary scales of brain modeling within the broader domain of whole-brain computational neuroscience. While TVB operates at the macroscale, simulating large-scale brain networks composed of dozens to hundreds of brain regions connected by [[structural-connectivity]] matrices derived from diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), HNN focuses on the mesoscale dynamics of a single cortical column with cell- and layer-specific resolution. However, the two platforms share philosophical commitments to biophysically grounded modeling and to connecting computational simulations to empirical neuroimaging data. Both software packages produce outputs that can be compared directly to source-localized experimental data—TVB generates region-level activity time series comparable to fMRI or EEG/MEG parcel time courses, while HNN produces current dipole signals directly comparable to sensor-level or source-localized MEG/EEG data. Researchers interested in multi-scale modeling could potentially use TVB's large-scale network predictions as input to HNN's detailed cortical column model, though such integration remains an active area of methodological development. Both HNN and TVB benefit from the neuroimaging ecosystem including [[mne]] for source localization and [[neuron]] as the underlying simulation engine.

## Key Papers

The foundational description of HNN appeared in Neymotin et al. (eLife, 2020), which introduced the software and demonstrated its application to simulating event-related potentials and rhythmic activity in primary somatosensory cortex. This paper established the biophysical basis of the template model and validated it against experimental MEG data. The more recent hnn-core implementation was described in Jas et al. (Journal of Open Source Software, 2023), which focused on the Python API and community-driven development aspects. Earlier theoretical work establishing the circuit mechanisms was published in Jones et al. (Journal of Neuroscience, 2007) and subsequent studies examining alpha, beta, and gamma rhythms (Journal of Neurophysiology, 2009; Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 2013; PNAS, 2016).

## Technical Implementation

HNN is implemented in Python and built atop the [[neuron]] simulator for the numerical solution of Compartmental models of pyramidal neuron dynamics. The model architecture follows canonical neocortical circuitry with layer-specific populations of excitatory pyramidal cells and inhibitory interneurons connected through anatomically realistic synaptic pathways. Each pyramidal neuron is represented with multiple dendritic compartments enabling simulation of active properties and synaptic integration across different dendritic domains. This level of biophysical detail distinguishes HNN from simpler [[neural-mass-models]] like the [[jansen-rit-model]] commonly used in TVB, which represent entire populations with simplified firing rate equations rather than individual neuron dynamics. The computational cost of detailed single-neuron modeling limits HNN simulations to individual cortical columns rather than whole-brain networks, though this trade-off enables investigation of mechanisms inaccessible to population-level models.
