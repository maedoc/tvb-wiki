# HNN

## Overview

HNN (Human Neocortical Neurosolver) is an open-source computational modeling software package designed to simulate and analyze [[neocortical]] microcircuits, with particular emphasis on interpreting electroencephalography ([[EEG]]) and magnetoencephalography ([[MEG]]) data. Developed primarily by [[stephanie-jones]] and collaborators at Brown University and the MGH Martinos Center for Biomedical Imaging, with Samuel Neymotin as lead developer [1], HNN provides a unified framework for building data-constrained cortical models that can be directly fitted to empirical electrophysiological recordings [1][2]. The software implements detailed biophysically-realistic neuron models based on conductance-based dynamics, allowing researchers to investigate the cellular and circuit-level mechanisms underlying macroscopic brain signals observed in non-invasive [[neuroimaging]] modalities. HNN occupies a unique position in the [[computational-neuroscience]] ecosystem, bridging the gap between detailed [[spiking-neural-networks]] simulators like [[neuron]] and [[brian2]] and the more abstract [[neural-mass-model]] approaches used in [[whole-brain-modeling]].

## Motivation and Context

The challenge of linking invasive animal studies to non-invasive human neuroimaging measurements has long limited our understanding of the cellular and circuit-level mechanisms generating [[brain-oscillations|neocortical oscillations]] and evoked responses. While [[neural-mass-model|neural mass models]] like the [[jansen-rit-model|Jansen-Rit model]] have long been used to simulate macroscopic brain activity, they often lack the biophysical detail necessary to make specific predictions about underlying cellular mechanisms [1][2][3]. HNN was developed to bridge this gap by providing a model that retains sufficient biophysical detail to make predictions about synaptic currents and cellular activity while remaining computationally tractable enough to fit directly to human [[electrophysiology]] data.

The software emerged from the recognition that [[MEG]] and [[EEG]] measurements contain rich information about the timing and amplitude of postsynaptic currents, but interpreting this information requires models that can relate macroscopic signals to their microscopic origins. By fitting HNN's detailed neocortical circuit models to empirical data, researchers can infer which cellular mechanisms are most likely generating observed brain dynamics—a capability that distinguishes HNN from simpler mean-field approaches that treat populations as abstract units without biophysical grounding [1][4].

## Technical Description

HNN implements a layered neocortical circuit model consisting of multiple neural populations that represent the major cell types found in the neocortex. The model includes excitatory [[pyramidal cell]]s organized in layers 2/3 and 5, as well as inhibitory [[interneuron]]s that mediate feedforward and feedback inhibition [1][2]. Each population is modeled using a version of the [[hodgkin-huxley-model|Hodgkin-Huxley]] formalism that captures the essential dynamics of action potential generation while remaining computationally efficient [1]. The laminar structure of the neocortex—organized into distinct layers with characteristic cell types and connectivity patterns—is explicitly represented in HNN's architecture, making it particularly suitable for studying [[cortical-columns]] and their role in generating macroscopic brain signals [2]. This represents a significant advance over earlier neural mass models like the [[jansen-rit-model|Jansen-Rit model]], which uses coupled second-order differential equations for population postsynaptic potentials—a mean-field approach that could not capture the detailed membrane dynamics underlying spike generation or the laminar-specific dynamics of real cortical circuits [3].

The model simulates the coordinated activity of thousands of neurons within each population using a mean-field approach, where the collective activity is characterized by the average membrane potential and firing rates [1][4]. Synaptic connections between populations are modeled with biologically realistic time courses for [[excitatory postsynaptic potential]]s (mediated by AMPA receptors) and [[inhibitory postsynaptic potential]]s (mediated by GABA-A receptors) [2][3]. The model also incorporates [[local-field-potentials|LFP]] contributions from both synaptic currents and membrane fluctuations, allowing direct comparison with electrophysiological measurements [1][2].

Key equations governing the model dynamics include the membrane potential dynamics for pyramidal cells, which can be approximated by a two-variable system capturing spike generation and adaptation. The synaptic input to each population is computed as the weighted sum of firing rates from presynaptic populations convolved with synaptic kernel functions. The model's output includes both the simulated [[LFP]] (which approximates what would be measured by EEG or [[MEG]] source sensors) and the firing rates of individual populations [1][4]. Critically, HNN captures the [[excitation-inhibition-balance]] that is fundamental to cortical dynamics—through proper tuning of AMPA-mediated excitatory and GABA-A-mediated inhibitory synaptic conductances, the model can reproduce the full spectrum of neocortical oscillations from slow delta rhythms to fast gamma oscillations [3][4][5].

## Key Features

One of HNN's primary features is its ability to perform parameter optimization to fit model outputs to empirical data. The software includes optimization routines—implemented using the [[parameter-estimation]] framework—that adjust synaptic connectivity strengths, intrinsic neuronal properties, and external drive parameters to minimize the difference between simulated and observed brain signals [1][4]. This fitting process allows researchers to infer which circuit mechanisms are most consistent with their experimental observations—a capability that has proven valuable for testing hypotheses about the cellular basis of neurological and psychiatric disorders, including work connecting HNN to research on conditions such as [[epilepsy-modeling]] and autism spectrum disorders [5][6].

HNN provides tools for both single-cell and population-level analyses, enabling researchers to examine how changes in specific cellular parameters or synaptic connections affect emergent network dynamics [1]. The software includes visualization utilities for comparing model outputs with empirical data, as well as routines for performing spectral analysis and time-frequency decompositions. These tools allow detailed investigation of how specific biophysical parameters give rise to observed oscillation patterns in different frequency bands [4][5].

The package supports simulation of both spontaneous activity and evoked responses, making it suitable for studying both [[resting-state]] networks and task-related brain dynamics [1][2]. Researchers have used HNN to investigate the neural basis of [[brain-oscillations]] including alpha rhythms, beta oscillations, and gamma oscillations, as well as the mechanisms underlying auditory and somatosensory evoked responses [2][3][4][5]. The ability to model both spontaneous and evoked activity makes HNN particularly valuable for clinical research applications where researchers seek to understand how circuit alterations manifest in both baseline brain dynamics and stimulus-evoked responses.

## Relationship to TVB

While [[the-virtual-brain]] (TVB) focuses on [[whole-brain-modeling]] at the network level, simulating large-scale brain regions connected through [[structural connectivity]] derived from diffusion tensor imaging, HNN operates at a finer spatial scale, concentrating on [[cortical-columns]] or microcircuit dynamics [3]. TVB typically employs reduced [[neural-mass-model|neural mass models]] that capture population-level activity without detailed single-neuron biophysics, whereas HNN prioritizes mechanistic realism at the cellular level using [[hodgkin-huxley-model|Hodgkin-Huxley]]-type neuron models [3][4]. This complementary relationship has led researchers to propose hierarchical modeling approaches where HNN-derived local circuit parameters inform TVB's mesoscopic population models—effectively using HNN to provide mechanistic insight into the node dynamics that TVB treats abstractly [3]. Additionally, while TVB can incorporate [[connectome]] data from databases like [[human-connectome-project|HCP]], HNN typically focuses on parameterizing models based on empirical electrophysiological measurements from [[EEG]] and [[MEG]] rather than anatomical connectivity [1][2].

## Related Software

HNN occupies a unique niche in the computational neuroscience software landscape, complementing both general-purpose neural simulators and other neural mass implementations. Unlike simulators such as [[neuron]], [[brian2]], or [[nest]] which model individual spiking neurons in detail, HNN operates at the neural mass level, making it more efficient for fitting to macroscopic electrophysiological data [1]. Compared to the [[jansen-rit-model|Jansen-Rit model]] and similar neural mass implementations in packages like [[spm]] or [[fieldtrip|FieldTrip's]] DCM, HNN provides more biophysically detailed circuit architecture and sophisticated parameter optimization capabilities [3][4]. The level of detail in HNN also distinguishes it from [[whole-brain-simulators]] like TVB that focus on region-to-region connectivity patterns rather than intraregional microcircuit dynamics [3][4].

Related tools in the ecosystem include [[brain-dynamics-toolbox|Brain Dynamics Toolbox]], [[mne-python|MNE-Python]], [[eeglab|EEGLAB]], and [[fieldtrip|FieldTrip]] for EEG/MEG data analysis. The HNN approach shares conceptual foundations with [[dynamic-causal-modeling|Dynamic Causal Modeling]] in that both aim to infer underlying neural mechanisms from observed electrophysiological data, though they differ in their degree of biophysical detail and the specific inversion procedures they employ [1][3]. Researchers using HNN also frequently employ [[bctpy|Brain Connectivity Toolbox]] for analyzing [[functional-connectivity]] patterns in their data, and may combine HNN simulations with visualization tools like [[brainnet-viewer]] for displaying model results on cortical surfaces [1][4].

## Key Papers

The foundational paper describing HNN is "Human Neocortical Neurosolver (HNN): A software platform for interpreting MEG/EEG data" by Neymotin et al. (2020), published in eLife, which established the software's architecture and demonstrated its utility for analyzing sensory-evoked responses [1]. Earlier work by Jones et al. (2007) established the theoretical foundations for the underlying neocortical model, demonstrating how conductance-based modeling could reproduce human MEG data during tactile detection tasks [2]. Subsequent work established the mechanistic basis of alpha and beta rhythms (Jones et al., 2009; Sherman et al., 2016), showing how specific combinations of synaptic and intrinsic properties give rise to these neocortical oscillations [3][4]. Research on gamma frequency oscillations (Lee & Jones, 2013) demonstrated the model's capability to capture fast rhythmic activity in human cortical circuits [5]. These publications collectively demonstrate HNN's validity as a tool for relating cellular-level mechanisms to human electrophysiological measurements, with applications spanning basic neuroscience and clinical research on conditions including autism and epilepsy [5][6].
