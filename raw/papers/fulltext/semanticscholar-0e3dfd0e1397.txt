Abstract

Since its founding in 1971, the United Arab Emirates (UAE) has experienced rapid population

growth, urbanization, and increased life expectancy, leading to a rise in health issues common in

developed countries, such as diabetes and cardiovascular disease. These conditions significantly

impact brain health, often contributing to perceptual and cognitive deficits. However, the UAE

and the broader Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region lack large, controlled normative

neuroimaging data to assess these effects on the brain. To address this gap, the ASPIRE Research

Background & Summary

       Since its foundation in 1971, the UAE has experienced rapid advancements in

infrastructure, education, standard of living, and life expectancy 1. The country’s evolving

demographic landscape has led to health challenges common to the developed world, such as high

prevalence rates of diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and obesity. However, the genetic makeup of

the local population differs markedly from those in other developed nations. Consanguinity rates

are an order of magnitude higher in Middle Eastern populations than in Western countries due to

intermarriage and higher levels of relatedness 2, resulting in a substantial burden of regions of




                                                                 S
homozygosity. At the same time, the UAE represents a unique genetic diversity, as over 85% of




                                                          ES
the population are expatriates and no single ethnicity constitutes a majority. The UAE also has a
                                                    PR
unique set of environmental factors. For example, perhaps owing to its climate, over 90% of the
                                            IN

population is vitamin D deficient 3–5, which may contribute to the high prevalence of multiple
                                    LE




sclerosis in the UAE 6. Taken together, these factors create an urgent need to collect and curate
                                 C




data that can tailor our understanding of health outcomes to the local and regional context.
                            TI




       This paper describes the ASPIRE Precision Medicine Research Institute Imaging
                        R
                     A




normative reference dataset designed to investigate the relationship between brain-based

biomarkers and major health challenges in the UAE, including diabetes, cardiovascular disease,

and obesity. This dataset integrates neuroimaging and behavioral data to identify early markers

and risk factors unique to the UAE population.

       Researchers are increasingly recognizing the value of establishing disease phenotypes

through large-scale population-based studies. This approach is instrumental in identifying imaging

targets for disease diagnosis and prevention 7,8. Modern medical imaging technologies, particularly

magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), have profoundly advanced our understanding of human brain
                                     ARTICLE IN PRESS

structure and function in health and disease. Indeed, MRI is now recognized as a crucial tool for

early detection of pathological changes, long before clinical symptoms arise 9–12. This early

detection capability is vital for identifying at-risk individuals and tailoring interventions to prevent

or delay disease onset. Medical imaging also plays an essential role in population health studies,

offering a way to monitor the impact of lifestyle and environmental factors on brain health.

        Large-scale population studies and initiatives, such as the Human Connectome Project

(HCP) 7, the UK Biobank 8, the Pediatric Imaging Neurocognition and Genetics (PING) study 13,

the Australian Imaging, Biomarkers and Lifestyle (AIBL) study 14, and the Alzheimer’s Disease




                                                                    S
Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) 15 have yielded valuable insights into health and disease-related




                                                             ES
imaging phenotypes, genetic markers, and behavioral measures within Western populations.
                                                      PR
However, the applicability of these findings to other populations is limited due to genetic diversity,
                                              IN

environmental exposure, and disease prevalence; human brains exhibit significant variability

among individuals within populations and across different phenotypic categories (e.g., age,
                                      LE




ethnicity) 16–18.

                      A

to enhance brain health and promote healthier aging in diverse populations, with a specific focus

on the MENA region.

Study Overview

       Figure 1 provides an overview of the study protocol. Once participants are confirmed to

meet the eligibility criteria, they proceed to an MRI session, followed by a behavioral assessment.

Following data acquisition, MRI scans are securely transferred and archived on an XNAT

repository (https://www.xnat.org/). The data undergo standardized preprocessing executed via

XNAT’s integration with our high-performance computing infrastructure. From the preprocessed

outputs, we extract image-derived phenotypes (IDPs) across multiple imaging modalities. These

IDPs are then used in association analyses to investigate potential relationships with behavioral




                                                                  S
measures. Variables such as age, sex, and other factors are available to ensure robust and




                                                          ES
interpretable results. The database is formatted according to the Brain Imaging Data Structure
                                                    PR
(BIDS) standard convention 19, containing structural, diffusion, perfusion, and resting state MRI
                                            IN

scans, with demographic and behavioral data in separated files.

Participants

       The study is designed to be inclusive of all nationalities and ethnicities who reside in the

UAE. Participants are between 18-60 years old. This study is conducted in compliance with the
                                   ARTICLE IN PRESS

Helsinki Declaration, and has been approved by the local ethics committee, Human Research

Ethics Committee at New York University Abu Dhabi (NYUAD).

       We intentionally adopted a balanced recruitment strategy comprising 50% Emirati

nationals and 50% expatriate residents. While this does not mirror the national demographic

distribution—where expatriates constitute approximately 85% of the population—this approach

was designed to enhance the scientific utility of the dataset. Emirati nationals possess a unique

genetic and cultural profile that is severely underrepresented in global brain research. Over-

representing this group allows us to capture region-specific neurobiological variation and generate




                                                                 S
normative benchmarks that are directly relevant to the local population. In contrast, the expatriate




                                                            ES
population in the UAE includes individuals from ethnic backgrounds (e.g., South Asian, East
                                                     PR
Asian, and Caucasian) that are already well represented in international datasets. Including both
                                            IN

groups enables meaningful cross-cohort comparisons while ensuring that our normative data

reflect the diversity and specificity of the UAE context.

                                          LE

                                      C

Testing occurs at 4 meters, with a closer 2-meter test for those with visual difficulty, and results

are expressed in the Logarithm of the Minimum Angle of Resolution (LogMAR) values, where

positive numbers signify vision loss and negative numbers indicate normal or superior acuity.

            Sighting eye dominance is measured using a modified Miles Test 27. Participants stand

three meters from an eye-level object, form a triangle aperture with their hands, and alternately

open each eye to identify which eye provides a clearer view of the object through the aperture.

            Interpupillary distance (IPD) is measured using an Essilor Digital Corneal Reflection

Pupillometer (Essilor Instruments, Chicago, IL, USA). Participants are instructed to look at a

corneal-reflected target light through the pupillometer, allowing for the alignment of corneal

millimeters.

        Contrast sensitivity, which evaluates the ability to discern between varying levels of

contrast and predicts real-world visual performance, is measured using the Pelli-Robson Contrast

Sensitivity test (Precision Vision, Woodstock, IL, USA). This test, conducted at 1 meter with best-

corrected vision and uniform chart illumination, assesses both monocular and binocular contrast

sensitivity by determining the minimum contrast needed to identify letters of a uniform size. It




                                                                    S
features rows of letters with decreasing contrast, and participants' scores are based on the lowest




                                                             ES

                                                      PR

Stereo Test (Stereo Optical, Chicago, IL, USA), which assesses the ability to discern slight
                                      LE




differences in position between images seen by each eye. This test, crucial for identifying depth
                                  C
                             TI




perception impairments due to conditions like strabismus and amblyopia, comprises ten panels,
                         R

                      A

MRI Data Acquisition

       MRI supports acquisition of several imaging modalities offering complementary

information. We chose specific modalities based on generalizability, automated analysis

feasibility, robust acquisition methods, and time constraints aiming to limit the total scanning time

to one hour. Data is collected using a Siemens Prisma 3T full-body MRI scanner, equipped with a

64-channel head coil. Eye-tracking data during four resting state functional MRI (rsfMRI) scans




                                                                   S
is collected using the Eyelink 1000 Plus to ensure participants are awake during resting state. The




                                                            ES

                                                      PR
from 1-to-3 based on data quality, with 1 being low quality and 3 being high quality.

MRI Data Quality Control

        All data undergoes conversion from DICOM to NIFTI format in the Brain Imaging Data

Structure       (BIDS)        standard,       facilitated      by        the      dcm2bids         tool

(https://unfmontreal.github.io/Dcm2Bids/3.1.1/), to arrange the imaging data into standard and

modality-specific directories 19.
                                    ARTICLE IN PRESS

       Subsequently, a comprehensive data quality assessment is conducted utilizing MRIQC 30.

We generate individual and project-level reports for the entire dataset and provide no-reference

image quality metrics (e.g. contrast-to-noise ratio, signal-to-noise ratio, intensity non-uniformity,

head motion, blurred data, etc.). MRIQC generates these reports for different MRI data including

structural (T1w and T2w), diffusion weighted MRI, and functional MRI data. Moreover, our team

of trained researchers conducts a visual inspection of the data to ensure its quality prior to

commencing the preprocessing stage. This meticulous approach guarantees that our research is

built on a solid foundation of high-quality data.

       Structural data preprocessing: T1 images are processed using fMRIPrep’s 31 anatomical
                                 C
                            TI




preprocessing pipeline, including correction for intensity non-uniformity 32, segmentation of tissue
                         R




classes including cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), white matter (WM), and gray matter (GM) using FSL-
                     A




FAST 33. The T1 and FLAIR images undergo cortical reconstruction using FreeSurfer recon-all 34.

The output from FreeSurfer is visually inspected to check for segmentation quality, and manually

edited to correct for errors in segmentation. Once corrected, brain surface maps, and cortical and

subcortical segments are generated using a hybrid segmentation algorithm by Mindboggle 35 (Fig.

2). After skull-stripping, the T1-weighted image is normalized to the MNI152 template space. To

align other imaging modalities—such as functional, diffusion, and perfusion MRI scans—these
                                  ARTICLE IN PRESS

images are first co-registered to the T1 scan. The warp fields generated during the T1-to-MNI

normalization are then applied to transform the co-registered scans into MNI space.

       fMRI data preprocessing: fMRIPrep (https://fmriprep.org/en/stable/) is used for

preprocessing of fMRI data 31. fMRI data is slice-time corrected, corrected for B0 susceptibility

distortion using FSL-topup 36, motion corrected, skull-stripped, and resampled to an isotropic

resolution of 2 mm3. rsfMRI data is co-registered with the T1-weighted images using bbregister,

which can then be used to generate an individual-based cortical and surface fMRI signal. Finally,

rsfMRI data are normalized to the standard MNI152 space by applying the registration metrics and

deformation (warp) fields obtained during the T1-weighted image normalization process. The

rsfMRI data for each subject is further cleaned through the use of independent component analysis

(ICA) algorithm for automatically identifying and removing structured artifacts, using ICA-

AROMA 37. Finally, the fMRI data is temporally filtered with band-pass (f = 0.007 – 0.1 Hz) and

smoothed using a 6-mm Gaussian kernel of full width at half maximum.

       Diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) Preprocessing: DWI preprocessing is performed

using Tractoflow 38 (https://github.com/scilus/tractoflow), as it generates both diffusion measures

and tractography of WM (Fig 3). Briefly, DWI data preprocessing involves denoising, Gibbs

ringing correction, geometric distortion correction, motion and Eddy-current correction, skull




                                                                  S
stripping and field inhomogeneity correction. Once DWI data are preprocessed, the fiber




                                                           ES
orientation distribution function (fODF) is generated using constrained spherical deconvolution 39.
                                                     PR
Four Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI) measures including fractional anisotropy (FA), mean
                                             IN

diffusivity (MD), radial diffusivity (RD) and axial diffusivity (AD) are computed and HARDI-

derived measures including total apparent fiber density (AFD total) and number of fiber
                                     LE




orientations (NuFO), is extracted from the fODF. The whole-brain ensemble tractogram is
                                 C
                            TI




generated from a fODF map and tracking masks using both the anatomically constrained particle
                         R




filter tracking algorithm 40, and local tracking with 5 and 2 seeds per voxel respectively. We use
                     A




the default Tractoflow settings (for complete pipeline description, see 38).

       The structural connectome is estimated using the tck2connectome command in MRtrix3,

which computes the weighted number of streamlines connecting each pair of parcels defined in an

atlas 40. To enhance biological interpretability and reduce methodological biases, several weighting

strategies are applied. First, individual streamline weights are incorporated using the -

tck_weights_in option. Second, the connectome matrix is scaled by streamline length (-

scale_length) to mitigate the inherent bias of probabilistic tractography toward shorter
                                      ARTICLE IN PRESS

connections. Third, the matrix is further normalized by the inverse of the product of the volumes

of the connected parcels (-scale_invnodevol), correcting for the disproportionate connectivity that

can arise from larger parcel sizes.

        Perfusion data preprocessing: ASL data is preprocessed using BASIL 41. To summarize,

ASL data is slice timing corrected, motion corrected with the reference volume set at the median

volume, skull stripped, susceptibility distortion corrected using B0 fieldmap images, and co-

partial volume correction applied. The final maps are normalized to MNI space to address

anatomical variability among subjects and facilitate group-level analyses.

Data organization

The dataset contains regular BIDS files across 41 participants’ data folders, as well as

a demographics folder.    The    stand-alone    files   offer   an   overview   of   the   dataset:

i) dataset_description.json is a JSON file describing study name, dataset type and authors;

ii) participants.tsv contains participants’ information, such as age, sex, and handedness;
                                    ARTICLE IN PRESS

iii) participants.json describes the column attributes in participants.tsv; iv) README.md contains

a detailed introduction of the dataset.

structural MRI scans, including FLAIR and T1-weighted (T1w) images in NIfTI format,

acquisition parameters such as TR, TE, FOV, and spatial resolution. The dwi folder included the

raw diffusion-weighted imaging data in NIfTI format, a .json file describing the sequence

parameters, a .bval file specifying the b-values for each volume, and a .bvec file indicating the

                                                       PR

Proposed Improvements of the current study protocol

       At the completion of data acquisition for 100 participants, we aim to assess our data

collection methods and refine our practices. We have initiated discussions on potential

enhancements to our investigation, focusing on gathering additional information to deepen our

understanding of the population under study. Proposed improvements include implementing new

imaging protocols, and enhancing data collection procedures. These adjustments aim to optimize

the study protocol and enrich the dataset for future stages of data collection. In the following

subsections, we discuss potential improvements that we aim to implement.




                                                                  S
Changes to the imaging protocol




                                                           ES
       Firstly, we will consider an MP2RAGE sequence instead of the current MPRAGE
                                                     PR
sequence to enhance our capability to estimate the quantitative T1 values 44. The MP2RAGE

sequence consists of an adiabatic inversion pulse, followed by two gradient-echo readout modules
                                             IN


that sample the longitudinal magnetization, to generate two images at two different inversion time
                                     LE




(TI) points (TI1 and TI2) of the T1 recovery curve 45. In addition, MP2RAGE is a bias free
                                 C
                            TI




sequence as compared to MPRAGE, with better contrast to noise ratio 46, which improves
                         R




structural data analysis and segmentation.
                     A




       Moreover, we will consider dividing the rsfMRI data acquisition into a pair of forward and

reverse phase scans to be acquired at the start of the MRI session, immediately following the

acquisition of the T1 scan, while the second pair will be collected at the end of the session. This

adjustment may be made in response to the feedback received from participants who expressed

difficulty in remaining awake and fixation during the rsfMRI data collection for a full 20 minutes.

Inclusion of physiological signal collection for improved resting state fMRI processing.
       When conducting fMRI data analysis, challenges related to head motion and high-

frequency cardiac and respiratory artifacts present significant obstacles, impacting data quality and
                                    ARTICLE IN PRESS

impeding reliable observations of functional connectivity and activity 47. To address these

challenges, we plan to incorporate the collection of physiological signals, including cardiac and

respiratory signals, during rsfMRI scans. Previous research has demonstrated that regressing out

heart-rate data can improve the temporal signal-to-noise ratio during resting-state scans 48. In cases

where peripheral recordings of physiological signals are unavailable, data-driven approaches like

individual independent component analysis (ICA) and regressing out the noise signal from WM

and CSF regions can serve as effective alternatives for fMRI data denoising 49,50. These

comprehensive strategies aim to optimize data quality and facilitate more accurate interpretations




                                                                  S
of functional connectivity in our study.

Relationship to other initiatives nationally and internationally
                                                    PR
       Our current study aims to improve understanding of neuroimaging within the Middle
                                            IN

Eastern population, providing distinct advantages over existing initiatives. We implement a
                                    LE



comprehensive imaging approach that covers multiple organ systems, employs a highly multi-
                                 C




modal brain protocol, and integrates rich existing phenotypic data. All data is collected using the
                            TI




same MRI scanner, which facilitates data harmonization and reduces potential confounding effects
                        R




associated with data collected from diverse sites. While the statistical advantages of large sample
                     A




sizes are evident, our primary goal in enrolling 2000 subjects is to generate prospective imaging

data conducive to identifying early markers and risk factors across a wide spectrum of diseases.

        Over the past two decades, numerous large-scale neuroimaging cohort studies have been

conducted, involving thousands of participants. These include the HCP (n=1200) 7,43, PING study

(n=1000) 13, AIBL Study (n=1100) 14, Maastricht Study (n=10,000) 51, the German National

Cohort (n=30,000) 52, and UK Biobank imaging cohort (n=100,000) 8. These initiatives leverage
                                    ARTICLE IN PRESS

rich phenotyping to comprehensively investigate brain function and structure with robust statistical

power. However, they predominantly include participants of European descent.

       In contrast, middle eastern families are typically characterized by larger sizes, higher

maternal and paternal ages at conception, and significant levels of endogamy, with consanguinity

rates ranging from 25% to 60%. These rates are typically an order of magnitude higher than the

less than 0.2% consanguinity rate observed in Western countries 2, resulting in a substantial burden

from the higher levels of homozygosity. Therefore, the establishment of the current study is

essential to understand the impact of these factors on brain health in relation to genetic and




                                                                 S
behavioral changes.
