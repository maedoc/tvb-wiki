                  1. Background
                  The human brain is one of the most complex systems in the world, consisting of approxi-
                  mately 86 billion neurons. These neurons are interconnected by around 150 trillion
                  synapses and communicate through the transmission of signals, including both electri-
                  cal and chemical signals, in order to generate human behaviours (Azevedo et al. 2009;
                  Pakkenberg 2003). Understanding the underlying mechanism of human cognition
                  and behaviour under various conditions, including normal and abnormal states, is an
                  extremely important goal of neuroscience. Recently, numerous studies have considered
                  modeling and analysing the brain from the perspective of connectivity patterns. This
                  approach provides comprehensive information behind human cognition, behavior, and
                  perception (Park and Friston 2013; Reijneveld et al. 2007; Bullmore and Sporns 2009).
                  Brain connectivity patterns can be divided into the structural and functional connectiv-
                  ity. However, the majority of studies have been devoted to functional connectivity (Bull-
                  more and Sporns 2009).
                     There are many tools for analyzing connectivity patterns; however, due to the com-
                  plexity of brain connectivity patterns, as well as its network nature, graph theory is more
                  commonly used. This theory has become the most popular tool for analyzing brain con-
                  nectivity patterns (Sporns et al. 2004, 2005). Graph theory provides a robust mathemati-
                  cal framework to model and investigate brain connectivity from the network point of
                  view. The graph-based network analysis has the potential to reveal meaningful informa-
                  tion about the topological architectures of the human brain and their changes under
                  various conditions, including normal and abnormal states (Bullmore and Bassett 2011;
                  Stam 2014). These changes can provide insight into the underlying mechanism of human
                  cognition, and can be used to guide diagnosis and treatments.
                     Thanks to advancements of neuron imaging, nowadays, neuronal imaging modalities
                  such as diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI),
                  Fluorodeoxyglucose Positron Emission Tomography (FDG-PET) can be used to model
                  brain networks and study different disease pathways, contributing significantly to our
                  knowledge about the brain and brain disorders. Among those imaging modalities, fMRI
                  is a most common one and there have been numerous studies using fMRI to model brain
                  networks (Wang 2010; Farahani et al. 2019), just to name a few. Compared to fMRI and
                  other neuroimaging modalities, FDG-PET images provide unique information which
                  cannot be achieved by the others. It provides information about glucose consumption
                  in the brain, which is directly correlated with brain synaptic activity (Attwell and Iade-
                  cola 2002). Moreover, metabolic brain networks highlighted by FDG-PET images are not
                  completely explained by functional or structural brain networks (Thielcke et al. 2017; Li
                  et al. 2023), even highly correlated. This suggests that metabolic connectivity networks
                  might exhibit distinct properties from functional connectivity, highlighting the impor-
                  tance of brain metabolic connectivity analysis using FDG-PET images. However, the
                  number of studies on metabolic networks and graph theory is still limited compared to
                  fMRI imaging. Moreover, there is a lack of survey articles discussing FDG-PET-based
                  brain connectivity networks and graph theory as tools for analyzing metabolic connec-
                  tivity patterns.
                     In this study, we conduct a systematic search to review recent studies using graph
                  theory for studying FDG-PET based connectivity patterns. Our work has two primary
Tuan et al. EJNMMI Reports    (2025) 9:4                                                                         Page 3 of 28




                  objectives: first, to determine where FDG-PET based connectivity and graph the-
                  ory can enhance our understanding of underlying mechanisms across various con-
                  ditions; and second, to provide a brief overview of the most common techniques for
                  constructing and analyzing metabolic networks. The following sections details our
                  methodology, an introduction to graph theory applications in metabolic networks,
                  findings from our literature review, and a discussion on the critical roles, challenges,
                  as well as potential future developments in this rapidly advancing field.

                  Methodology
                  Research Questions (RQs): Based on the objectives of this study, the following research
                  questions were derived and form as the basis for this study:

                    • RQ1: How have methods for modeling brain metabolic patterns with FDG-PET

                    • RQ2: How can we categorize research on mapping the human metabolic connec-

                    • RQ3: How has the application of graph theory concepts evolved in understanding

                    • RQ4: What are the latest advancements in utilizing graph-based approaches to
                      explore metabolic networks using FDG-PET images? (Applications)
                    • RQ5: What challenges and promising future directions emerge from the current
                      state of graph-based research on metabolic networks with FDG-PET? (Challenges

                     For those RQs, while RQ1, 2 and 3 provide the answers for the important graph the-

                  and 5 give a more detailed picture about related research studies, as well as future

                     Search Strategy and Eligibility Criteria: The search strategy involves several steps,
                  including determining the search space and exploring relevant materials. In this study,
                  we focus on the studies regarding graph-based methods for analyzing FDG-PET-based
                  metabolic network, including peer-reviewed journal articles, proceedings, conferences
                  articles. The research process is performed on Web of Science with two data collections,
                  including Web of Science Core Collection and MEDLINE. Since initial work with PET

                  ing criteria: (a) written in English; (b) peer reviewed; (c) identify and describe methods
                  to build, quantify or compare metabolic connectivity patterns in the human brain net-
                  work; (d) applied to FDG-PET data. Based on searching articles, we perform analysis
                  and summarize results in order to find out the answers for research questions.

                  A brief introduction to graph
                  Graph Theory: The roots of graph theory and network analysis can be traced back to
                  eighteenth century when Leonhard Euler solved the Konigsberg Bridge Problem. In
                  Euler’s work, he described graph as a finite set of nodes that are connected together
                  by edges. Since then, graph theory has been used and applied in a variety of fields,
                  including physics, biology, chemistry, information systems, just to name a few. How-
                  ever, the application of graph theory for brain networks, only became widespread
                  after 2005, following the introduction of the "Human Connectome" by Olaf Sporns
                  and his colleagues (Sporns et al. 2004) (Sporns et al. 2005). From this time point, the
                  term "Brain Connectome" or "Brain Connectomics" became more prevalent.
                    When working with brain networks, two fundamental questions need to be
                  addressed: (i) the methodology for graph construction and (ii) the techniques for graph
                  analysis (Fornito et al. 2016; Bassett and Sporns 2017; Sporns 2018). Specifically, dur-
                  ing the construction phase, the essential components of any brain network are its
                  nodes and edges (Rubinov and Sporns 2010). Therefore, two key considerations must
                  be taken into account: (i.1) how to define the nodes, and (i.2) how to determine the
                  connections between them (Qi et al. 2015; Sporns 2013). Meanwhile, when analyzing
                  a graph, various metrics can be computed to reveal its topologies and characteristics
                  (Rubinov and Sporns 2010; He and Evans 2010). Studying the changes in graph topol-
                  ogies is expected to provide insights into brain structure and the underlying mecha-
                  nisms of behavior (Medaglia et al. 2015; Bullmore and Bassett 2011). In the following
                  paragraphs, we will briefly describe several important properties of the brain from a
                  graph theory perspective.
                    To survive in a changing world, our brains have to process a lot of information and
                  convert it into clear thoughts to help us make smart decisions. This process is related
                  to two important properties of brain networks: integration and segregation (Sporns
                  2013). Integration refers to the ability of different brain regions to work together,
                  allowing efficient exchange of information and the coordination of various cognitive
                  functions. Segregation, on the other hand, is the capacity of the brain to separate and
                  process information within specialized, local networks or modules. Maintaining a
                  balance between integration and segregation is crucial for the optimal functioning of
                  the brain (Sporns 2013; Lord et al. 2017).
                    Another important property of brain networks is the "small-world" property. This con-
                  cept originated from the work of Watts and Strogatz in 1998 (Watts and Strogatz 1998),
                  which revealed that various networks in social and biological domains exhibit a unique
                  organization. Small-world networks display characteristics of both regular and random
                  networks, featuring a short average path length between nodes and a high clustering
                  coefficient. This architecture efficiently balances the minimization of resource costs and
                  the maximization of information flow within the network components. The anticipated
                  small-world architecture of the human brain network is attributed to lower metabolic
                  and wiring costs in connections among nearby brain areas compared to distant regions.
                  Both theoretical and empirical studies support the notion that brain regions tend to
                  interact more with their neighbors to reduce overall metabolic costs, while maintaining
                  a small number of long-distance connections to enhance the efficiency of data transmis-
                  sion Bassett and Bullmore (2006; Meunier et al. 2010; Bullmore and Sporns 2012).

                    In addition to integration, segregation, and small-world properties, there are other
                  graph metrics employed to assess the topological properties of networks, such as short-
                  est path length, global coefficient, hub, and so on (Fornito et al. 2016; Rubinov and
                  Sporns 2010). They all play a crucial role in network-based studies. Each of these metrics
                  has its own advantages and disadvantages. While it’s challenging to determine the most
                  suitable measures for studying the complex structure of the human brain, those capable
                  of representing small-world properties and hub topologies are deemed significant. In the
                  following subsections, we will briefly describe the process of building a brain connectiv-
                  ity network using FDG-PET data, as well as the most common graph measures found in
                  the literature.
                    Construction of Metabolic Connectivity network using FDG-PET: The most common
                  procedure for building a graph involves several steps, including the definition of the net-
                  work’s nodes and edges (Qi et al. 2015; Sporns 2013). For node definition, it is essential
                  to determine a suitable parcellation scheme, such as brain parcellations, random tem-
                  plates, or predefined anatomical templates (Qi et al. 2015), to divide the entire brain into
                  smaller parts known as regions of interest (ROIs). While various parcellation schemes
                  exist, the Automated anatomical labelling (AAL) atlas (Tzourio-Mazoyer et al. 2002)
                  can be considered as the most common one (Qi et al. 2015). The ROIs can then be con-
                  sidered as the nodes of the graph. Subsequently, the edges or links between nodes can
                  be computed using correlation metrics such as covariance estimation (Liu et al. 2022),
                  Pearson correlation coefficients (Yao and Bin 2016; Huang et al. 2018), probability diver-
                  gence measures (Li et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2020) and so on. After these steps, we obtain
                  a correlation matrix which illustrates relationships between vertices in a network. Brain
                  graphs can be either directed or undirected, depending on the presence of directional
                  information in the links. Furthermore, brain graphs can be weighted or binary, with
                  weighted networks incorporating diverse information, such as fiber number and length,
                  obtained from techniques like diffusion MRI. In order to facilitate further analysis, pre-
                  vious studies (Li et al. 2023; Huang et al. 2020; Zhijun Yao et al. 2018) typically convert
                  the correlation matrix into a binary connectivity matrix by applying a specific threshold
                  value, which is often set to retain the top 5% to 50% of the strongest connections. Finally,
                  several key topological metrics of graph can be computed and used to compare graphs
                  together.
                    Computing Graph Measures: In graph theory, an adjacency matrix illustrates rela-
                  tionships between vertices in a network. Extracting metrics from this matrix enables
                  the topological analysis of graphs, such as the human brain network. Due to technical
                  constraints, most human brain investigations have focused on undirected networks
                  (Rubinov and Sporns 2010). Graph metrics can be divided into two groups: global and
                  local metrics (Bassett and Sporns 2017). The differences between the two groups are:
                  while global metrics reveal global characteristics of the network such as segregation,
                  integration, smallworldness, network resilience; local metrics give information about
                  most significant nodes which have a lot of contribution to network topology (Rubinov
                  and Sporns 2010).

                    a) Global metrics: Global measures in brain network analysis aim to uncover several
                       key aspects: (a) functional segregation, which assesses how network elements form
Tuan et al. EJNMMI Reports   (2025) 9:4                                                                           Page 6 of 28




                       specialized communities; (b) functional integration, providing insights into the effi-
                       ciency of global information communication; (c) small-worldness, indicating an opti-
                       mal balance between network segregation and integration; and (d) network resilience
                       against failure (Fornito et al. 2016; Rubinov and Sporns 2010). Segregation is meas-
                       ured by clustering coefficients and modularity, which quantify topological proper-
                       ties in brain networks, considering anatomically adjacent or functionally connected
                       areas as modules (Fornito et al. 2016). Functional integration is typically assessed by
                       the characteristic path length, representing the ability for global information inte-
                       gration (Fornito et al. 2016). Various studies show that networks based on modu-
                       lar structures often reflect small-world properties Bassett and Bullmore (2006). The
                       smallworld property ensures an optimal balance between segregation and integra-
                       tion, facilitating efficient communication across the network (Sporns et al. 2004;
                       Rubinov and Sporns 2010). Additionally, assortativity measures the network’s resil-
                       ience against random or deliberate damages in its main components, addressing a
                       significant concern in network science.
                    b) Local metrics: In network science, hubs are nodes with high nodal centrality, signifi-
                       cantly influencing the network topology (Fornito et al. 2016). There are two types of
                       hub nodes: connector hubs, which interconnect nodes between different modules,
                       and provincial hubs, responsible for linking nodes within the same module. Detect-
                       ing hubs in a network is commonly done by calculating nodal degree, i.e., counting
                       the edges connected to each node (Fornito et al. 2016; Rubinov and Sporns 2010).
                       The degree distribution of a network, represented by P(k), provides insights into
                       the presence of hubs; for example, in scale-free networks, the existence of several
                       high-degree nodes is associated with a power-law distribution. Other frequently used
                       measures for nodal centrality include betweenness, closeness, eigenvector, participa-
                       tion coefficient, and PageRank (Fornito et al. 2016; Rubinov and Sporns 2010). These
                       measures help assess the importance and influence of nodes within a network.

                    Most common graph metrics: In this section, we provide a brief introduction to the
                  most popular graph metrics appearing in our review; more details about these metrics
                  can be found in Fornito et al. (2016); Rubinov and Sporns 2010). Formally, a graph can
                  be represented by G = (V,E) with n nodes, V = {vi|i ∈ 1,2,…,n} is the set of all nodes (or
                  vertexes) in the network, |E| is the set of links (or connections, edges) between two
                  nodes E = {(vi,vj)|i,j ∈ 1,2,…,n}, ai,j is the connection status between node vi and node vj.
                  |V |, |E| denotes the number of nodes and the number edges, respectively. Graph can be
                  classified as directed or undirected based on whether the edges have sense of direction
                  information. Graph can also be divided into unweighted (binary) or weighted graph. If
                  every edges in the graph has an equal value 1, it can be considered as binary graph, while
                  edges are signed with different values, we call it as weighted graph. Since weighted and
                  directed graph can be very complex for visualizing and analysis, most of studies focus
                  on undirected and unweighted graph. In this section we will mainly focus on graph met-
                  rics for undirected and unweighted graph. However, Graph metrics for directed and
                  weighted graph can be found in Rubinov and Sporns (2010).
                    For an undirected and unweighted graph G(V,E) the connectivity pattern can be
                  described by a symmetric matrix with size n × n, named adjacency matrix A, whose

                    Small-world: A small-world network (Rubinov and Sporns 2010; Bassett and Bullmore

                  transfer across the network.

                       selection. In the first step, 68 articles were identified. The oldest article is from 1998

                       step, relevant articles were selected from the 68 articles using an abstract screening
                       process with inclusion criteria, as mentioned in Methodology section. We mainly

                       research subject, removing any research regarding animals, as well as non-graph-

                  metabolic connectivity), this approach can help detect early signs of abnormal brain
                  states, such as AD (Wang et al. 2020). Observations from our results underscore the
                  critical role of metabolic network analysis in enhancing our understanding of brain
                  function and related disorders. As shown in Fig. 1, there is a growing interest in

                  Healthy control‑based metabolic network analysis
                  In exploring brain connectivity within the healthy control group, previous studies reveal
                  valuable insights into the intricate organization of human brain networks. More specifi-
                  cally, in the study (Li et al. May 2023), authors concentrate on cross-modality similarity
                  and dissimilarity between metabolic and structural brain networks. Based on compar-
                  ing graph metrics computing from metabolic and structural graphs, it shows that both
                  networks exhibited small-world architecture; highlight the central role of default mode
                  network. The study came to the conclusion that there was a structural substrate that
                  supported the brain to globally and efficiently integrate and process metabolic interac-
                  tion across brain regions. The cross-modality cooperation or specialization in both met-
                  abolic and structure networks might imply mechanisms of achieving higher-order brain
                  functions.
                    Other studies (Yuxiao et al. 2015; Huang et al. 2020, 2022) have considered the effects
                  of age and gender to metabolic brain organization. Study (Huang et al. 2022) delves into
                  aging-related reorganization, noting shifts in modular architecture and reduced connec-
                  tor nodes in older age, particularly in temporo-occipital areas. Study (Huang et al. 2020)
                  focuses on brain developmental trajectories. Based on the analysis of metabolic net-
                  works from hundred subjects across different age groups, the study shows that both net-
                  work connectivity and global efficiency increase with age. Brain networks were grouped
                  into modules, showing that modular architecture was dramatically reorganized from
                  childhood to early adolescence. Hubs spatiotemporally rewired, and the ratio of connec-
                  tor hub to provincial hub also changed with age. Based on these studies (Huang et al.
                  2020, 2022), it appears that topological properties and modular reorganization of human
                  brain networks dramatically change with age, especially from childhood to early adoles-
                  cence. Not only age but gender might also modify brain metabolic connectivity. Study
                  (Yuxiao et al. 2015) explores gender differences in brain metabolic networks. Based on
                  the analysis of 400 healthy subjects, study (Yuxiao et al. 2015) showed while both gen-
                  ders exhibited small-world properties in metabolic networks with no significant global
                  gender differences, nodal differences were observed in a few brain regions. Hubs were
                  distributed bilaterally and showed lateralized patterns in females and males. In compar-
                  ing those studies together, all studies leverage graph theory to explore the topological
                  properties and modular architectures of brain networks, emphasizing the applicability of
                  this analytical framework for understanding complex neural systems. Moreover, effects
                  of age and gender on connectivity highlighted in the above studies suggest the need for a
                  multifaceted approach when studying brain connectivity. One should consider age, gen-
                  der, and their interplay in shaping the intricate architecture of the healthy brain.



                  Metabolic network patterns in brain disorders
                  Many brain disorders can be conceptualized as a disconnection phenomenon within
                  brain networks. In (Catani and Ffytche 2005), authors detail disconnection syndromes,
                  which occur when localized and specialized areas are disconnected to each other, lead-
                  ing to dysfunction. The disconnection phenomenon has been successfully applied to
                  analyze various types of brain disorders. For instance, in Yakushev et al. (2017) and
                  (Rahmani et al. 2020), authors reported that based on the analysis of network properties
Tuan et al. EJNMMI Reports   (2025) 9:4                                                                         Page 18 of 28




                  of metabolic brain networks using the graph theory, AD subjects could be distinguished
                  from control subjects. In the following, we will describe several studies which have used
                  graph theory and FDG-PET-based brain networks for the diagnosis of brain disorders.

                    1) Epilepsy: is a neurological disorder characterized by recurrent, unprovoked seizures.
                       Seizures occur due to abnormal and excessive electrical activity in the brain, leading
                       to temporary disruptions in normal brain function. The manifestations of seizures
                       can vary widely, ranging from brief lapses in attention or muscle jerks to more severe
                       convulsions. Temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) is a common form of partial epilepsy.
                       Studies (Wang et al. 2018; Ren 2021; Kyoo Ho Cho et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2023)
                       on metabolic networks in TLE provide a complementary understanding of altered
                       network dynamics, prognostic indicators, and specific regional changes in this dis-
                       ease. More specifically, study (Wang et al. 2018) examined the metabolic covariance
                       networks in mesial temporal lobe epilepsy (mTLE). The study highlighted damag-
                       ing and compensatory effects in metabolic networks of mTLE,—emphasizing the
                       role of reduced inter-hemisphere connection. Study (Ren 2021) focused on surgical
                       prognosis in drug resistant TLE. This study demonstrates that ongoing postoperative
                       seizures are associated with elevated networks integration, altered connectivity pat-
                       terns, and redistributed hub nodes. It provides insights into the complexity of TLE
                       cases that do not achieve seizure freedom after surgery. Study (Kyoo Ho Cho et al.
                       2022) investigated differences in metabolic networks among TLE patients with hip-
                       pocampal sclerosis, based on surgical outcomes following anterior temporal lobec-
                       tomy: seizure-free and non-seizure-free. Authors found that the seizure free group
                       had a lower assortative coefficient and widespread regional differences. This empha-
                       sizes the potential role of metabolic network analysis on FDG-PET to predict surgi-
                       cal outcomes in TLE patients with hippocampal sclerosis. Study (Wang et al. 2023)
                       focused on the default mode network (DMN) in TLE. This study reveals abnormal
                       metabolic connectivity patterns in DMN of TLE, particularly in regions like the bilat-
                       eral posterior cingulate gyrus and hippocampus. It provides insights into the dys-
                       function mechanism of DMN in TLE as well as pathophysiology of TLE. The above
                       studies gave interesting insights into the altered network dynamics associated with
                       TLE. They offer distinct perspectives on different aspects of TLE, ranging from sur-
                       gical prognosis and outcomes to specific network alterations in DMN; emphasizing
                       the importance of metabolic connectivity analysis in epilepsy.
                    2) Parkinson’s Disease: is a neurodegenerative disorder that primarily affects move-
                       ment. It is characterized by symptoms such as tremors, stiffness, slowness of move-
                       ment, and difficulty with balance and coordination. The condition results from the
                       gradual loss of dopamine-producing neurons in the brain. Previous studies using
                       FDG-PET-based metabolic network and graph theory analysis have emphasized the
                       important role of FDG-PET images as a valuable tool for both diagnosis and treat-
                       ment evaluation of PD. Authors in Mure et al. (2011) revealed a Parkinson’s disease
                       tremor-related metabolic network. When investigating metabolic brain networks of
                       PD patients with tremor, they found a distinct pattern involving cerebello-thalamo-
                       cortical pathways. This specific metabolic network, identified through FDG-PET
                       and analyzed using ordinal trends canonical variate analysis, demonstrated excellent
Tuan et al. EJNMMI Reports   (2025) 9:4                                                                           Page 19 of 28




                       reproducibility and responsiveness to interventions, providing a valuable objective
                       measure for evaluating treatment effects on tremor symptoms. The study (Ge et al.
                       2020) explored the potential of a metabolic network derived from PET in moni-
                       toring subthalamic nucleus deep brain stimulation (STN DBS) treatment for PD.
                       PD patients with STN DBS were longitudinally followed after FDG-PET scans. It
                       showed that Parkinson’s Disease Related Pattern (PDRP) expression was elevated in
                       PD patients, decreased significantly post-STN DBS treatment, and correlated with
                       clinical improvements. Based on that, authors concluded that PDRP could serve
                       as an objective biomarker for monitoring and assessing STN DBS treatment in PD
                       patients. The exploration of the rich-club organization in the metabolic connec-
                       tome of PD patients uncovered alterations in connections, suggesting potential early
                       markers for PD diagnosis (Peng et al. 2022). Lastly, the investigation into individual
                       metabolic connectomes using Jensen-Shannon Divergence Similarity Estimation
                       method demonstrated altered nodal properties in PD (Li et al. 2023), highlighting the
                       method’s promise for accurate PD diagnosis. In conclusion, these studies collectively
                       underscore the power of FDG-PET and graph theory in providing novel insights into
                       the intricate metabolic networks associated with PD, offering valuable tools for both
                       diagnosis and treatment evaluation.
                    3) Alzheimer’s Disease: is a neurodegenerative disorder affecting memory and cogni-
                       tive functions, leading to dementia. It is characterized by altered structural and func-
                       tional connectivity, making it a disconnection syndrome. Mild Cognitive Impair-
                       ment (MCI) is a condition that represents a noticeable decline in cognitive abilities
                       beyond what is expected with normal aging but is not severe enough to be classi-
                       fied as dementia. MCI can be a precursor to Alzheimer’s disease, but not everyone
                       with MCI will progress to AD. FDG-PET and graph theory have been extensively
                       used to study AD, revealing notable alterations primarily observed in the small-
                       worldness properties of metabolic brain networks in AD subjects ( Zhijun Yao et al.
                       2018; Zhijun Yao et al. 2015). More specifically, upon exploring brain network char-
                       acteristics, the main differences between AD and cognitive normal (CN) groups
                       includes reduced number of connectivity, global efficiency, clustering coefficient,
                       loss of small-world properties. The key patterns can be found in parieto-occipital,
                       and parieto-occipital connectivity. Graph theory applied to FDG-PET data in those
                       studies has also revealed changes in the inter and intra-hemispheric connectivity pat-
                       terns at both group-based and individual-based networks (Huang et al. 2018; Huang
                       et al. 2020). These metabolic network patterns not only serve as reliable biomarkers
                       for diagnostic purposes (Yao and Bin 2016; Huang et al. 2018) disease progression
                       assessment (Wang et al. 2020; Lee et al. 2014; Shengfu et al. 2017), but also can be
                       used to investigate the relationship between several factors regarding the risk of AD
                       progression. In (Zhijun Yao et al. 2015), authors used graph theory and FDG-PET-
                       based metabolic networks to explore the relationship between APOE ε4 and the risk
                       of AD progression. Based on the comparison of graph metrics between APOE ε4
                       carriers and noncarriers, the author concluded that there was a less optimal organi-
                       zation of metabolic networks in APOE ε4 carriers, which might explain the risk of
                       developing AD. Loss of small-world properties, topological aberrations, and altered
                       connectivity identified through graph theory differentiate CN from AD, and disease
Tuan et al. EJNMMI Reports    (2025) 9:4                                                                           Page 20 of 28




                        states like stable MCI (sMCI) and progressive MCI (pMCI). They also offer interest-
                        ing insights into the pathophysiological mechanisms driving cognitive dysfunction
                        (Zhijun Yao et al. 2018).
                        The application of machine learning has played a significant role as a powerful tool
                        for better diagnosing AD. Studies such as (Yao and Bin 2016; Li et al. 2018; Shengfu
                        et al. 2017) have harnessed the capabilities of machine learning as a diagnostic tool,
                        showcasing the potential of metabolic networks to achieve remarkable accuracy in
                        distinguishing disease groups and predicting clinical progression. The roles of FDG-
                        PET in these studies are pivotal, with metabolic networks emerging as sensitive bio-
                        markers for MCI progression (Shengfu et al. 2017), determining persistent features
                        for disease (Lee et al. 2014; Kuang et al. 2019), disease expression (Wang et al. 2020).
                        More specifically, in study (Kuang et al. 2019), authors focused on employing persis-
                        tent homology from algebraic topology to investigate alterations in network organi-
                        zation across various stages of AD. They developed a novel network measurement,
                        called Kernel-based integrated persistent feature (KBI), to quantify differences in
                        integrated persistent features curves in AD. Compared to standard graph metrics,
                        this new feature showed a superior sensitivity in revealing network disruptions
                        throughout the progression of AD. Based on that, authors suggested KBI as a poten-
                        tial preclinical AD imaging biomarker. In study (Wang et al. 2020), authors develop a
                        new method to capture individual pathological connectivity and use this method to
                        study metabolic connectome expression between sMCI and pMCI group. This new
                        method provides detailed individual information, greater stability, and is a better pre-
                        dictor of MCI conversion than other clinical assessments. In order to explore disrup-
                        tions in the modular structure of brain networks, study (Lee et al. 2014) investigated
                        persistent homology to detect higher-order topological invariants, called holes, in
                        AD metabolic networks. The combination of FDG-PET, graph theory, and machine
                        learning not only enhances diagnostic precision but also offers a deeper understand-
                        ing of the complex and evolving landscape of AD, paving the way for more effective
                        diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.
                        The landscape of Alzheimer’s disease analysis using graph theory has undergone
                        an interesting evolution, with a notable shift from traditional group-based network
                        studies to an individual approach. Studies at a group level utilized Pearson correla-
                        tion to compute graph connections (Huang et al. 2018; Zhijun Yao et al. 2018; Zhi-
                        jun Yao et al. 2015), while recent individual-level studies, such as (Wang et al. 2020;
                        Huang et al. 2020), use diverse methods to construct graphs. This approach distin-
                        guishes itself by focusing on the unique profile of patients, moving beyond gener-
                        alized group-based analyses. More specifically, in Yao and Bin (2016), authors used
                        cube to define node, and Pearson score for computing correlation matrix for AD
                        classification. In another work from the same authors (Shengfu et al. 2017), they used
                        brainnetome atlas to define nodes, and combined rough and precision distance to
                        compute correlation matrix and used them for predicting MCI. Both of those meth-
                        ods showed promising results. In study (Huang et al. 2020), the authors proposed
                        a new method for constructing an individual connectivity matrix based on adjust-
                        ments to a correlation matrix derived from a group of HC subjects. This method
                        involves normalizing the effect size of differences in metabolism level between brain
Tuan et al. EJNMMI Reports   (2025) 9:4                                                                          Page 21 of 28




                       regions in an individual PET scan and the average activity observed in the HC group.
                       Through experiments, they showed that the insights obtained from the individual
                       network align with the group-based network using Pearson scores. In a recent work
                       (Wang et al. 2020), authors proposed a novel method for constructing individual
                       brain networks using Kernel Density Estimation and Kullback–Leibler Divergence
                       Similarity Estimation. These methods offer superior performance in predicting MCI
                       conversion, and are adapted to the study of other brain disorders such as PD (Li et al.
                       2023), or even the brain of patients with cancer (Jie et al. 2023). These studies col-
                       lectively underscore the importance of individualized analyses, allowing for a more
                       nuanced understanding of AD heterogeneity. Moreover, the transition from group to
                       individual-based graph studies not only enhances diagnostic precision but also rep-
                       resents a promising trajectory for tailoring interventions to the unique profiles of AD
                       patients.
                    4) Others: The applications of FDG-PET-based metabolic networks and graph theory
                       are not limited to the most prevalent neurological disorders like AD and PD; they
                       also offer a valuable insight into the complexities of rare disorders and other neuro-
                       logical conditions. The studies on White Matter Hyperintensities (WMHs) (Ma et al.
                       2022) reported the impaired metabolic networks in patients with WMHs. The find-
                       ings reveal a distinctive redistribution of the brain metabolic network in individu-
                       als with WMHs. On the other hand, study (Liu et al. 2022) investigated metabolic
                       network patterns in presymptomatic microtubule-associated protein tau mutation
                       carriers for genetic frontotemporal dementia. Through metabolic network analysis
                       in asymptomatic carriers, the study identifies reconfigured hubs in brain regions, lost
                       hubs in specific regions, and compensation in task-related regions, enhanced met-
                       abolic connectivity in salience and default mode networks. Study (Liu et al. 2021)
                       focused on Meige Syndrome, a rare movement disorder. Firstly, authors identified
                       hypometabolism in the globus pallidus and thalamus on FDG-PET, which might
                       be a sign that the basal ganglia-thalamocortical motor circuit is altered. Secondly,
                       authors observed abnormal small-world properties and centrality changes int the
                       same regions found hypometabolic, reinforcing the possible roles of these regions in
                       the pathogenesis of Meige syndrome. Study (Huang et al. 2022) demonstrated altered
                       metabolic connectivity networks in AntiNmethyl-D-aspartate receptor encephalitis,
                       the most common autoimmune encephalitis. It showed the following: reduced global
                       and local efficiencies, longer characteristic path length, enhanced network functions
                       in specific regions, and poor internal connection integration in the left hemisphere.
                       Another subject of interest which is not a proper brain disorder, is the study of brain
                       function in patients with cancers, and more specifically, non-small cell lung cancer
                       (NSCLC). In (Jie et al. 2023), authors found significant differences in the degree and
                       betweenness distributions of brain network nodes between the NSCLC and con-
                       trol groups. Abnormal brain network connections and nodes were identified in the
                       temporal lobe, frontal lobe, and limbic system of patients with advanced NSCLC.
                       The predictive model, constructed using these abnormal brain network features,
                       showed a significant correlation with actual survival times of NSCLC patients. Based
                       on these observations, authors suggested that individual metabolic brain networks
                       could provide additional clinical information to guide further treatment. However,
Tuan et al. EJNMMI Reports   (2025) 9:4                                                                             Page 22 of 28




                       more studies and larger sample sizes are necessary to confirm this suggestion. Nev-
                       ertheless, it still emphasizes the significance of metabolic networks in understanding
                       cognitive dysfunctions in cancer patients.
                    5) Multiple/Cross disorder Analysis: Given the intricate landscape of neurological dis-
                       orders, it’s essential to acknowledge that multiple diseases can coexist within an
                       individual, giving rise to complex interplay. Additionally, some brain disorders may
                       exhibit similar symptoms and affect the same brain regions. This realization under-
                       scores the critical need for a cross-disorders approach to provide a holistic perspec-
                       tive on the interconnected nature of neurological conditions. Considering this, in
                       study (Imai et al. 2020), the author investigated the differences between AD patients,
                       dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) patients, and CN individuals. The main findings
                       are the whole metabolic network was preserved in CN individuals, while AD patients
                       exhibited diffusely decreased connection, and DLB patients showed a partially but
                       more deeply decreased connection. It also revealed significant metabolic topology
                       differences in the right posterior cingulate and the left transverse temporal gyrus
                       between AD and DLB. This study not only sheds light on commonalities and differ-
                       ences in affected brain regions but also emphasizes promise cross-disorder approach
                       to uncovering shared pathophysiological mechanisms, paving the way for more effec-
                       tive interventions different brain disorders.




                  Challenges and future directions
                  In this section, we briefly discuss several challenges and future perspectives in metabolic
                  network analysis. This section aims to address research question 5.

                    1) From Data Perspective: Limited number of related studies: Although FDG-PET dem-
                       onstrates great potential for analyzing and studying brain disorders, the application
                       of graph theory in analyzing metabolic-based networks is still limited compared to
                       other neuroimaging modalities such as fMRI (Farahani et al. 2019). Given that FDG-
                       PET can be considered as a proxy for regional neuronal activity (Rahmani et al.
                       2020), it can provide early signs of abnormal brain states. In the future, it would be
                       interesting to explore the effectiveness of graph theory and FDG-PET in other brain
                       and psychiatric disorders.
                       Small sample size: Many previous studies have had small sample sizes (smaller than
                       50 subjects for each group of data) (Mure et al. 2011; Wang et al. 2018; Ren 2021).
                       In order to archive more robust observations and generalized results, it is impor-
                       tant to increase the dataset size. Additionally, longitudinal studies are also crucial for
                       improved diagnosis and treatment monitoring.
                       Mono scale: Most previous studies solely rely on static FDG-PET images, implying
                       a single modality and a single time point. However, it would be interesting to create
                       a network that takes integrated information from a single modality but at multiple
                       time points (e.g., dynamic PET images) and scales (e.g., resolutions), or from mul-
                       tiple modalities. Recent studies demonstrate that FDG-PET images combined with
                       sMRI or fMRI can offer complementary views (Thielcke et al. 2017; Li et al. 2023).
Tuan et al. EJNMMI Reports   (2025) 9:4                                                                              Page 23 of 28




                       Therefore, combining networks from multiple modalities is expected to provide a
                       more comprehensive understanding about higher-order brain functions.
                       Mono nature: Most prior studies have focused on a single disorder at a time. How-
                       ever, recent research on cross-disorders (Heuvel and Sporns 2019; Lange and
                       Scholtens 2019) indicate that multiple diseases can have similar effects on the same
                       brain regions and being able to distinguish them is extremely important in order
                       to provide effective treatment. Hence, cross-disorder analysis is a crucial research
                       direction. It is necessary for better understanding and distinction between various
                       disorders, ultimately leading to improved diagnosis.
                    2) Building and Analyzing brain metabolic networks: Group-based or Individual-based
                       Network Analysis: When building metabolic-based networks from FDG-PET images,
                       previous studies have either followed group-based or individual-based approaches.
                       Individual approaches provide more precise information, contributing to better diag-
                       noses (Wang et al. 2020) and can be considered a step towards individualized treat-
                       ments or precision medicine. However, it is challenging to build an individual graph
                       from single static FDG-PET images, as the methods used need to capture both group
                       properties and subject variances. Therefore, for future work, it would be relevant to
                       study and explore optimal methods to build individual graphs. Moreover, it is also
                       important to compare multiple approaches and to compare the significance between
                       group-based approaches and individual methods to understand the similarities and
                       differences between them.
                       Brain network nodes: It’s obvious that the choice of nodes greatly affects the analy-
                       sis results. In the literature review, the most common choice for defining nodes is
                       the use of predefined brain atlases such as AAL, as in Huang et al. (2018); Wang
                       et al. 2020; Yuxiao et al. 2015). Besides using predefined atlas for defining nodes, the
                       cube-based approach can be used to define the network nodes. This approach can be
                       considered as an intermediate level analysis between voxel-based nodes, and region-
                       based nodes (Yao and Bin 2016). It can both represent details about nodes and over-
                       come the limitation of using voxel-based approach. Moreover, using adapted brain
                       mapping, which is more adapted to a specific type of brain disorders ( Pham Minh
                       Tuan et al. 2022) could be an interesting approach for future study. Lastly, examin-
                       ing the network at various node scales (e.g., ranging from hundreds to thousands of
                       nodes, or possibly more) can provide a complementary view of the disease, revealing
                       subtle patterns and connections that may not be apparent at a single scale.
                       Brain network connections: Similar to the selection of graph nodes, the choice of
                       connectivity measurements also significantly impacts graph construction and analy-
                       sis. In previous studies, for group-based analysis, Pearson correlation has been one
                       of the most commonly used connectivity metrics, as found in Yuxiao et al. (2015);
                       Huang et al. 2020; Kuang et al. 2019). Concerning individual-based analysis, most
                       common used metrics are information-based distance measures such as Kullback-
                       Leibler divergence (Wang et al. 2020; Jie et al. 2023) and Jensen Shannon divergence
                       (Li et al. 2023; Li et al. 2023); even though there is still room for exploring alternative
                       correlation methods and similarity measurements. It is also important to assess the
                       effectiveness of these approaches, their stability regarding artifacts, and their ability
                       to perform well across multiple scales.
Tuan et al. EJNMMI Reports   (2025) 9:4                                                                             Page 24 of 28




                       Furthermore, all the studies discussed so far have primarily focused on the con-
                       nectivity between two brain regions, often referred to as pairwise connections or
                       second-order relations. However, this approach tends to overlook the interactions
                       among multiple ROIs, known as higher-order relations. Considering that brain net-
                       works are inherently more complex and involve intricate relationships, exploring
                       higher-order connections can provide valuable insights into the non-random attrib-
                       utes of these networks (Sporns 2018). Tools from hypergraph theory (Bretto 2013)
                       can be utilized to represent and study these higher-order relations. For instance, in
                       a recent study (Jie et al. 2016), authors investigated a hypergraph-based approach to
                       construct a hypergraph from fMRI data and its applications in AD diagnosis. The
                       results of this study demonstrated that a hyperconnectivity-based network not only
                       enhances classification performance but also helps identify crucial disease-related
                       biomarkers. Therefore, it is worthwhile to explore the potential applications of hyper-
                       connectivity and hypergraph theory in the analysis of metabolic networks.
                       Graph metrics: Since, graph metrics play a pivotal role in extracting meaningful
                       information from the complex network structures, selecting optimal graph metrics
                       is an extremely important and crucial step before the analysis. There is no rules for
                       selecting optimal graph metrics, it depends on the type of study and its objectives.
                       Most commonly used graph metrics are shortest path length, global coefficient, local
                       efficient, small-worldness. Other types of metrics such as motif measurements and
                       resilient measurements can be used depending on the purpose of the study (Rubinov
                       and Sporns September 2010). Integrating results from different studies also raises a
                       big challenge. Graph metrics can exhibit variability with different network thresh-
                       olds, across diverse sources of datasets, and in the presence of varied graph construc-
                       tion methods. This variability hampers the reliability and generalizability of findings.
                       Additionally, addressing the need to establish a clear connection between graph met-
                       rics and cognitive meaning is crucial. As we delve into the intricate details of brain
                       network analysis, elucidating how specific graph metrics relate to cognitive functions
                       becomes essential for translating the findings into clinically relevant insights.
                    3) Neural Network as a tool for exploring brain connectivity: Studying brain disorders
                       across various scales and resolutions is crucial, yet it introduces complexities in
                       understanding networks and determining connectivity patterns. To address this chal-
                       lenge, the application of neural networks becomes paramount. Their inherent abil-
                       ity to autonomously learn pertinent features from data offers a promising solution.
                       Furthermore, within the realm of Deep Learning, a specific subset known as Graph
                       Neural Networks (GNNs) (Bessadok et al. 2023) has garnered significant interest
                       from the research community. Hence, employing deep learning and GNNs as tools
                       for analyzing metabolic networks appears particularly relevant. This approach holds
                       the potential to enhance our comprehension of underlying patterns and correlations
                       between different groups, paving the way for more effective analyses and interpreta-
                       tions.
                    4) Clinical Intervention: Transition from neuroimaging and graph theory analyses to
                       practical clinical applications is crucial for a better diagnosis and treatment. Translat-
                       ing insights gained from intricate graph analyses into actionable clinical diagnoses
                       and treatment strategies is paramount for the field’s advancement. Integrating neuro-
Tuan et al. EJNMMI Reports        (2025) 9:4                                                                                                 Page 25 of 28




                         imaging features into disease classification might help to define novel sub-types clas-
                         sification, based on pathophysiological mechanisms and not only clinical symptoms.
                         Following diagnosis, the predictive and monitoring capabilities of neuroimaging
                         and graph theory might help to better grasp disease progression, enabling accurate
                         tracking of changes over time or in response to interventions. This not only refines
                         diagnostic precision but also establishes a foundation for tailoring treatments to indi-
                         vidual patient needs.
                         Overcoming these challenges will be pivotal for advancing the utility of FDG-PET
                         and graph theory in unravelling the complexities of neurological disorders and cog-
                         nitive processes.


                  Conclusion
                  This paper offers a brief overview of graph theory and its application to the analysis of
                  FDG-based metabolic networks. We begin with a concise description of graphs and
                  graph metrics. Following that, we provide a summary of recent work on metabolic net-
                  works and graph theory, covering various aspects such as types of graphs and targeted
                  brain disorders. And in the final section, we provide a brief description of several chal-
                  lenges and emphasize some interesting research directions for the future.
