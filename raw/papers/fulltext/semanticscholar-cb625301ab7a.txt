Multiple sclerosis classification and segmentation in

Abstract
Multiple Sclerosis (MS) is a type of brain disease that affects both the brain and spinal
cord. In order to diagnose MS, many modalities are used, including Magnetic Resonance
Imaging (MRI). MRI modalities are noninvasive medical tests that provide physicians
with detailed images containing essential details on the anatomy and physiology of the
brain. Diagnosing with MRI of the brain is crucial since it is laborious, time-consuming,
and, above all, prone to manual or human error. In the past ten years, Artificial Intelligence
(AI)- based Computer Aided Diagnostic (CAD) tools have been increasingly popular due
to their low manual error rate and ability to produce accurate and dependable findings for
diagnosing Multiple Sclerosis (MS) using MRI neuroimaging modalities. Automated MS
diagnosis in AI is performed using both traditional and contemporary AI. Machine Learn-
ing (ML) approaches, which rely on selecting and extracting features through trial and
error, were utilized in classical AI. Meanwhile, modern AI uses Deep Learning (DL) tech-
niques, which extract and select suitable features automatically, save time and are more
efficient than standard classical ML approaches. In this work, we give a summary of recent
automated MS diagnosis approaches that combine MRI neuroimaging modalities with ML
and DL algorithms. Segmentation and classification are two of the main categories into
which AI techniques for MS medical diagnosis can be roughly divided, where each one of
them is divided based on whether a supervised learning, un-supervised learning or both are
used together to get better analysis for MS diagnosis. We briefly discuss the related work
in each category, and finally, we present the important drawbacks and challenges for each
work as well as some proposed ideas that can solve the drawbacks and challenges faced.

Keywords Multiple sclerosis · MRI modalities · CAD tools · Machine learning · Deep

1 Introduction

The chronic autoimmune disease known as multiple sclerosis (MS) is caused by the immune
system attacking the brain and spinal cord because of an erroneous action (BetterHealth
Channel 2021). Multiple sclerosis usually results in damage to the neurological system,
which includes the myelin sheath, nerve fibers, and cells that make up myelin. If these inju-
ries are not particularly severe, they go away in a few days to a few weeks; if they are, they
may result in lasting alterations to the spinal cord. The condition is also known as multiple
sclerosis because the lesions, which are irreversible alterations, occur in multiple locations
in the brain. Four MS characteristics were taken into account: Clinical Isolated Syndrome
(CIS) (Efendi 2016), Relapsing Remitting MS (RRMS) (Cunill et al. 2018), Secondary Pro-
gressive MS (SPMS) (Ziemssen et al. 2022), and Primary Progressive MS (PPMS) (Maca-
ron and Ontaneda 2019).
    Over the last decade, MRI modalities have been used in the medical field, where phy-
sicians mainly depend on discovering non-invasive and critical pathologies. Many MRI
modalities were existed including T1- weighted (T1-w) (Chen et al. 2018a), Fluid Attenu-
ated Inversion Recovery (FLAIR) (Bailey 2007), T2- weighted (T2-w) (Chen et al. 2018b).
Figure 1. Shows different MRI modalities. The brain seems to have a brighter core and
darker surrounding areas on T1 and T2 images. With FLAIR scans, the opposite happens,
with lighter tones surrounding and darker portions in the center. High-fat tissues, such as
white matter, look bright on T1-w scans, and water-filled compartments are visible. Accord-
ing to (Hrishi and Sethuraman 2019), cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) looks dark. This works well
to illustrate anatomy. Tissues having a high fat content, such white matter, look dark on a
T2-w scan, while compartments filled with water, like CSF compartments, seem bright. This
is a great method to show pathology because most, but not all, lesions (damaged tissue)
have a tendency to cause edema and are associated with an increase in water content. As of
right now, this order is the most accurate method for assessing the brain for demyelinating
illnesses like multiple sclerosis.
    Physicians find it difficult and time-consuming to manually diagnose and report MS
regions in MRI neuroimaging modalities. Therefore, CAD tools are required to address
these issues. For this reason, in order to precisely pinpoint these locations, experts are com-
ing up with new approaches. AI methods are becoming a valuable diagnostic tool for a range
of diseases when used in conjunction with medical professionals. To obtain better analysis
for MS diagnosis, AI methods for diagnosing disorders can be broadly classified into (i) ML

    While many literatures have applied different techniques, they generally follow a stan-

analysis between different methods used in the literature along with the evaluation measures

2.2 eHealth lab

The T2-w MRI modalities of 38 patients (17 males and 21 females) with MRI brain lesions
and a mean age of 34.1 ± 10.5 years are available in the eHealth Lab dataset. The modalities
were recorded twice, with a gap of 6–12 months.

2.3 ISBI 2015

Nineteen MRI scans of MS patients with two tests and a training package are included in
the ISBI 2015 dataset. There are five subjects in the training set: four have four time points
each, and one has five time points. Ten subjects make up Test Set A: eight have four time
points, one has five time points, and one has six time points. There are four subjects in
test set B: two have five time points and three have four. T1-wi MPRAGE, T2-w & PD-w
DSE, T2-w, and FLAIR modalities are present in all participants. Both the original and the
pre-processed photos are included in this collection. Also, two specialists have carried out
manual segmentation.

2.4 IXI

Almost 600 MRI pictures of healthy, normal patients are included in the IXI dataset. For
every subject, the MR image acquisition routine consists of T1-w, T2- w, PD-w, MRA, and
diffusion-weighted images. Three distinct hospitals in London have provided the data: the
Institute of Psychiatry, which used a GE 1.5 T system, Guy's Hospital, which used a Philips
1.5 T system, and Hammersmith Hospital, which used a Philips 3 T system.

2.5 long-MR-MS

The longitudinal MRI images of 20 MS patients with matching ground truth segmentations
of changes in white matter lesions are included in the long-MR-MS dataset. Every patient
has undergone two independent imaging sessions. Each patient's folder has a collection of
photographs. Co-registered and N4 corrected T1-w, T2-w, and FLAIR images for both MR

2.6 MICCAI 2008

MRI images from Boston Children's Hospital (CHB) and the University of North Carolina
(UNC) were gathered for the MICCAI 2008 dataset. A CHB expert and two UNC experts
each segmented every image in the collection. Twenty samples total—ten CHB and ten
UNC samples—were manually separated from the CHB expert in the training database.
There is no segmentation present in the test dataset, which consists of 25 samples (15 CHB
samples and 10 UNC samples). All of the T1-w, T2-w, FLAIR, DTI-derived FA, and MD
image modalities are included in the dataset. On this database, numerous pre-processing
procedures have also been carried out.

2.7 MICCAI 2016 (MSSEG)

Three sequences are available in the MICCAI 2016 (MSSEG) dataset: T1-w, T2-w, and
FLAIR. It contains 53 MS sufferers' MRI pictures. Three 3 T magnets and one 1.5 T mag-
net are included in these photos, which were produced by Siemens, Philips, and GE, three
distinct French centers. MRI images that have already been manually segmented by seven
professionals are included in this collection. Additionally, it underwent preprocessing using
the following techniques: image denoising, image registration, intensity standardization,
and skull stripping. There are training and testing versions of this dataset. sets of, corre-
spondingly, 15 and 38 patients. Every patient also has access to the modalities (T1-w, T2-w,
and FLAIR) that were previously described.

2.8 MICCAI 2021 (MSSEG-2)

One hundred patients' worth of MRI neuroimaging data make up the MICCAI 2021
(MSSEG-2) dataset. Every patient has had two sets of 3D FLAIR images taken, with vari-
able amounts of time elapsed between the two scans: a first timepoint 3D FLAIR sequence
and a second timepoint (1 to 3 years following the first one). Following that, those pic-
tures were registered in the space between the two time points. New MS lesions (those that
appeared at the second time-point but not at the first) were manually segmented by four
specialists. Lesions identified by three out of four experts were chosen, and the remaining
lesions (those identified by two or fewer experts) were examined by a different expert for
confirmation or rejection. 60% of the lesions in the dataset are part of the testing set after it
has been separated into a testing set and a training set.

3 Preprocessing

The learning process to machines is the backbone process that makes any ML/DL model
capable of being efficient and powerful enough to deal with real-world problems. Typically,
any real-world dataset may require some preprocessing techniques to prepare the incoming
data for efficient learning or even for production. In the medical field, especially MRI neu-
roimaging, some problems (e.g., random noise, low-frequency variation in signal intensity,
existence of unimportant brain parts, scanner-dependent intensity variations) can affect the
data negatively. This requires applying a set of preprocessing methods to solve these prob-
lems. In the next sub-sections, the set of preprocessing techniques which have been used by
the literature are being discussed.

3.1 Data augmentation

While medical MS diagnosis is suffering from lack of huge data, such a technique is
required to handle it or somehow reduces the gap. In data augmentation, we have a small
number of dataset instances and try to increase it by applying some traditional geometrical
transformation (Shear, Flipp, Rotation, and/or translation) or some other techniques. (Barile
et al. 2021a) used GANs to generate new images. This would lead to increase performance
when the model learns new features by adding new data to the existing one (Mikołajczyk
and Grochowski 2018).

3.2 Image registration

While MRI can be generated from different protocol settings. Different modality types are
acquired such as (T1-w, T2-w, and FLAIR images). Therefore, we need to combine these
different modalities into one single image or in other words to place them into one standard
space before MS diagnostic process (Zhang et al. 2019; Dağdeviren et al. 2015).

3.3 Skull stripping

In MRI neuroimaging studies, the most important area is the brain tissue because it contains
all the important features. Thus, to improve the accuracy of the automated MS diagnosis,
non-brain tissues such the mouth, nose, eyes, and skull are removed (Kalavathi and Prasath
2015). Figure 4. Shows an example of applying skull stripping to a brain MR image.

3.4 Bias correction

The intensity of inhomogeneity is referred to as the bias field. An extremely smooth, low
frequency signal known as the bias field signal has the ability to contaminate MRI images,
particularly those obtained with outdated MRI equipment (Manson et al. 2022). Segmen-
tation, texture analysis, and classification techniques in image processing that rely on the
gray level values of picture pixels will not yield acceptable outcomes. Before presenting
damaged MRI images to a model, a pre-processing procedure is essential to fix the bias field
signal (Vovk et al. 2007). The N4 bias field correction algorithm is a widely used technique
for rectifying bias or gain fields, which are low frequency intensity non-uniformities found
in MRI image data (Tustison et al. 2010).

3.5 Intensity standardization

An essential preprocessing step in brain MRI analysis is intensity standardization. Signifi-
cant intensity differences may occur when multiple scanners or parameters are utilized to
scan the same subject at different times or different subjects at different times during MR
image collection. The performance of subsequent MRI processing and population analysis,
including image registration, segmentation, and tissue volume assessment, is seriously com-

During MRI recording process, it is possible to have some corruption to the images by a
variety of erratic noises. Thus, several techniques are used to eliminate these noises. Such as

et al. 1985; Cobo et al. 2014), and Fourier transform (McVeigh et al. 1985; Stetter et al.

4 Features extraction and diagnosis

Extraction of features from the processed or raw data is the next stage. Two trends were fol-
lowed to extract features, manual and automatic feature extraction. Manual feature extrac-
tion requires human intervention. Meanwhile automatic feature extraction does not require
any human intervention instead DL mechanisms are used to automatically extract features
from images. The diagnosis process for MS in the reviewed literature is mainly divided
into two parts, segmentation, and classification where each of them is divided to supervised
and un-supervised learning methods as shown in detail in Fig. 7. The next section briefly
discusses these concepts.

split into two main categories. These categories are (i) segmentation and (ii) classification,

analysis for MS diagnosis. In this section, the top 3 research works for each technique are

5.1 Multiple sclerosis lesions segmentation

This section presents two subgroups of learning techniques—supervised and unsupervised
learning—that are utilized for automatic MS segmentation on MRI neuroimaging. Each of
them is branched into different techniques. In the supervised approach, different methods
have been used such as CNN architectures (Long et al. 2015; Yang et al. ) or U-Nets (Ron-
neberger et al. 2015). Meanwhile, for literature following unsupervised learning approach,
they applied pure Autoencoders architectures (Bank et al. 2021) or hybrid Autoencoders
with RNNs (Sherstinsky 2020).

5.1.1 Supervised learning methods

The majority of the research on supervised learning techniques concentrated mostly on
two well-known techniques: (i) pure CNN/hybrid CNN-RNN and (ii) U- Net architectures,
which will be covered in more detail in the next subsections. Moreover, Table 2 and Table 3
provide comparison between research works in each sub-category according to the used
dataset, preprocessing techniques used, and the proposed methodology.

5.1.1.1 Pure CNNs or hybrid CNNs-RNNs Rondinella et al. (2023) used a Hybrid version
of CNN-RNN architecture where they proposed a framework that exploited an adaptive ver-
sion of CNN architecture with a convolutional Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) (Shi et
al. 2015) layer and attention mechanism (Zhong et al. 2020). Magnetic resonance imaging
of multiple sclerosis lesions was able to be segmented and quantified using the framework
that was applied. They applied their model on ISBI 2015 dataset and used a set of prepro-
cessing techniques such as image registration, bias correction, and skull stripping.

    Fenneteau et al. (2020) used CNN model with lesion patches extraction. Their model was
a modification of U-Net architecture. They applied the proposed model on MICCAI 2008,
MICCAI 2016 (MSSEG), ISBI 2015, OASIS-3, BraTS 2017, and clinical (private) datasets.
Afterward they used a preprocessing tool called FSL (Jenkinson and Smith 2001; Greve and
Fischl 2009; Jenkinson et al. 2002).
    The work of Afzal et al. (2021) consisted of two parallel 2D-CNNs where the first one
segmented MS lesions accurately and the second one increased the segmentation efficiency
by reducing lesions’ false positive rate. One pathway, which was connected to the second,
was essential to its construction. CNN had taken the position of the fully connected layer
by the end. The model was fed three distinct MRI sequences: T1-w, T2-w, and FLAIR. Two
public datasets were used including ISBI 2015 and MICCAI 2016 (MSSEG). Finally, the
patch extraction method was applied before feeding data to the model.
    It can be concluded that most literature in 2019 and 2020 used CNNs and their variations.
Most of the works preferred to work with their private clinical datasets where ISBI 2015
is the most publicly used dataset. For preprocessing, most of the works used bias field cor-
rection, image registration, and skull stripping as they show a great effect on performance.
Another remarkable note is that using CNNs along with RNNs gave a significant improve-
ment due to its ability to capture spatial and temporal features at the same time.
    Of all reviewed literature in this sub-section, most of them used traditional CNN archi-
tectures with variations in the dataset used and the preprocessing technique which did not

5.1.1.2 U-Nets With a 2D U-Net architecture, Narayana et al. (2020a) used a fully convo-
lutional neural network to autonomously separate lesions, GM, WM, and CSF across 1000
MS patients. Fifteen combinations of FLAIR, T1-w, T2-w, and PD-w pictures made up the
network's input. They applied a variety of preprocessing methods, including skull stripping,
bias correction, intensity standardization, image denoising, and data augmentation.

    Kumar et al. (2019) presented a Dense U-Net which consisted mainly of dense blocks
and U-Net which was a modification of the original U-Net. First, their model took four inde-
pendent input image modalities (T1-w, T2-w, T1-c, and FLAIR) and passed them to a dense
block where they applied some convolutions and skip connection operations. Afterwords,
they passed the output to the max pooling layer in the network. The same operation was
repeated until they reached the bottleneck in the encoding part. The decoding part applied
the same operation but in reverse manner. Their model replaced the first 3 convolution lay-
ers at the beginning and the end of the original U-Net with dense blocks. They used MIC-
CAI 2016 (MSSEG) as their dataset, while applying some preprocessing methods such as
removing null data samples, scaling intensity values towards zero (Reinhold et al. 2019),
and data augmentation.
    Narayana et al. (2020b) also presented another version of U-Net architecture with a small
modification making it multiclass U-Net which was different from the original U-Net. The
model was used to semantically segment four different classes GM, WM, CSF, and MS
lesions. They used a clinically private dataset consisting of FLAIR, T2-w, PD-w, and T1-w
in total of 1008 images. The dataset was preprocessed before feeding to the model using
skull stripping, bias correction, intensity standardization, and image denoising.
    It can be concluded that U-Nets were more popular than CNNs due to their ability and
efficiency to work with complex segmentation problems, especially medical problems. Most
of the works preferred to work with their private clinical datasets as well as ISBI 2015 pub-
lic dataset, and for preprocessing, most of the works used skull stripping, data augmenta-
tion, bias field correction, and image registration as they show a great effect on performance.
    It can be estimated from the top three works that the third and the first group of authors
(Narayana et al. (2020b) and Narayana et al. (2020a), respectively) applied two different
approaches with the same dataset and slight differences in the U-Net architecture, Narayana
et al. (2020b) validated the need for a large set of MRI images to perform well while using
FLAIR and T1w modalities. However, using dense blocks along with U-Net and multi-
modal MRIs as input even with a small set of data made a good performance by Kumar et
al. (2019). The work by Narayana et al. (2020a) achieved the top performance using the
same method of multimodal MRI inputs but with a large enough dataset and different U-Net
architecture. Figure 8. Shows the output for a UNETR (Hatamizadeh et al. 2022) model tak-

   Gessert et al. (2020b) developed a combined version of Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) and
Autoencoder models to improve MS lesion segmentation performance by incorporating the
temporal features with the spatial ones. They used a clinical dataset and did not apply any
preprocessing to the data.
   Aslani et al. (2020) presented architecture of Autoencoder and Regularization Network.
This architecture depended on three different parts encoder, decoder, and regularization net-
work. Encoder and decoder are the main part of autoencoder network where the regulariza-
tion network is the main core to enhance the MS lesion segmentation task's performance.
The regularization network consisted of three perceptron layers and a softmax layer. The
main goal was to take the output feature vector of the encoder and feed it to the regulariza-
tion network then feed the output to the decoder. This improved the results significantly as
the additional network helped to prevent overfitting. They used a clinical dataset and applied
only two preprocessing methods including skull stripping and image registration.
   Andresen et al. (2022) proposed an autoencoder with a small modification where their
architecture depended on an encoder that consisted of baseline, follow up, and their subtrac-
tion image. It had two separate decoders, each taking the output of the encoder and produc-
ing separate outputs. Creating the diffeomorphic deformation field for baseline registration
and providing it for follow-up was the first one's function, whilst the second one created
a segmentation mask with all MS lesions, if any, if they were present. They applied their
methodology on MICCAI 2021 (MSSEG-2) dataset, while applying image registration and
data augmentation.

5.2 Multiple sclerosis classification

In this section, two learning methods are presented for the classification task of MS using
MRI neuroimaging, including classical ML and modern DL learning. The use of classi-
cal ML techniques requires extracting input features manually and then applying one of
the classical ML algorithms. Meanwhile, modern DL methods are efficient and intelligent
enough to learn information from any input automatically and without any human interven-
tion. In the next sub-sections, both approaches are described along with summary tables for
each approach by the end of each section.

5.2.1 Classical machine learning

The works that applied classical ways to classify MS, used both supervised and unsuper-
vised methods. The research works that used classical ML techniques are summarized for
comparison in Table 5.
   Barile et al. (2021b) proposed a new approach by applying Diffusion Tensor Imaging
(DTI) (Cao et al. 2017; Kocevar et al. 2016) methodology and Non-negative Tensor Factor-
ization (NTF) on structural graph data. The brain white matter regions impacted by notable
longitudinal fluctuations allowed them to identify abnormal patterns. They performed unsu-
pervised classification of the MS into one of 3 phenotypes (CIS, RRMS, or SPMS) using a
clinical (private) dataset and K-Means algorithm (Silveira et al. 2020).
   Barile et al. (2022a) developed another approach for classifying MS clinical profiles
(CIS, RRMS, SPMS, and PPMS) utilizing six global graph metrics that were taken from the
cerebral Gray Matter (GM) morphological connectome of MS patients. This study showed
that by integrating four basic machine learning models— (Logistic Regression (LR) (Cox
1958), Random Forest (RF) (Breiman 2001), SVM (Cortes and Vapnik 1995), and Ada-
Boost (Schapire 2013) —GM morphological connectivity data may yield good classifica-
tion performances, without the cost of long and complex MRI techniques, such as MRI
diffusion (Baliyan et al. 2016), and/or deep learning architectures. They applied their meth-
odology on a clinical (private) dataset while performing some of the well-known prepro-
cessing methods such as skull stripping, bias correction, image registration, and intensity
standardization.

5.3 Other machine learning based analysis for multiple sclerosis diagnosis

Beyond segmentation and classification, machine learning has expanded its application
in MS research to include disease progression prediction, synthetic image generation, and
anomaly detection. Predictive models, such as those by Zhang et al. (2023), utilize time-
series and recurrent neural networks to forecast disease progression, offering insights for
personalized treatment. Given the limited availability of MS datasets, studies like Salem
et al. (2019) employed an encoder decoder U-Net to create synthetic MRI images, improv-
ing model robustness. Additionally, anomaly detection models, such as those developed
by Castellano et al. (2023), use autoencoders to distinguish between MS cases from those
are being healthy. These advancements illustrate the breadth of ML applications in MS
research, enhancing both the accuracy and scope of analysis.

   Gessert et al. (2020b) proposed a method that depended on constructing a 4D input to
a model of which it is a combination of GRU and autoencoder to detect and segment MS

and its task is to detect and segment MS lesion activity between BL and FU using a spatio-
temporal tensor x ∈ RT ×H×W ×D . The idea of constructing 4D spatio-temporal is to extract
more reliable and hidden features by learning the activity of MS lesion through time. Their
methodology achieved the best DSC, TPR, and FPR scores with scores of 64%, 64%, and
19%, respectively.
   Schlaeger et al. (2023) proposed a 3D autoencoder which consisted of an encoder and
one decoder. Two different MRI sequences were taken patch extraction operation were made
to both then the same encoder path was fed these extracted patches with shared weights. The
decoder received the concatenated feature maps for the T1-weighted and FLAIR input. The
predicted segmentation mask showed lesions that enhanced the MS contrast. This meth-
odology helped them to extract more reliable and informative features from two different
sequences. They achieved the highest PPV/precision with a score of 91%.

7.2 Machine learning risks

Using ML in medical imaging, especially for conditions like MS, has great potential but also
carries significant risks. Some key risks include:

● Bias and fairness in model predictions:

Machine learning models in MS imaging can be prone to bias due to the composition of
their training data. In the classification problem, the model’s performance may be skewed
if it is trained on an imbalanced dataset. For instance, if certain MS types or healthy cases
are over-represented, the model may perform well on these categories but poorly on under-
represented MS subtypes, leading to misclassification. In the segmentation problem, where
the model segments and identifies MS lesions, the model could struggle if the dataset con-
tains lesions predominantly of one size or severity. A bias towards larger lesions could result
in missed detection of smaller lesions, which are critical for early diagnosis and monitoring
of disease progression. Addressing this bias requires careful data curation to ensure a bal-
anced representation of different MS subtypes, healthy controls, and lesion sizes, allowing
the model to generalize effectively across the spectrum of MS presentations.

● Data quality and variability:

Medical imaging data, especially in MS, is prone to variability across different healthcare
institutions due to variations in MRI protocols, equipment settings, and technician expertise.
Such variability can introduce noise and artifacts into the data, which can reduce the reli-
ability of machine learning models. Additionally, inconsistencies in image quality, patient
positioning, or contrast use may further affect model predictions. Therefore, ensuring high
data quality and developing models robust to these variances are critical challenges in
applying ML to MS diagnosis. Standardizing imaging protocols and using techniques such
as data augmentation and normalization can help mitigate this risk.

● Overfitting and generalization:

One common issue in developing machine learning models is overfitting, where the model
becomes too specialized in the training data and fails to generalize to new, unseen data.
In the case of MS, where lesion characteristics and disease progression vary significantly
across patients, an overfitted model might perform well in controlled experimental settings
but fail in real-world clinical environments. This is particularly problematic when train-
ing on small or homogeneous datasets. To prevent overfitting, techniques such as cross-
validation, regularization, and the use of larger, more diverse datasets should be employed,
allowing the model to generalize better to the broader MS population.

● Model interpretability:

Interpretability is a crucial aspect of deploying ML models in clinical practice, particu-
larly for diseases like MS that require nuanced decision-making. Many advanced models,
especially deep learning-based approaches, are often considered “black boxes,” making it
difficult for clinicians to understand why a certain diagnosis or prediction was made. This
lack of transparency can erode trust in the model’s recommendations, limiting its clinical
adoption. For ML models to be useful in MS diagnosis and treatment, they must offer clear,
interpretable outputs that provide insights into which features (e.g., lesion characteristics or
volume) drive their decisions. Techniques such as attention mechanisms (Guo et al. 2022),
saliency maps (Boyd et al. 2022), or simpler models can help bridge this gap between model
complexity and interpretability.

● Regulatory and ethical considerations:

ML models designed for medical applications face significant regulatory and ethical hurdles
before they can be widely adopted in clinical settings. In the context of MS, regulatory
agencies such as the FDA (2019) require rigorous validation and evaluation of ML models
to ensure they meet safety and efficacy standards. Ethical concerns also arise regarding the
transparency of these models and the protection of sensitive patient data used to train them.
Given the life-impacting nature of MS diagnoses and treatments, it is essential that ML
models are not only accurate but also comply with regulatory guidelines and ethical stan-
dards to maintain patient trust and safety. Developing explainable, privacy-preserving mod-
els while navigating the regulatory landscape will be key to their successful deployment.

8 Future research directions

In this section we present research directions which may be able to solve some of the previ-
ously discussed problems or limitations and help to get better and reliable results.

8.1 Data augmentation:

Multiple sclerosis diagnosis aspects: Segmentation and classification
   Problem:
   Limited publicly available MS datasets, ethical issues regarding patient data, and class
imbalance (fewer MS samples compared to healthy controls (HC) or fewer small MS
lesions) pose significant challenges to both classification and segmentation tasks.
   Prospective Solution(s):
   Traditional techniques: Apply geometric transformations like rotation, flipping, crop-
ping, and translation to increase the diversity of training data (Mikołajczyk and Grochowski
2018). These techniques are relatively simple but effective for addressing small sample
sizes.
   Advanced synthetic data generation: Cutting-edge methods, such as U-Nets or Auto-
encoders, can generate high-quality synthetic MS images by learning from the existing data.
Approaches like those by El-Assiouti et al. (2023) and Salem et al. (2019) have demon-
strated the potential of such models to simulate MS pathology with higher accuracy, specifi-
cally targeting the generation of small lesions, a critical limitation in segmentation tasks.
   Potential advancements: Investigation into applying diffusion models (Wu et al. 2024)
or GAN variants like StyleGAN (Karras et al. 2019) for more detailed synthesis of MS
lesions could provide further accuracy in medical image generation. Focus on simulating
rare MS lesions that can improve performance in real-world diagnosis.

8.2 Domain adaptation

Multiple sclerosis diagnosis aspects: Segmentation and classification
   Problem:
   Limited target domain data for MS classification and lesion segmentation tasks.
   Prospective solution(s):
   Transfer learning: Leverage large-scale MRI datasets from similar domains (e.g., brain
tumors) and fine-tune the model using the limited MS data. Domain adaptation strategies
by Ghafoorian et al. (2017) and Kamnitsas et al. (2017) have shown promise in brain lesion
segmentation.
   Adversarial domain adaptation: Techniques such as those discussed by Guan and Liu
(2021) can be explored further, where adversarial learning adjusts the decision boundary
between source and target domains, enhancing the model’s adaptability to the target MS
domain.
   Potential advancements: Further research should focus on domain-specific loss func-
tions to better capture fine-grained differences between brain tumor lesions and MS lesions.
Additionally, unsupervised domain adaptation techniques that reduce dependency on
labeled data should be explored.

8.3 Image-to-image translation

Multiple sclerosis diagnosis aspects: Segmentation task
    Problem:
    Lack of direct access to sufficient MS MRI images and the challenge of transferring
lesion characteristics from one domain (e.g., brain tumors) to another (MS lesions).
    Prospective solution(s):
    GANs for MRI translation: Use GAN-based models to map between MRI images from
different medical conditions. Zhu et al. (2017) introduced CycleGAN, which is a promising
approach for generating synthetic MS images from a related domain such as brain tumor
MRI.
    Application in small lesion synthesis: Advanced GAN architectures like StyleGAN3
(Zhu et al. 2023) or Diffusion Models (Wu et al. 2024) can be explored to create better qual-
ity and higher resolution images of MS lesions. These can be especially useful for generat-
ing hard-to-detect small MS lesions that are underrepresented in current datasets.
    Potential advancements: Focus on extending image-to-image translation to multi-
modal MRI datasets, combining different MRI sequences to create more diverse and realis-
tic synthetic images for both classification and segmentation tasks.

8.4 Zero-shot learning (ZSL):

Multiple sclerosis diagnosis aspects: classification task
    Problem:
    The inability to generalize existing models to handle unseen data distributions, particu-
larly when dealing with new MS cases that were not part of the training dataset.
    Prospective solution(s):
    ZSL for MS classification: ZSL enables models trained on “seen” domains (e.g., brain
tumors) to classify “unseen” domains such as MS clinical profiles or distinguish between
MS and HC cases, as proposed by Kodirov et al. (2017) in the Semantic Autoencoder for
Zero-Shot Learning (SAE-ZSL).
    Generalized ZSL for real-world adaptability: Techniques discussed by Pourpanah et
al. (2023) offer solutions for generalizing ZSL to real-world scenarios, where models need
to adapt rapidly to new data types without additional training.
    Potential advancements: Further work should focus on improving ZSL for rare lesion
types in MS, developing hybrid models that combine ZSL with few-shot learning for better
adaptability. Additionally, investigation of how ZSL can be incorporated into the segmenta-
tion domain to identify unseen lesion types.
