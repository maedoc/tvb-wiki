15                                                             Abstract

16              The study of brain activity and its function requires the development of computational
17           models alongside experimental investigations to explore different effects of multiple mecha-
18           nisms at play in the central nervous system. Chemical neuromodulators, such as dopamine,
19           play central roles in regulating the dynamics of neuronal populations. In this work, we propose
20           a modular framework to capture the effects of neuromodulators at the neural mass level. Using
21           this framework, we formulate a specific model for dopamine dynamics affecting D1-type recep-
22           tors. We detail the dynamical repertoire associated with dopamine concentration evolution
23           and characterize the transitions across qualitatively different oscillatory states as a function
24           of relevant parameters, along with the variations in frequency of the emergent multi-scale
25           oscillations.




                                                                   1
26   1     Introduction
27   Neuromodulators, such as dopamine and serotonin, play central roles in regulating the dynamics
28   of neuronal populations within the central nervous system. These biochemical agents modulate
29   the activities of multiple neurons simultaneously, acting on the global dynamics of the brain.
30       Neuromodulators can regulate the intrinsic excitability of neurons, influencing their firing rates
31   and patterns of activity [51, 56]. Additionally, at the synaptic level, they can modify the strength
32   of connections between neurons, impacting synaptic transmission and plasticity.
33       On a broader scale, neuromodulators contribute to the synchronization and coordination of
34   neuronal ensembles, thereby shaping network dynamics involved in various cognitive and behavioral
35   processes [6,22,54,59,64,68]. For instance, dopamine is implicated in reward processing, motivation
36   and motor control [7], while serotonin is associated with mood regulation, sleep-wake cycles, and
37   emotional processing. Dopaminergic pathways have also been implicated in cognitive processes
38   and behaviors such as wakefulness, working memory, and cognitive control [58].
39       Whole-brain models incorporating the effects of various neuromodulators have been used to
40   investigate the variability in cognitive decline associated with aging [12]. In [38], aging-related
41   changes in dopaminergic regulation have been linked to cognitive performance [5] and hypothe-
42   sized to play a crucial role in dynamic compensation as a marker of improved cognitive abilities
43   during aging. Impairment in dopamine neuromodulation is also known to play a role in different
44   neurological disorders [36], such as Parkinson’s disease (PD) or Schizophrenia [35].
45       Psychiatric disorders encompass a heterogeneous group of conditions that disrupt cognition,
46   emotional regulation, and behavior, affecting 970 million people worldwide in 2019, according to
47   the World Health Organization [50]. Among them, schizophrenia impacts approximately 24 million
48   individuals globally. PD and psychiatric disorders are distinct neurological conditions with unique
49   clinical features and prevalence rates. PD, the second most common neurodegenerative disorder,
50   primarily presents with motor symptoms such as tremors, rigidity, and bradykinesia, along with
51   non-motor symptoms. Its prevalence increases with age, affecting 0.04% of individuals aged 40-49
52   and 2% of those over 80 [4, 53].
53       In particular, the pathophysiology of PD is characterized by the degeneration of dopamine-
54   producing neurons in the substantia nigra of the basal ganglia, leading to the loss of dopaminergic
55   nigrostriatal neurons along the nigrostriatal pathways, with milder losses in the mesolimbic and
56   mesocortical circuits.
57       While empirical data provide critical insights into dopamine’s role, computational models are
58   essential for integrating diverse experimental findings, testing mechanistic hypotheses, and predict-


                                                       2
59   ing emergent neural dynamics that may not be directly observable. Models allow for the systematic
60   manipulation of parameters, simulation of pathological states, and exploration of causal relation-
61   ships. To model the neuromodulatory aspects of these pathways within personalized whole-brain
62   network models, we need to capture structural changes reflecting synaptic connectivity alterations,
63   particularly within the basal ganglia-thalamocortical circuit [4].
64      Dopaminergic pathways are also known to play a role in psychiatric disorders via a variety
65   of distinctive mechanisms or dysfunctions in neurotransmission and neuromodulation, primarily
66   featuring the mesolimbic dopamine pathway. This pathway, extending from the ventral tegmental
67   area to limbic regions, takes a central role in the generation of positive psychotic symptoms integral
68   to Schizophrenia. Integrating these diverse neuromodulatory pathways into whole-brain network
69   models will require accounting for inter-regional connection between source and target regions in
70   conjunction with other region-specific parameters. This will be a key ingredient for the development
71   of whole brain network models that can advance our comprehension of the intricate interplay of
72   mechanisms contributing to the emergence of psychiatric and neurological disorders [45, 48].
73      Furthermore, both PD and psychiatric disorders are accompanied by local and brain-wide
74   changes in neural dynamics. In the context of PD, aberrant dynamics manifest in anomalous
75   bursting activity in the beta frequency range across the basal ganglia-thalamocortical motor net-
76   work [70], a phenomenon closely associated with clinical disability [69]. Similarly, in psychiatric
77   disorders, there is a disruption in the balance between excitation and inhibition within cortical re-
78   gions. This imbalance may result from synaptic pruning or alterations in neurotransmitter systems,
79   contributing to the pathophysiology of these conditions [24, 30].
80      Deep brain stimulation is a therapeutic intervention strategy aimed at rebalancing synchrony
81   dynamics of target neuronal populations in both conditions. Computational modeling studies play
82   a crucial role in providing insight on underlying mechanisms and informing the design of optimal
83   stimulation patterns in silico, thereby advancing the effectiveness of treatment and deepening our
84   understanding of these disorders [19, 40, 63, 71].
85      Understanding the intricate interplay between neuromodulators action and electrophysiological
86   features of brain is fundamental for elucidating the neural mechanisms underlying complex brain
87   functions and disorders. Mathematical models are indispensable for understanding the workings
88   of multi-scale non-linear interactions out of which emerges the different dynamical repertoires of
89   observed electrophysiological activity.
90      Additionally, the application of brain network models offers promise of informing the classifica-
91   tion and stratification of various forms of these multidimensional neurological conditions. Recent



                                                          3
92    studies underscore this potential of enhancing the classification of PD patients or individuals with
93    psychiatric disorders by supplementing empirical data with simulated data generated from patient-
94    specific brain network models [34].
95       Integrating personalized whole-brain network models and computational methodologies [23,67]
96    can contribute to our understanding of the dynamic aspects of these disorders, facilitating refined
97    treatment strategies and a deeper comprehension of their multifaceted etiologies, as it has been
98    done previously for other conditions, such as epilepsy [14]. For that aim, there is a need for
99    a mathematical model that incorporates how neuromodulators influence the electrophysiological
100   activity patterns in the human brain. In order to capture macroscopic brain wide effects and allow
101   for comprehensive parameter exploration, the said model ought to provide a level of description
102   [13,15] able to capture relevant features observed in human data [1,3] in a computationally efficient
103   framework; As such, it will be formulated on the mesoscopic scale as a neural mass model to be
104   implemented in the simulation environment of The Virtual Brain (TVB) [2, 57].
105      It is crucial to consider the impact of degeneracy and multi-realizability [18] (i.e. multiple
106   models or parametrizations can lead to similar observable behaviors), as well as experimental chal-
107   lenges associated with biophysical mechanisms that are difficult to access, which could impede the
108   interpretability of the model. The selected model should thus incorporate identifiable and measur-
109   able parameters that allow for linking biophysical mechanisms to observables of neural dynamics
110   such as known inhibitory and excitatory connections along with accompanying neuromodulation
111   affects on different synaptic receptors activity. It ultimately aims to possess a broad range of
112   dynamic behaviors that is rich enough to encompass complex spatiotemporal features of healthy
113   and aberrant neural activity.
114      To tackle this challenge, this paper introduces a generic framework for modeling neuromodu-
115   lator dynamics within a next generation neural mass model [10]. We specifically concentrate on
116   dopamine by presenting a tailored formulation of the corresponding model and an examination of
117   how dopamine dynamics influence neural activity. We present a bifurcation analysis that delin-
118   eates transitions between qualitatively different oscillatory regimes as the relevant parameters are
119   varied, and provide a quantitative characterization of the variations in the frequencies of emergent
120   multi-scale oscillations in population firing rate.

121   2     Methods
122   The starting point for this work is a neural-mass model [9], derived by applying a mean-field
123   approach [49] to a network of adaptive quadratic integrate-and-fire model (aQIF) neurons [28].
124   The key feature of this model, for our purposes, is that it enables the consideration of different
125   conductance-based synaptic inputs.
126       The aim is to include neuromodulation as a variable in our model. We follow the formalism
127   adopted by Kringelbach et al. [37], which introduces the effects of serotonin on the neural mass level
128   as an additive current, denoted by M, using the Michaelis-Menten equation [31, 46] to capture the
129   dynamics of neuromodulator re-uptake. In our study, we adapt this formalism to account for the
130   generic dynamics of neuromodulators by including the Michaelis-Menten equation for the evolution
131   of neuromodulator concentration, but we depart from [37] by formulating the modulatory effect,
132   M , as a multiplicative scaling of the synaptic conductances rather than as an additive current
133   term. This modification aims to enhance the precision and relevance of our model in capturing
134   the nonlinear influence of neuromodulators on neural excitability. Then we propose a specific
135   application for dopamine, with a possible further reduction for this specific case brought about by
136   the relatively slow timescale of dopamine concentration evolution. In the following subsection, we
137   detail the model construction.


138   2.1    Neural mass model

139   The set of equations for the neural mass model for the dynamics of a population of N neurons
140   is a modified version of that studied in the work of Chen and Campbell [9] and Sheheitli and
141   Jirsa [60]. The mean-field derivation invokes the Lorentzian ansatz [49]. Assuming a neuronal
142   heterogeneity that takes the form of additive currents, ηi , the distribution of the membrane voltages
143   converges, in the thermodynamic limit N → ∞, to a Lorentzian-shaped function, regardless of the
144   initial conditions. The resulting mean-field variables correspond to the population mean firing
145   rate r, mean membrane potential V and mean adaptation variable u. We consider conductance-
146   based synapses of two types, excitatory AMPA receptors of maximal conductance ga and reversal
147   potential Ea , and inhibitory GABA receptors of maximal conductance gg and reversal potential
148   Eg . This adds two other variables to the model, Sa and Sg , for the evolution of the activation of
149   AMPA and GABA synapses. The resulting mean-field equations become:

158   2.2    Generic equations for neuromodulation

159   At least two aspects are important in neuromodulation dynamics. First, the availability of the
160   neuromodulator is considered, i.e. the evolution of the local concentration. Secondly, its effect on
161   neural activities through specific receptors. One equation for each type of chemical neuromodu-
162   lation (i.e. capturing the evolution of the concentration) and one equation for each receptor type
163   (i.e. capturing the activation of each of these receptors) are necessary. The functional form for
164   the concentration is made up of two terms, where the first corresponds to the mechanisms leading
165   to an increase of the local concentration which often depend on the activity of a specific brain
166   region that is afferent to the considered node. The second term corresponds to the decrease in
167   concentration due to re-uptake mechanisms that can be captured by Michaelis-Menten formalism.
168   In the following subsection, we describe the construction of the equations specific for dopamine.
169   The variable denoted M , captures the proportion of target receptors activated by the neuromod-
170   ulator. The typical functional form is a sigmoid function [33], the parametrization of which must
171   be specific for each receptor type. In this case, given the slope of the sigmoid function, receptors
172   can either increase (positive slope) or decrease (negative slope) the activity of the correspondent


                                                        6
173   synapse. Then the M variable enters the mean membrane potential equation according to the
174   considered receptor’s specific biophysical properties. It can either modulate a non-synaptic con-
175   ductance through an additional term of the form M gi (Ei − V ) with gi and Ei associated with a
176   charged molecule; or directly modulate the synaptic conductances, as it is the case for dopamine.
177   In the following subsection, we take the example of D1 receptors modulating the conductance of
178   AMPA synapses.


179   2.2.1   Dopamine reuptake

180   The reuptake of dopamine is a process in which dopamine is returned to the presynaptic neuron
181   from the synaptic cleft. The primary molecular mechanism responsible for dopamine reuptake in-
182   volves a protein known as the dopamine transporter (DAT). The reuptake process can be described
183   by the following simplified equation:

                                                       Reuptake
                                         [Dp ]e + DAT −−−−−−→ [Dp ]i

184      In this equation: [Dp ]e represents dopamine molecules in the extracellular space, DAT repre-
185   sents the dopamine transporter protein on the neuron membrane, and [Dp ]i represents dopamine
186   molecules that have been taken back into the neuron.
187      The dopamine transporter (DAT) actively transports dopamine from the extracellular space
188   back into the presynaptic neuron, terminating the signal transmission at the synapse. This reuptake
189   process is crucial for regulating the dopamine concentration in the synaptic cleft and maintaining
190   proper neurotransmission. Additionally, it serves as a target for various drugs, including certain
191   antidepressants and psychostimulants, which can modulate dopamine reuptake.


192   2.2.2   Michaelis-Menten Formalism for Dopamine Reuptake

193   The dynamics of dopamine reuptake can be described using Michaelis-Menten (M-M) formalism,
194   which involves applying the M-M equation to represent the process of dopamine transport via the
195   dopamine transporter (DAT).
196      The general form of the M-M equation is:

                                                       Vmax · [S]
                                              VM M =
                                                       Km + [S]

197      and is a mathematical description of the relationship between substrate concentration [S] and
198   reaction rate V , characterized by the maximum reaction rate (Vmax ) and the Michaelis constant


                                                       7
199   (Km ). We can adapt this equation to describe the dynamics of dopamine reuptake:

                                                       Vmax · [Dp ]e
                                              VDpr =
                                                       Km + [Dp ]e

200   where: VDpr is the reuptake rate and represents the velocity dopamine returns to the neuron.
201   Vmax is the maximum reuptake rate, which corresponds to the rate when the dopamine trans-
202   porter (DAT) is fully saturated with dopamine. Km is the Michaelis constant, representing the
203   extracellular dopamine concentration at which the reuptake rate is half of Vmax . [Dp ]e is the
204   concentration of dopamine in the extracellular space.
205      This formulation represents a deliberate simplification, as the actual dynamics of dopamine
206   reuptake are shaped by a multitude of cellular and molecular factors. Beyond simple enzymatic
207   kinetics, dopamine clearance depends on the density and conformational state of dopamine trans-
208   porters (DATs), which are themselves modulated by phosphorylation, membrane potential, in-
209   tracellular signaling cascades, and protein–protein interactions [26, 62]. Moreover, extracellular
210   conditions and competing substrates can dynamically alter transporter efficiency, making reup-
211   take a highly regulated and context-dependent process rather than a static reaction. While the
212   Michaelis–Menten formalism offers a tractable approximation that captures the saturating nature
213   of transporter-mediated uptake [11], it does not aim to reproduce all the mechanistic intricacies
214   of dopamine signaling at the synaptic level. Given that our objective is to model the collective,
215   population-level regulation of neuronal excitability, this coarse-grained representation provides an
216   appropriate balance between biological plausibility and analytical tractability, preserving the essen-
217   tial temporal and functional properties of dopaminergic modulation without introducing excessive
218   microscopic detail.

268      The time constant in Eq.14 reflects that dopamine’s modulatory effects are not instantaneous
269   but mediated by a cascade of biochemical processes. After release, dopamine must diffuse and
270   bind to D1 receptors, which activate G-proteins and the cAMP/PKA signaling pathway, leading
271   to phosphorylation and insertion of AMPA receptors that modify synaptic efficacy. These steps
272   introduce delays on the order of hundreds of milliseconds to seconds [44, 61, 62]. Accordingly, we
273   represent receptor activation as a first-order relaxation process with τm = 500ms, which aggregates
274   these mechanisms into a single effective timescale. This formulation can be adapted for other
275   neuromodulators, with τm adjusted according to their specific receptor kinetics and downstream
276   signaling cascades. In the specific case of dopamine, the time scale of modulation and that of
277   concentration evolution are similar and M is strongly correlated with the [Dp ] variable. It is
278   important to note that the simplification of Eq.(14), where the receptor activation dynamics are
279   treated as instantaneous, is a modeling approximation specific to dopamine in the present context.
280   This assumption cannot be generally extended to other neuromodulators, as many of them act
281   involving multistep signaling cascades with slower kinetics, including ligand binding/unbinding, G-
282   protein activation, second-messenger production, and phosphorylation events [41]. These processes


                                                       11
283   can integrate neuromodulatory signals over hundreds of milliseconds to seconds, or even minutes,
284   depending on receptor subtype and cellular context. And thus the dynamics of the receptor is
285   not necessarily in the same time scale of the concentration evolution. For example, serotonin
286   and muscarinic acetylcholine receptors display slower or more sustained effects than dopaminergic
287   D1 signaling, making the instantaneous approximation less appropriate. Consequently, the full
288   kinetic formulation of Eq.(14) should be retained when modeling neuromodulators whose receptor
289   dynamics are not on the timescale of its corresponding neuromodulator concentration dynamics.

304   3     Results
305   We studied the emerging dynamics of this model. We considered an excitatory population, with
306   recurrent connections receiving excitatory and dopaminergic inputs. Given the number of dimen-
307   sions of the system which obstructs an analytical study of its bifurcation structure, we resort to


                                                         12
308   numerical analysis to unfold the different dynamical regimes that can emerge as the different pa-
309   rameters are varied. Specifically, similarly to previous works [29, 55], we leverage the time scale
310   separation that exists in the system, between the fast sub-system associated with the r, V and Sa ,
311   on one hand, and the slow variable u and ultra-slow [Dp ], on the other. We start with a bifurcation
312   analysis of the fast sub-system constituted by the r, V, Sa variables, considering the slow variables
313   u and [Dp] as parameters of this subsystem. Then, through numerical simulations, we characterize

316   3.1    Fast-subsystem r, V, Sa

317   In this first subsection, we focused on the sub-system r, V, Sa , the three fast variables of the system,
318   revealing diverse qualitative behaviors, notably the first emergence of a limit cycle, and a region
319   of bistability. The variables r and V were selected as primary observables constituting the fast
320   sub-system. Synaptic activation Sa exhibits nearly the same rapid dynamics, depending on r.
321   Importantly, this variable is influenced by dopamine levels in the r and V equations. Therefore,
322   r, V and Sa are the 3 fast variables, while u and [Dp ] being respectively slow and ultra-slow
323   variables, are considered fixed here (i.e. treated as parameters). Thus, to investigate the dynamics
324   of the fast sub-system we performed bifurcation analysis with respect to parameters u and [Dp ]. In
325   previous work [9] u has been identified as responsible for bursting behaviors. Here, bursting refers
326   to population-level activity in spiking neurons, characterized by periods of synchronized firing
327   separated by phases of reduced, asynchronous activity. We chose [Dp ] as the second bifurcation
328   parameter as we would like to see how the external dopamine concentration affects the behavior of
329   our neural population. The result of the analysis is shown in 2. In the chosen range of parameters,
330   we observe the emergence of a region, in white in Fig. 2 I, with a single stable fixed-point (Fig. 2
331   II (a)). In the light blue region, delimited by a Hopf curve, the fixed point loses stability in favor
332   of an attracting limit-cycle, leading to the oscillations shown in Fig. 2 II (b). For higher values of
333   [Dp ] and/or u, after crossing again the Hopf curve, the system returns to a fixed point, associated
334   with saturation, at a low firing rate r and high value of mean membrane potential V , as shown
335   in Fig. 2 II (c). Two curves of fold (saddle-node) bifurcations delimit a region, shaded in orange,
336   within which the fast sub-system presents a bistable behavior, characterized by the co-existence of
337   an up and a down state. An example of the dynamics of the system for parameters value inside this
338   region is represented in 2d, which shows the co-existence of a state of higher activity, characterized
339   also by higher mean membrane potential, with another state of low firing rate, with the value of V


                                                         13
340   close to the resting membrane potential. Finally, the overlap between the orange and blue regions
341   is characterized by the bistability between a fixed point and a limit cycle, as observed in Fig. 2 II
342   (e).

442   from a fixed point (low steady state) to bursting behavior, then back to a fixed point, followed
443   by oscillations, and finally reaches another fixed point (high steady state). We then examined a
444   sinusoidal cdopa to study a simple case of dopaminergic fluctuations. We first focused on the role

445   of amplitude in the dopaminergic input in Fig. 8 (b) and then on its frequency aspects in Fig. 8
446   (c). Our results show that the amplitude determines access to the different dynamical patterns
447   illustrated in panel (a). For very low amplitudes (cdopa <∼ 0.05), the neural activity is entrained
448   by cdopa . For higher amplitudes, the system progressively explores a richer dynamical repertoire,
449   transitioning from simple bursting and then transitions to alternating bursting and steady states.
450   As the amplitude increases further, these alternating patterns become accompanied by sustained
451   oscillations until finally reaching the upper fixed-point (steady state), as displayed in Fig. 8 (b).

453   varying the frequency, as shown in Fig. 8 (c), we explored how the temporal characteristics of
454   dopaminergic input influence the stability and switching speed between dynamical regimes. We
455   selected a peak amplitude of cdopa ∼ 0.2, which places the system within the alternating regime
456   characterized by transitions between the low steady state, bursting, and intermediate steady state.
457   At low frequencies, the system has sufficient time to fully express each dynamical pattern before the
458   next phase of cdopa modulation, leading to clear transitions between distinct states. As frequencies

466   4     Discussion
467   We proposed a modeling framework to capture the effects of neuromodulator dynamics on the
468   neuronal electrophysiological activity at a neural mass level. We presented a reduced version,
469   specific for dopamine dynamics, as a proof of concept, and provided a thorough bifurcation analysis
470   of the qualitatively different possible dynamical regimes that emerge as steady state dopamine
471   concentration and input excitation levels are varied.
472       Such an approach enables us to capture a rich and complex dynamical repertoire with a reduced
473   number of variables that can still be interpreted or associated with meaningful biophysical units.
474   Indeed, while the proposed model is phenomenological in nature by its high level of description,
475   it can nevertheless serve to explicitly represent and capture the evolution of physical variables
476   that can be directly related to observables, such as firing rates, mean membrane potentials, and
477   synaptic currents. Thus, this is a biophysically inspired model [23], in which the fast dynamics is
478   obtained through mean field theory and complemented with slow dynamics in a heuristic efficient
479   formulation at the mesoscopic scale. The end result is a minimal mechanistic neural mass model
480   that lends itself to parameter fitting for capturing complex electrophisiological oscillatory dynamics
481   that may arise in different types of neuronal populations across brain regions.
482       While fitting the proposed model to specific experimental data is beyond the scope of the
483   current work, it is worth noting that the frequencies of inter and intra-burst oscillations, emerging
484   for the parameter values studied here, are in the range of what has been observed in basal ganglia
485   neuronal activity recorded from implanted deep brain stimulation electrodes in PD patients [43].
486   More specifically, the administration of dopamine replacement medication (L-DOPA) was reported
487   to induce a shift in the peaks of the power spectrum of the subthalamic nucleus activity, from
488   15HZ and 250Hz to 25Hz and 350Hz (see Fig. 2 in [43]). This is inline with what is suggested
489   by the results in Fig. 5e and 5c, that is, there exists a range of parameter combinations for
490   which the proposed model would exhibit inter and intra-burst frequencies that would be consistent
491   with those experimentally reported for the concurrent shift in spectral peaks. More generally,
492   our model generates oscillations across multiple frequency bands whose emergence and frequency
493   content depend on dopamine levels. Specifically, the model exhibits oscillation frequency shifts that
494   span the 5-40Hz range in response to changes in dopamine tone. This is also consistent with the
495   experimental findings in [27] that acute modulation of dopamine tone leads to frequency shifts in
496   beta oscillations (8-35Hz) in both cortex and globus pallidus externus (GPe). Their work supports
497   the view that progressive slowing of beta frequency may serve as a more sensitive biomarker of
498   Parkinson’s disease progression than beta power.


                                                       27
499      The model also generates high-frequency oscillations (HFOs) in the 100-500Hz range, under spe-
500   cific dopaminergic and excitatory input states. This is in line with experimental reports from [20],
501   who observed that dopaminergic medication in Parkinsonian patients induces oscillatory activity
502   around 300Hz in the STN, suggesting a new dynamic equilibrium driven by dopaminergic input.
503   Our model replicates this high-frequency regime under increased dopamine conditions, consistent
504   with this shift, and shows that HFOs can emerge through intrinsic dynamical transitions in the
505   system. On the other hand, [32] found that HFOs emerge in the internal globus pallidus (GPi)
506   of MPTP-treated monkeys, and are attenuated with dopaminergic medication in human patients.
507   This highlights the need to account for network level interactions as our model suggests that the
508   emergence or attenuation of HFOs in a given brain region can arise from the interplay of dopamin-
509   ergic modulation and direct inputs from other network nodes. The minimal mean field model that
510   we present here is designed to serve as an efficient building block for large scale network mod-
511   els that can sustain such multiscale rhythms and provide insight into their alterations in disease
512   states brought about by chemical neuromodulatory imbalances, such as in the case of dopaminergic
513   depletion in PD.
514      A key feature of our model is the generation of multi-scale oscillations, with activity occurring
515   simultaneously in the beta and HFO bands. The multiscale oscillation in the population firing rate
516   represents synchronized neuronal spiking with intrinsic slow and fast timescales reflected in the
517   inter- and intra-burst periods, respectively. This raises the possibility of cross-frequency interac-
518   tions between the two timescales that can manifest in the corresponding local field potentials; more
519   specifically, it can lead to phase-amplitude coupling in which the amplitude of the fast oscillation
520   (corresponding to the intra-burst oscillation) is coupled to the phase of the slow (inter-burst) cy-
521   cle. Importantly, [42] demonstrated that in the dopamine-depleted motor state, the amplitude of
522   HFOs is coupled to the phase of abnormal beta activity in the basal ganglia. This pathological
523   phase-amplitude coupling (PAC) was reduced with dopaminergic medication, suggesting that such
524   coupling may reflect abnormal dynamical entrainment contributing to motor impairment. Our
525   model captures the emergence of mean firing rate states exhibiting nested oscillatory components,
526   from which both beta and HFO rhythms emerge endogenously within the same population-level
527   system, thus creating natural conditions for phase-amplitude coupling. Unlike models where PAC
528   is imposed through an external oscillatory drive, our framework shows that PAC can emerge from
529   intrinsic nonlinear interactions, modulated by dopamine-dependent parameters. Our model paves
530   the way for further mechanistic inquiry of how pathological PAC, such as beta-HFO coupling ob-
531   served in Parkinson’s disease, may originate from altered dopamine-modulated dynamics at the



                                                       28
532   population level. In short, the proposed model captures biologically plausible features of dopamine-
533   regulated brain dynamics, including: frequency shifts in the beta band, emergence and suppression
534   of high-frequency oscillations (100–500Hz) and nonlinear cross-frequency coupling (PAC) between
535   beta and HFO bands. Together, these phenomena align with key experimental findings in the
536   literature and support the model’s relevance for exploring dopaminergic modulation of multiscale
537   oscillatory brain activity in health and disease.
538      One novelty of the framework presented here, in comparison to other neural mass modeling
539   approaches, is the expression of the action of dopamine as a scaling of the synaptic conductance
540   at the mesoscopic neural mass model level; this formulation can in fact be seen as a natural
541   extension of the seminal works of Humphries [25] and Durstewitz [16, 17] in which dopaminergic
542   action was modeled as such but on the microscopic level of individual spiking neurons. This
543   latter approach was also employed in [39] to capture the effects of dopamine depletion in a multi-
544   population spiking neural network model of the basal ganglia. As such, our proposed form for
545   dopamine action on the neural mass model builds on previous work of validated biophysical models
546   of spiking neurons to provide a computationally efficient framework that extends the modeling of
547   dopaminergic neuromodulatory action, from the level of populations of spiking neurons, to that of
548   the whole brain network level.
549      This modeling framework also enables the introduction of effects on other receptors, such as
550   NMDA type receptors [60]. The framework is modular and can be adapted to fit the scope of
551   different applications; that is, it can serve as a foundational building block to be used in person-
552   alized brain models, such as in the context of virtual brain twins [67]. Virtual brain twins is a
553   novel concept, in which only features that are of interest to a neurological condition are selected
554   to be captured by the model, so as to maximize efficiency and relevance to corresponding clini-
555   cal data [67]. This methodology aims to then personalize the model to the individual patient’s
556   brain by constraining the model to anatomical and functional patient-specific data using advanced
557   parameter fitting algorithms. The resulting models can then serve as in-silico simulation engines
558   for the guidance of more optimal diagnosis and treatment of brain disorders. By tailoring the
559   modeling to individual subjects, researchers can better understand the intricacies of brain function
560   and mechanisms of its pathology. In Wang et al. [67], the standard model was presented in a
561   generic format, including local dynamics with both global and local connectivity. With this work,
562   we present a first detailed neural mass model that allows the incorporation of neuromodulator dy-
563   namics on a mesoscopic level in large-scale whole-brain dynamics. Ultimately, the proposed model
564   paves the way for the development of virtual brain twins [66], particularly in the context of PD [3]



                                                          29
565   and psychiatric disorders, for which the disruption in chemical neuromodulator action is known to
566   play a significant role in the underlying psychophysiology.
