                 1 Introduction
                 The chronic syndrome of the brain is known by the name epilepsy. It affects about 79
                 million people in the world’s population. Epilepsy is an irregular electrical activity in the
                 brain [1]. Nowadays, the detection of epilepsy is a challenging job not only for research-
                 ers and neurologists but also for expert anatomists who rely on EEG recordings and
                 visual examination of EEG data. The major function of an EEG signal is to identify a
                 patient who is having a seizure [2]. A neuronal synchronous discharge with a sharply
                 elevated amplitude that is transient and episodic is the hallmark of an epileptic seizure,
                 which is abnormal in EEG recordings. This abnormal synchrony can affect the entire
                 brain (generalised seizures), which are seen in all EEG signal channels, or just a portion
                 of the brain (partial seizures), which are only visible in a few channels [3]. Electrodes
                 applied to the scalp are used to record EEG signals from the brain. EEG signals consist
                 of five subbands. These subband frequency ranges can effectively depict the dynamics of
                 the brain. The subband provides more meaningful information related to the neurologi-
                 cal behaviour and related activities in the actual EEG signal [4]. The biomedical systems
                 need more high-capacity data processing capabilities and more accuracy. The design of
                 such real-time systems for seizure detection is very challenging, as the system design is
                 more constrained for low power, high accuracy, and robust noise capability, as the dis-
                 ease can occur in individuals of any age, creating their identification and analysis a vig-
                 orous aspect of neurological healthcare [5]. Moreover, the physicians propose extended
                 EEG monitoring for patients, compelling them to stay in hospitals for a prolonged time
                 for proper study and capturing seizure activity [6]. The seizure detection is a time-con-
                 suming and exhausting process, with no conviction about when a seizure will occur.
                 Moreover, the manual processing of EEG recordings is tedious and susceptible to human
                 error, especially in the presence of pervasive signal artifacts. Therefore, it is required to
                 automate seizure detection and prediction systems capable of providing diagnostic accu-
                 racy, efficiency, and overall clinical reliability.
                    The neurological disorder recognised as epilepsy, characterised by epileptic seizures,
                 has been recognised since 400 BC. According to the WHO’s 2019 report, an estimated
                 79 million people globally are affected by this disease [7]. Epilepsy is primarily initiated
                 by abnormal, synchronous neuronal firing in the brain. This abnormal electrical activ-
                 ity models to the classic symptoms of seizures, including loss of consciousness and sud-
                 den, involuntary body movements. The seizures are broadly classified into two main
                 types: generalised seizures and partial (or focal) seizures. The identification can be done
                 based on the monitoring of EEG signals and brain activity, generally in the 0.5–30 Hz
                 frequency range [8]. The processing of the raw EEG signal needs filtering for hardware
                 design, and an FIR filter can be implemented to minimise computational delay during
                 signal preprocessing. The EEG signals largely comprise five main types that are clinically
                 important in epilepsy detection [9]. Figure 1 depicts the different types of EEG wave-
                 forms, namely alpha, beta, gamma, theta, and delta.
                    In EEG-based epilepsy studies, the frequency spectrum plays a crucial role in identi-
                 fying abnormal brain activities [10]. Each frequency band reflects a specific functional
                 state of the brain, and variations in these signals help in detecting seizure onset zones.
                 During preprocessing, EEG signals are decomposed into standard bands: delta (0–4 Hz),
                 theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz), beta (12–40 Hz), and gamma (> 40 Hz) using digital
                 filters or time frequency transforms such as DWT, or STFT [11]. This separation allows

                        though low in amplitude, are vital in distinguishing normal high-frequency activity
                        from pathological synchronization during seizures.

                 After band-specific filtering, statistical and nonlinear features such as power spectral
                 density, Hjorth parameters, entropy measures, and fractal dimensions are computed.
                 These parameters are then fed into classification models such as SVM, CNN, or hybrid
                 deep networks to distinguish between normal, inter-ictal, and ictal EEG segments.
                    In general, the process of EEG signal processing and analysis is carried out in four
                 steps, as depicted in Fig. 2: first, the raw signals are pre-processed using filtering or other
                 techniques; second, the most significant information is extracted from them in the form
                 of features; third, feature selection techniques are applied for more optimal results; and
                 fourth, during the result analysis stage, disease diagnosis or recognition of various func-
                 tional states of the brain is performed. Statistical tests and classification algorithms are
                 the two most commonly used result analysis techniques. The small fluctuations in EEG
                 readings serve as an excellent means to record the continuously changing functional
                 states of the brain. The EEG of a normal person differs from that of an abnormal one;
                 therefore, it is essential to detect these variations using various signal processing and
                 analysis techniques. EEG signal processing involves several interference signals that
                 must be removed before performing further steps, such as feature extraction and clas-
                 sification, to ensure more accurate results.
                    The raw EEG data commonly used for analysis are publicly available online, typically
                 obtained from the CHB-MIT database (Physionet.org). A low-pass FIR filter is generally
                 applied to EEG signals. Digital filters are mostly preferred in biomedical applications due
                 to their high stability, absence of feedback, and linear phase response [15]. The bioelec-
                 tric signal known as EEG provides valuable information about brain activity. However,
                 the interference signals, often referred to as artifacts, arise from external environmen-
                 tal noise and introduce significant challenges in EEG signal analysis [2, 16]. During pre-
                 processing and filtering, an FIR filter is typically used. Although an IIR filter can also
                 be employed, the FIR filter is preferred because of its superior frequency stability and
                 linear phase characteristics [17]. The main drawback of time-domain approaches, how-
                 ever, is their high sensitivity to selected features and the requirement for greater storage

                   When comparing depressed patients to normal individuals, slower brain waves are
                 indicative of a deep sleep stage [18] or reduced power in the alpha and theta bands [19]
                 across all brain regions (central, occipital, frontal, parietal, and temporal). An EEG sig-
                 nal consists of several waves with distinct properties, captured using electrodes placed
                 on the scalp. These electrodes are arranged in arrays across the scalp. The large volume
                 of data obtained from even a single EEG electrode pair poses significant challenges
                 [20]. Various types of electrodes are used during EEG recording to acquire high-qual-
                 ity data [21]. EEG analysis serves as a diagnostic tool for numerous brain-related dis-
                 orders, including epilepsy, dementia, and mental health conditions such as stress [22].
                 The most commonly used electrode arrangement is the international 10–20 system [23].
                 EEG is also applied clinically for diagnosing coma, brain death, encephalopathies, and
                 sleep disorders. Moreover, EEG has broader applications, such as emotion recognition
                 [24]. For small datasets, various existing EEG feature extraction and classification algo-
                 rithms [25–31] can almost perfectly differentiate between seizure and non-seizure EEG
                 signals. However, these methods often fail to achieve sufficient classification accuracy
                 during both the ictal phase (the period when a seizure occurs) and the interictal phase
                 (the interval between seizures) due to the non-abrupt and consistent nature of EEG sig-
                 nals across different brain regions [32]. Furthermore, achieving an acceptable level of
                 accuracy with a specific approach becomes more difficult without adequate domain
                 knowledge of EEG data, as variability arises from different seizure types (i.e., partial or
                 generalized), individual differences, hospital environments, brain regions, and artifacts
                 [33, 34]. Each time a seizure occurs, patients with epilepsy are at risk of severe injury
                 or even death due to uncontrollable movements, loss of consciousness, and convulsions
                 [35]. Epileptic seizures are generally classified into two categories: partial and gener-
                 alized. A generalized epileptic seizure affects the entire brain, while a partial epileptic
                 seizure affects only a localized area of the brain, typically resulting from excessive and
                 synchronized electrical discharges [36].

                 2 Literature review
                 2.1 Review of the filters
                 The EEG signal is generally of small amplitude in the order of microvolts (µV). The sig-
                 nal is having several interferences or artifacts, which can distort its actual character-
                 istics. The main source of the noise in the signal is the electrical power line of 50 Hz,
                 which interferes with the raw EEG signal and affects the EEG signals. This noise causes
                 difficulties in the extraction of signal and clinical analysis with meaningful parameter
                 information. Therefore, it becomes necessary to reject such variabilities in EEG signals
                 to make certain decisions and process accurate disease diagnosis. Different windowing
                 techniques are used for EEG signals to reduce ripples and leakages, and control the pass-
                 band and stopband signals to determine the features. The performance has been ana-
                 lyzed using FIR and IIR filters for the suppression of interference in EEG signals [37].
                 The results have shown that the Kaiser window method provided effective suppres-
                 sion of interference in EEG signals. The IIR filter has effectively been employed for the
                 rapid recognition of EEG signals, offering equalization and signal compression capabili-
                 ties [38]. FIR digital filters are generally configured in such systems due to their lower
                 cost, reduced noise, and low power consumption [39]. Using the FIR window method,
                 different EEG signal bands were extracted, demonstrating the superiority of specific
                 windowing techniques [40]. Various FIR windowing approaches have been studied for
                 interference removal, with results showing that the Blackman window performs best
                 [41]. They have shown that the Blackman window provided optimal outcomes in EEG
                 data filtering following the transition bandwidth, stop-band attenuation, and cutoff fre-
                 quency. The Blackman window provided the second-highest stop-band attenuation,
                 the broadest transition bandwidth, and the highest cutoff frequency among all window
                 methods. An adaptive FIR filter was used to minimize eye blink artifact (EBA) inter-
                 ference [42] for different types of artifacts that affect EEG signals. The IIR filters are
                 also designed to remove distortions, and comparative analysis between IIR and FIR fil-
                 ters assisted researchers in selecting the most suitable filter for their applications [43].
                 Implementation of FIR filter architectures on an FPGA has achieved reduced hardware
                 resource usage and low power consumption [44]. FIR filters are widely used for noise
                 removal from EEG signals, and related designs have been simulated using the Xilinx
                 FPGA ISE environment [45]. Furthermore, FIR band-pass filters are used to eliminate
                 unwanted noise components from EEG signals [46]. A bank of IIR filters has also been
                 analyzed for different EEG frequency bands [47]. However, in some cases, residual noise
                 persists before and after FIR filtering, where impulse response filters have demonstrated
                 better performance [48]. Overall, FIR filters are effective for removing interference from
                 EEG signals and contribute to reduced computational delay [49]. Table 1 lists the find-
                 ings from the review of other types of filters used in EEG signal processing.
                   IIR and FIR filters with different windowing techniques are used, and the results prove
                 that the finite impulse response (FIR) filter using the Kaiser window [50] performs the
                 best. The Frequency Response Masking (FRM) technique is applied in FIR filter design
                 since the number of multipliers in FIR filters is typically higher than in IIR filters. By
                 using the FRM technique, the number of multipliers can be reduced, thereby achieving
                 a low-power FIR design [51]. An FIR filter with a linear phase response suitable for EEG
                 or ECG signal processing is discussed in [52]. An adaptive filter architecture employ-

                 2.2 Feature extraction techniques
                 The features of interest are chosen after noise is removed from the EEG data, and those
                 features are subsequently extracted. The feature Extraction can be done either in the
                 time domain, frequency domain, or time-frequency domain. In the time domain basis,
                 the feature can be extracted either through linear prediction methods, component anal-
                 ysis methods, or statistical analysis methods like mean, mode, median, kurtosis, vari-
                 ance, and standard deviation methods. The FFT and DFT are two feature extraction
                 methods available in frequency domain techniques. Additionally, the wavelet transform
                 can be used to extract features by combining both methods. Figure 4 shows an overview
                 of different feature extraction techniques.

                 2.3 Feature extractions review
                 It is difficult to detect seizures in an EEG signal because the human brain’s EEG output
                 is unpredictable. For real-time applications, an epileptic seizure detection system must
                 be implemented in hardware. This paper develops an accurate method for identifying
                 epileptic seizures and implements the detection method on hardware. An FIR band-
                 pass filter is used to separate frequency bands. The modeled EEG signal is then used to
                 extract features, which are subsequently classified using an ELM classifier [61]. Pattern
                 recognition is employed for identifying epileptic seizures in EEG signals. In this pattern

                 recognition work, NB and SVM classifiers are used to examine the impact of filtering
                 with time-domain characteristics in epileptic signal detection [62]. Feature extraction

                 feature ranking, which helps classify the features according to their clinical relevance

                 feature extraction based on Hjorth’s mobility is employed [66]. The feature extraction
                 methodology includes three phases: (1) EEG signal acquisition, (2) empirical mode
                 decomposition to divide signals into sub-bands, and (3) classification using feature vec-
                 tors with random forest, artificial neural network, and support vector machine classi-
                 fiers [67]. DWT is also used to extract features derived from autistic children’s sensory
                 responses. In this study, statistical properties of the DWT coefficients are estimated, and
                 an MLP neural network is applied for signal identification [68]. In another EEG signal
                 processing approach, noise is first removed, followed by segmentation using PCM. Fea-
                 ture extraction is then performed using wavelets, and an SVM classifier is applied for
                 the identification of specific features [69]. Seizure identification from EEG signals can
                 also be achieved using HMS analysis [70]. WT is used to extract features, followed by a
                 learning-based algorithm classifier for seizure classification [71].
                    To enhance the effectiveness of automated sleep stage categorization using machine
                 learning methods, features are extracted from EEG signals [72]. RTQWT is used for
                 feature extraction, suitable for dynamic EEG signals [73]. DTCWT features can be
                 extracted using FFT, enabling the derivation of highly discriminative traits for epilepsy
                 detection [74]. For seizure detection, STFT is used to extract frequency bands and rel-
                 evant features, followed by classification using CNN and Bi-LSTM models [75]. Due to
                 various artifacts and noise present in the original signal, an FIR Butterworth filter is used
                 before extracting the key information with the FFT tool [76]. Quadratic feature vectors
                 are extracted using an optimized auto-regressive approach, and a random forest classi-
                 fier is used for data identification [77]. Noise removal from EEG signals is achieved using
                 a Surface Laplacian and a 4th-order Butterworth filter [78]. FFT and PCA methods are
                 used for feature extraction from EEG signals [79]. Twenty frequency feature components
                 are extracted using STFT [80], and a CNN-based framework is employed for epilepsy
                 detection [81]. Table 2 summarizes the different feature extraction techniques reviewed

                 3.1 Classification review
                 The EEG classification was done for epileptic seizure detection, based on the Hjorth
                 Descriptor, and KNN using an FPGA. The system was deployed on a Zynq-7000 FPGA,
                 and its classification accuracy of 90.74% [90]. A feed-forward MLP-ANN model was
                 used. The model has proven significantly superior alternative to conventional super-
                 vised machine learning techniques. However, handling excessively long data processing
                 times during advanced MLP-ANN computations, along with multiple EEG signal char-
                 acteristics, poses challenges in implementing the 5–12−3 MLP-ANN architecture [91].
                 A unique supervised MKRVFLN classifier has been proposed. The classifier effectively
                 trained the RDCNN method for feature extraction in epileptic EEG analysis [79]. In EEG
                 signal analysis, DWT was used for feature extraction from FIR-filtered EEG inputs. The
                 proposed classifier then utilizes these extracted features for training, improving classi-
                 fication accuracy through the BPSO-based optimization of selected features [92]. The
                 behavior of the non-stationary EEG signals was verified on an FPGA by integrating the
                 DWT, PCA, and SVM for a seizure detection method for the analysis of and nonlinear-
                 ity of EEG signals. The PCA was used to reduce the feature vectors’ dimensionality by
                 analyzing variance from each wavelet, applied for faster classification, while the SVM
                 performed the classification [93]. An FPGA hardware kit is also utilized to implement
                 an autonomous seizure detection algorithm using an LSVM. The simulation and syn-
                 thesized test cases with experimental outcome resulted in an accuracy of 86.00% and
Pant and Kumar Discover Electronics   (2026) 3:20                                                             Page 12 of 19




                 an average sensitivity of 97.00% [94]. An epileptic seizure estimation method for EEG
                 signal processing was developed using MLPNN and SVM classifiers. The study also
                 explored whether WNNs can accurately predict seizure occurrences. Since EEG signals
                 are nonlinear and non-stationary, an NNA classifier was used for estimating epileptic
                 seizure activity from background EEG [95]. A combined WT-SVM classification method
                 effectively identifies epileptic seizures in EEG signals. EEG subbands were derived using
                 DWT, decomposing the signal into seven levels. Among these, alpha waves (100 µV
                 amplitude) are most commonly used for seizure detection. Statistical features from the
                 alpha band were extracted, followed by SVM-based classification [96]. An RFF classi-
                 fier was employed for classification, while ICFS was used for feature selection in EEG
                 signal analysis [97]. EEG feature extraction using DWT measured five wavelets and
                 four statistical parameters, optimized using a PSO-based learning algorithm. Network
                 performance was evaluated in terms of specificity, sensitivity, and overall classification
                 accuracy [64]. An ANN classifier combined with SFV-based feature extraction achieved
                 an excellent identification accuracy of 99.18% [98]. A real-time seizure prediction sys-
                 tem was implemented on an FPGA using a one-class SVM, designed to function as a
                 neurostimulator to forecast and control epileptic episodes. The algorithm accurately
                 predicted impending seizures with high sensitivity and an acceptable false-positive rate
                 when tested on patient EEG data [99]. The comprehensive reviews of machine learning-
                 based classification and multi-domain feature extraction methods for automated seizure
                 detection systems have been conducted to assess classifier performance [100]. Various
                 algorithms, including ELM, BPNN, and SVM classifiers, have been compared for their
                 classification accuracy [101]. Table 3 summarizes the different classification techniques
                 and their respective hardware platforms.
                    The study showed that smoothing the classifiers’ outputs can significantly improve
                 classification accuracy [102]. A new method for EEG classification used a kernel ridge
                 classifier, with PCA applied first for feature extraction using a time-domain algorithm
                 [103]. EEG signals were processed using STFT and statistical feature extraction meth-
                 ods, followed by classification with LightGBM and CatBoost to achieve high accuracy
                 [104]. Variable mode decomposition with statistical feature extraction was applied, and
                 the features were fed into an NLTWSVM for epileptic signal classification [105]. DWT
                 was used to decompose EEG signals, and the approximate and detailed coefficients were
                 computed. The difference between these coefficients was used in an ANN to classify epi-
                 leptic and normal signals [106]. The RQA technique achieved an accuracy of 98.67% for
                 EEG signal detection [107]. A method based on EMD was used for epileptic seizure rec-
                 ognition [108]. EMD produced IMFs, and several statistical measures such as variance,
                 kurtosis, and skewness, along with chaotic features like Lyapunov exponent and correla-
                 tion dimension, were extracted and classified using ANN, yielding 100% accuracy [109].
                 Another approach extracted statistical features in the time domain, using minimum
                 and maximum EEG values for seizure detection with a linear classifier [110]. In another
                 study, the S-transform was used as a time–frequency distribution derived from STFT
                 and WT. Each EEG segment was converted using a windowed S-transform, and after
                 spectrum power density estimation and feature extraction, classification was performed
                 using an ANN [111].

                 4 Research gaps & future scope
                 It has been well-known that computer-based methods for signal processing and machine
                 learning are useful research tools. They assist in creating automated solutions that might
                 reduce the strain on humans and improve the accuracy of the models by removing the
                 most crucial properties from the EEG signals. The brain signals of patients can be used
                 in healthcare to feed models that make automated choices for their disease, saving both
Pant and Kumar Discover Electronics   (2026) 3:20                                                               Page 14 of 19




                 patients’ and physicians’ time in hospitals. EEG signals may be used to create prediction
                 models that continuously track a person’s mental state. Based on changes in behavior, a
                 warning system can be created to assist patients in making informed decisions. In the
                 current time, the detection of this epileptic seizure is a very challenging job not only for
                 researchers, doctors, or neurologists but also for the skilled person in brain structure
                 who believes in the graphic scan of this EEG. So there is a need for a handy portable
                 automatic device or models for seizure detection. Models that are less computation-
                 ally demanding and take up less storage space. Real-time data contains many artifacts.
                 Therefore, it is necessary to provide extremely effective filtering methods that can assist
                 in reducing the noise and improving the performance of the models. Existing solutions
                 come up with microcontroller-based systems and low-capacity FPGAs like SPARTAN
                 2 and SPARTAN-3 FPGAs, which are limited to 180 nm technology and have a larger
                 delay. For future medical electronics applications and wearable devices. The proposed
                 solutions can be carried on large-scale FPGAs like Virtex-7, and Z board FPGA, which
                 work on 16–45 nm technology, with less delay and optimal area. The next scope can be a
                 comparison of system performance in MATLAB and HDL environments, and estimate
                 the minimum area, delay, and higher system performance for future medical electronics
                 applications and wearable devices. Based on the identification of the research gaps, the
                 following research gaps have been identified.

                    • 	 Limited Implementation of Low-Power Analysis in FPGA-Based EEG

                 Despite advancements, there remains a lack of effective integration of adaptive clock
                 gating and low-power FPGAs in EEG signal processing systems. Most existing designs
                 focus on performance rather than energy efficiency, which is critical for wearable or por-
                 table medical devices.

                    • 	 Real-Time Simulation of Epileptic EEG Processing Modules

                 Many studies focus on isolated components, such as filters or classifiers, but fail to pres-
                 ent a complete system simulation of epileptic EEG signals in real time. An integrated
                 hardware-based real-time framework is still underexplored.

                    • 	 Lack of a Scalable and Efficient Filter for Large-Scale EEG Analysis

                 There is a noticeable gap in scalable signal processing techniques capable of handling
                 high-resolution or long-duration EEG recordings. Optimized filter architectures that
                 ensure low latency and high accuracy in time-domain and frequency-domain analysis
                 are needed.

                    • 	 Limited use of HDL Design for FPGA Resource Optimization

                 Few studies address the use of advanced HDL coding practices, such as pipeline optimi-
                 zation and memory hierarchy management, to minimize area, delay, and power on high-
                 end FPGAs. This hampers the ability to push performance limits effectively.

                    • 	 Limited work on Software Simulations and Hardware Implementations

                 Comparative studies between MATLAB simulations and actual HDL implementations
                 are scarce. Quantitative insights into the performance differences in terms of area, tim-
                 ing, and power between these environments could guide more accurate system-level
                 design decisions.
Pant and Kumar Discover Electronics        (2026) 3:20                                                            Page 15 of 19




                    • 	 Limited use of Artificial Intelligence and machine learning

                 Current EEG signal processing systems lack the integration of adaptive intelligence and
                 learning mechanisms that could enhance real-time decision-making and system self-
                 optimization. There is minimal research on embedding lightweight, trainable modules
                 within FPGA hardware that can adapt to dynamic EEG patterns, especially for epilep-
                 tic seizure detection. This gap restricts the system’s ability to improve performance over
                 time or adjust to subject-specific EEG variations.

                 5 Conclusion
                 The paper presents that for the detection of seizures pipeline five main steps: the first
                 is gathering of data (signal acquisition), then preprocessing of data to remove noise or
                 interference, then feature extraction, classification of extracted features for identification
                 and and last is post-processing decisions for detection of disease. The review paper con-
                 cludes that the FIR better filter for removing noise as it has many advantages over the
                 IIR filter. Digital filters are generally used for biomedical applications because of their
                 higher stability, and these filters have no feedback. The FIR filters give a linear phase
                 response. The digital IIR filter using an FPGA is used to achieve faster acquisition of
                 biomedical signals, specifically EEG signals. A high-order IIR filter is used for this pur-
                 pose to make EEG signals noise-free, inexpensive, and easy to useFor removing the noise
                 from the EEG signal, we can use FIR filtering through the window design method. Low
                 stop band attenuation may result from excessive ripples in the passband caused by FIR
                 filtering. Therefore, the window methods solve the issue in the preliminary stage. The
                 wavelet approach, which operates in the time-frequency domain, is superior for feature
                 extraction. Finally, the SVM technique can be used for signal classification due to its bet-
                 ter accuracy.
