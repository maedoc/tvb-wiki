       31      Abstract
       32      Perivascular spaces (PVS) are cerebrospinal fluid-filled tunnels around brain blood vessels,
       33      crucial for the functions of the glymphatic system. Changes in PVS have been linked to
       34      vascular diseases and aging, necessitating accurate segmentation for further study. PVS
       35      segmentation poses challenges due to their small size, varying MRI appearances, and the
       36      scarcity of annotated data. We present a finely segmented PVS dataset from T2-weighted
       37      MRI scans, sourced from the Human Connectome Project Aging (HCP-Aging), encompassing
       38      200 subjects aged 30 to 100. Our approach utilizes a combination of unsupervised and deep
       39      learning techniques with manual corrections to ensure high accuracy. This dataset aims to
       40      facilitate research on PVS dynamics across different ages and to explore their link to
       41      cognitive decline. It also supports the development of advanced image segmentation
       42      algorithms, contributing to improved medical imaging automation and the early detection of
       43      neurodegenerative diseases.
       44
       45      Background & Summary
       46      Perivascular spaces (PVS) are annular cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)-filled tunnels around blood
       47      vessels in the brain1. As conduits of the central nervous system (CNS) to the lymphatic
       48      drainage in the brain, PVS play a substantial role in the clearance function of the glymphatic
       49      (glial-lymphatic) system2-4.

       50      Morphological changes in PVS have clinical implications, as enlarged PVS (ePVS) are linked to
       51      cerebrovascular diseases, including cerebral small vessel disease, cerebral amyloid
       52      angiopathy, hypertension, and stroke3,5-8. ePVS are associated with aging and cardiovascular
       53      risks in healthy adults as well as neurodegenerative diseases9-11. ePVS distribute throughout
       54      the white matter, basal ganglia, and hippocampus and are visualized using high-resolution
       55      Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI). Therefore, accurately identifying and segmenting ePVS
       56      on MRI is crucial for understanding their role in normal aging and brain diseases.

       57      ePVS have the same intensity characteristics as cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) on MRI, which is
       58      hypointense (dark) on T1-weighted (T1w) and hyperintense (bright) on T2-weighted (T2w)
       59      MRI images. Yet, they are varying in size from sub-millimeter to 3mm, and appear as
       60      thousands of thin, tubular structures. Further, ePVS are often confused with white matter
       61      hyperintensity and lacunar infarcts12, requiring high-level expertise that identifies ePVS in
       62      different shapes as round, ovoid, or tubular depending on the slice orientation of MRI13.
       63      These challenges make annotation difficult, resulting in limited datasets with ePVS labels.

       64      Efforts have been made to explore various computational methods to segment ePVS, yet
       65      significant challenges remain. Earlier, Frangi filter-based method was adopted as it can
       66      detect ePVS without dependency on labeled data14-16. However, this results in low specificity,
       67      resulting in numerous false positives. More recently, supervised automated methods have
       68      demonstrated improved performance17 but often generate fraction of labeled ePVS,
       69      resulting in false negatives. This is partly due to the limited data available for training
       70      supervised models, preventing machine learning models from achieving accuracy for clinical
       71      utility. These limitations underscore the need for high-quality, comprehensive ePVS label
       72      datasets to improve the accuracy and reliability of segmentation methods for new subjects.

       73      Here, we present a dataset of ePVS that has been finely segmented on T2w MRI. This dataset
       74      includes 200 subjects sampled from the Human Connectome Project Aging (HCP-Aging)18,
       75      ensuring a wide representation of ages and a uniform distribution across age groups ranging
       76      from 36 to 100 years old. We employ a combination of semi-supervised deep learning19, and
       77      iterative manual correction methods to label ePVS precisely. The release of this dataset aims
       78      to provide a reliable foundation for researchers to further study the dynamic changes of
       79      ePVS at a wide age range and investigate their potential link to various risk factors of
       80      cerebrovascular and neurodegenerative diseases, as well as cognitive decline. Additionally, it
       81      can be used to develop, train, and validate new image segmentation algorithms, thereby
       82      advancing the automation of medical imaging processing.

       83      Methods
       84      Subjects.        This study leveraged data from the HCP-Aging Lifespan Release 2.0. At the
       85      time of recruitment, subjects were in good health, without a diagnosed history of neurologic
       86      or major psychiatric disorder, symptomatic stroke or Alzheimer’s disease. The dataset also
       87      includes the self-reported Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) questionnaire20. Participants
       88      with Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scores below 19 were excluded from the HCP-
       89      Aging21, but those with a MoCA with 20 or higher were included. However, it is noted that a
       90      MoCA score within the range of 18-25 is generally classified as mild cognitive impairment
       91      (MCI)21.

       92      Clinical characteristics.           The patient selection workflow for the study dataset is
       93      illustrated in Fig. 1. Initially, there were 765 subjects with neuroimaging data available in
       94      HCP-Aging. First, we selected 217 subjects while considering as much as uniform distribution
       95      of age and sex. After visually confirming the image quality of T2w MRI, 17 were excluded due
       96      to image motion artifacts (n=11) or very large lacunar infarcts (n=6), resulting in 200 subjects

       99      As the vascular risk factors are associated with ePVS, we present the distribution of study
      100      cohort’s body mass index (BMI), blood pressure, and comprehensive metabolic panel in Fig.
      101      2. BMI was calculated from height (cm) and weight (kg) measurements collected during
      102      subject’s interviews using the formula weight (kg)/height (m)2. According to the Centers for
      103      Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)22, individuals with a BMI of 30 or higher are classified
      104      as obese. Systolic and diastolic blood pressure were measured with subjects in a seated
      105      position. According to the guidelines from the American College of Cardiology (ACC) and the
      106      American Heart Association (AHA)23, hypertension is defined as: a systolic blood pressure of
      107      130 mmHg or higher, or a diastolic blood pressure of 80 mmHg or higher. High density
      108      lipoprotein (HDL), hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), plasma glucose, triglycerides, vitamin D, and
      109      total cholesterol levels were tested from fasting blood samples. Diabetes is diagnosed using
      110      HbA1c and fasting glucose. According to the American Diabetes Association (ADA)24, diabetes
      111      is indicated by an A1c level of 6.5% or higher, or an fasting plasma glucose level of 126 mg/dL
      112      (7.0 mmol/L) or higher. The detailed measures and the diagnosis are shown in Table 1.

      113      Imaging parameters. Subjects were scanned using Siemens 3T Prisma scanner with an 80
      114      mT/m gradient coil and a 32-channel Siemens receive head coil, at Washington University in
      115      St. Louis, Missouri. T1 multi-echo MP-RAGE scans were acquired with the following
      116      acquisition parameters: repitition time (TR) = 2500ms, time of inversion (TI) = 1000ms, time
      117      to echo (TE) = 1.8/3.6/5.4/7.2 ms, 4 echoes per line of k-space, voxel size = 0.8 mm isotropic,
      118      FOV=256 x 240 x 166 mm, matrix = 320 x 300 x 208 slices, flip angle =8°. T2 turbo-spin echo
      119      (TSE) scans were acquired with TR/TE = 3200/564 ms, turbo factor = 314, and same voxel
      120      size, FOV and matrix with T1 images.

      121      Image processing.        T1 images were preprocessed using Montreal Neurological Institute
      122      (MNI)-CIVET pipeline25, including brain-extraction and bias correction. Both T1 and T2 images
      123      were linearly co-registered together, to eliminate the slight slice misalignment during image
      124      acquisition. Binary brain mask, excluding the dura matter, cerebellum, and brainstem,
      125      produced by CIVET pipeline were applied to T2w images25. Then histogram equalization was
      126      performed on T2w images, making ePVS more discernible26. Non-local mean denoising was
      127      employed to remove noise from T2w images27.

      128      Initial weakly auto-segmentation of ePVS.        We performed auto-segmentation using the
      129      Frangi filter. The parameters for the Frangi filter were empirically selected to accentuate
      130      tubular shapes across a range of diameters, using sigma values of [0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5,
      131      0.75, 1.0]. Given that ePVS typically span only 1-2 voxels in diameter, these sigma values
      132      were chosen to be smaller than those typically used for vascular segmentation, aiming to
      133      detect finer tubular shapes. The smaller sigma values are more noise-sensitive, highlighting
      134      the necessity of prior denoising. The MNI-CIVET pipeline was utilized to apply a white matter
      135      (WM) mask, effectively isolating ePVS within the WM by removing false positives outside
      136      WM25.

      137      Manual refinement for 40 subjects. After applying the Frangi filter, the initial
      138      segmentation underwent manual refinement, which was performed using ITK-snap28. To
      139      ensure the training samples for the upcoming semi-supervised learning, 40 subjects were
      140      selected out of the initial 200 subjects, by selecting four to five subjects from each decade of
      141      life. Two neuroanatomists manually refined them, aiming to correct and complete the PVS
      142      segmentation flagged by the Frangi filter and eliminate mis-labeled PVS.

      143      Given the widespread distribution of ePVS throughout the brain, the white matter including
      144      the centrum semiovale (CS) and the deep gray matter including the basal ganglia (BG) and
      145      hippocampus are known to exhibit the highest PVS burden7,29. In this study, we focus
      146      specifically on ePVS located within the white matter. Consequently, our neuroanatomists did
      147      not include ePVS in the deep gray matter region.

      148      When correcting ePVS and adding new ePVS for completeness, the primary criterion used
      149      was the appearance across the 3 orthogonal slices (i.e. axial, coronal, and sagittal). In one
      150      slice, most ePVS appeared as long tubular structures, whereas on the other views, they
      151      appeared as small ovoid shapes, ranging from submillimeter (one voxel thick on HCP-A T2w
      152      images) to 3 mm in diameter30. To evaluate the tubular shape of ePVS more specifically on
      153      T2w MRI with 0.8mm isotropic resolution, we examined whether hyperintense voxels
      154      consisting of an ovoid shape were present on at least three consecutive slices in one view
      155      and linearity in voxels appearing on 1-2 slices in other views. However, some ePVS that were
      156      located obliquely to all the 3 orthogonal planes appeared with a shorter tubular shape in all
      157      3 views13. The neuroanatomists confirmed these structures by simultaneously examining the
      158      3 orthogonal slices to differentiate them from lacunes, exhibiting a hyperintense “donut”
      159      shape, and white matter hyperintensities (WMH), exhibiting oval shape in all the 3
      160      orthogonal views. These features are often confused with or connected to ePVS when
      161      evaluated solely on a single view31.

      162      Automated segmentation generated by Frangi filter often misidentified WMH and lacunes as
      163      ePVS, particularly when these lesions are adjacent or connected to ePVS29,31. To address this
      164      issue, neuroanatomists manually excised voxels corresponding to WMH and lacunes by
      165      applying an imaginary boundary considering the tubular shape of ePVS.

      166      Semi-supervised deep learning segmentation.              The manually refined labels from
      167      the 40 selected subjects above were used to train and validate a U-Net model for supervised
      168      learning. The U-Net model was designed with a five-layer encoder-decoder structure; each
      169      encoder layer consists of two sets of 3x3 convolutional layers and Leaky ReLU layers,
      170      followed by a MaxPooling layer19. The loss function combined Dice loss and binary cross-
      171      entropy loss to address the issue of class imbalance present in the dataset, where ePVS
      172      occupy a significantly smaller proportion of the images. This choice of loss function improved
      173      model accuracy in imbalanced samples. Unlabeled images (n=40 randomly chosen from 160
      174      data) were augmented by enhancing contrast and adding noise32, aiming to enable the
      175      model to capture various curved and subtle ePVS with intensity levels closer to the
      176      surrounding white matter. These augmented data were included in the final step for semi-
      177      supervised learning process. Dice discrepancies between the model's predictions for
      178      augmented images and their original counterparts were used to calculate consistency loss,
      179      thus informing model updates. This strategy ensured that the model was exposed to both
      180      labeled and unlabeled dataset, accommodating the variability in ePVS appearances. After
      181      training, the model predicted ePVS segmentation across the left 160 dataset (aside 40 from
      182      initial 200).

      183      Quality assurance and final refinement.           Manual refinement of the deep learning-
      184      generated ePVS labels was performed by the aforementioned two neuroradiologists and one
      185      medical student trained by them, following the same protocol described earlier. To ensure
      186      consistency and accuracy, the three members convened weekly to reach a consensus on any
      187      uncertain cases. Fig. 3-5 present the final refined ePVS annotations on T2w MRI, along with
      188      3D renderings of the PVS burden for three subjects representing ages in 30’s, 50’s and 70’s.

      200      Technical Validation
      201      To validate the consistency and accuracy of the segmentation results, we randomly chose 10
      202      cases, and evaluated the reproducibility across three raters. We used Fleiss Kappa statistics
      203      to calculate the inter-rater reliability among the three raters33. As an additional agreement
      204      metric, we computed the DICE agreement coefficient between all pairs of experts. The Kappa
