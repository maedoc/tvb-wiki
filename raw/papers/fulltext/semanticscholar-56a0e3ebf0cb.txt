21   Abstract

22   Humans exhibit laterality preferences, with handedness being the most extensively studied.

23   Accordingly, brain-handedness associations are well documented. However, laterality

24   preferences extend beyond handedness to include other limbs and organs, such as footedness

25   and eyedness. Despite these distinctions, brain-footedness and brain-eyedness associations




                                                                               t
                                                                             ip
26   using resting-state functional connectomes remain largely unexplored. We utilize two large




                                                                           cr
27   datasets, the Human Connectome Project-Development (HCP-D) and Human Connectome




                                                                     us
28   Project-Aging (HCP-A), to study the associations between sidedness (i.e., handedness,

29   footedness, and eyedness) and whole-brain functional connectomes. While hand and foot




                                                              an
30   preferences were correlated significantly, they explained less than 40% of the variance,

31
                                                        M
     suggesting some distinctions between the measures. For both cohorts, significant associations
                                               ed
32   between handedness and connectivity were observed (p<0.05, NBS corrected). Notable

33   patterns include increased connectivity for left-handedness in the posterior temporal areas and
                                         pt

34   right-handedness in cerebellar regions. In contrast, significant associations between footedness
                                  ce



35   and connectivity were observed only in the HCP-A (p<0.05, NBS corrected) and not the HCP-D.
                           Ac




36   These connectivity patterns were similar to the handedness results. No significant associations

37   between eyedness and connectivity were observed for either group. Finally, we compared the
                   ro




38   effect size between brain-handedness and brain-footedness associations. A greater difference
            eu




39   was found in the HCP-D. The two cohorts primarily differed in edge distribution in the prefrontal
     eN




40   lobe, temporal lobe, and cerebellum. Overall, in adults, brain-handedness and brain-footedness

41   associations were similar. However, in children to adolescents, brain-handedness and brain-

42   footedness associations diverge, suggesting a developmental shift. Characterizing sidedness

43   associations with whole-brain connectomes may provide important insights into understanding

44   the motor and visual systems, rehabilitation and occupational therapy, and benchmarking

45   normative variations in the connectome.

46   Significance Statement

47   Lateral preferences stem from functional biases in brain hemispheres. However, studies

48   associating brain connectivity to these predominant preferences have often been oversimplified

49   to handedness. Although approximately 90% of the population is right-handed, other lateral

50   preferences—like footedness and eyedness—show much lower and more varied distributions.




                                                                               t
                                                                             ip
51   This study aims to provide a foundational understanding of how broader lateral preferences




                                                                           cr
52   might offer a more nuanced view of the brain's functional connectivity.

54   Introduction

55   Humans have laterality preferences, with handedness being the most studied. Not only does

56   handedness play a role in everyday life with motor control, but it has been linked to various

57   physical and mental health outcomes (Klar, 1999; Deep-Soboslay et al., 2010; Hirnstein and

58   Hugdahl, 2014; Wiberg et al., 2019; Robert L Sainburg, 2023).




                                                                                 t
                                                                               ip
59




                                                                             cr
60   Accordingly, brain-handedness associations are well-known. The brains of left-handed




                                                                      us
61   individuals are organized differently regarding language, motor, somatosensory, and the corpus




                                                               an
62   callosum (Habib et al., 1991; Gut et al., 2007). Studies have shown that language(Knecht et al.,

63

64
                                                         M
     2000; Szaflarski et al., 2002; Wilberg et al., 2009; Ocklenburg et al., 2014; Esteves et al., 2021),

     motor(Solodkin et al., 2001; Hammond, 2002; Hatta, 2007), and somatosensory(Hoshiyama and
                                                 ed
65   Kakigi, 1999; Sörös et al., 1999; Jung et al., 2008) networks demonstrate robust activation and
                                          pt

66   connectivity differences between left- and right-handed individuals. Recent work has shown that
                                    ce


67   brain-handedness associations extend beyond localized regions of interest to widespread

68   functional connectivity differences, affecting every canonical brain network(Faurion et al., 1999;
                            Ac




69   Perlaki et al., 2013; Tejavibulya et al., 2022).
                    ro




70
            eu




71   Laterality preferences extend far beyond handedness. Preferences exist for other limbs and
     eN




72   organs, including feet (i.e., footedness) and eyes (i.e., eyedness). It is often assumed that other

73   laterality preferences follow handedness (Schepman, 2024), and, as such, they remain under-

74   studied compared to handedness (Elias et al., 1998). Nevertheless, these preferences are

75   unique and potentially have different brain associations. For example, although 90% of the

76   population is right-handed, this percentage drops considerably for foot and eye

77   preference(Bourassa et al., 1996; Muraleedharan et al., 2020). Putatively, footedness predicts


                                                                                                        4
78   cognition and language better than handedness (Elias et al., 1998). Similarly, changes in ocular

79   dominance (i.e., switch from right eye preference to left eye preference) cause neural circuit

80   changes in the visual cortex (Sato and Stryker, 2008). Although distinct from handedness, brain-

81   footedness and brain-eyedness associations using whole-brain functional connectomes have

82   not been studied. Such associations may have important insights for understanding the motor




                                                                                t
83   and visual systems, for rehabilitation and occupational therapy, and for benchmarking normative




                                                                              ip
84   variations in the connectome.

133   Preprocessing and generating connectomes
               eu




134   Both the HCP-D and HCP-A datasets were analyzed with identical processing pipelines. T1w

135   scans were first skull-stripped using an optimized version of the FMRIB’s Software Library
      eN




136   (FSL)(Smith et al., 2004) pipeline(Lutkenhoff et al., 2014). Functional images were motion-

137   corrected using SPM12. All further analyses were performed using BioImage Suite(Joshi et al.,

138   2011). Several covariates of no interest were regressed from the data, including linear and

139   quadratic drifts, mean cerebral-spinal-fluid (CSF) signal, mean white-matter signal, and mean

140   gray matter signal. For additional control of possible motion-related confounds, a 24-parameter

141   motion model (including six rigid-body motion parameters, six temporal derivatives, and these

                                                                                                        9
142   terms squared) was regressed from the data. The data were temporally smoothed with a

143   Gaussian filter (approximate cutoff frequency=0.12 Hz).


144


145   Nodes were defined using the Shen 268-node brain atlas(Shen et al., 2013), which includes the

146   cortex, subcortex, and cerebellum. The atlas was warped from MNI space into single-subject




                                                                                t
                                                                              ip
147   space via a series of linear and non-linear transformations calculated using a previously




                                                                            cr
148   validated algorithm(Scheinost et al., 2017), implemented in BioImage Suite. Resting state




                                                                     us
149   connectivity was calculated as the mean time courses for each of the 268 nodes (i.e., averaging




                                                                an
150   the time courses of all constituent voxels). Node-by-node pairwise correlations were computed,

151   and Pearson correlation coefficients were Fisher z-transformed to yield symmetric 268x268

152                                                     M
      connectivity matrices, in which each matrix element represents the connectivity strength
                                                ed
153   between two individual nodes (i.e., ‘edge’).
                                          pt

154   Statistical analyses
                                   ce



155   To investigate the associations between handedness, footedness, and eyedness, Spearman's
                            Ac




156   correlations (ρ) were calculated between pairs of sidedness measures and subsequently

157   squared to obtain the variance explained. We used Z-tests and Steiger’s z-test for dependent
                    ro




158   correlations to compare effect sizes(Steiger, 1980; Lee, I. A., & Preacher, K. J., 2013) between
             eu




159   associations and groups.
      eN




160


161   We separately assessed edge-wise Spearman’s correlations with the handedness, footedness,

162   and eyedness scores for the HCP-D and HCP-A datasets to investigate brain-handedness,

163   brain-footedness, and brain-eyedness associations. We used the Network-Based Statistics

164   (NBS)(Zalesky et al., 2010) to identify components where functional connectivity was



                                                                                                     10
165   significantly associated with handedness while controlling for the family-wise error rate. We

166   used a component-determining threshold z=1.96, 2-tails, and K=5000 permutations. NBS is

167   analogous to cluster-based correction and solves the statistical problem of massively multiple

168   comparisons in a whole-brain connectivity analysis. In NBS, using the correlation between

169   sidedness measures and connectivity, the largest fully connected network of suprathreshold




                                                                                 t
170   edges, or “component,” is identified, and its extent is defined as the number of edges it




                                                                               ip
171   comprises. Finally, these calculations are repeated for 5,000 iterations, in which subjects’ group




                                                                             cr
172   assignments are randomly permuted to create a null distribution for the expected component




                                                                      us
173   size due to chance. NBS results are shown at p<0.05, corrected for multiple comparisons.




                                                               an
174


175                                                      M
      We examined the overlap of significant edges from the NBS analyses to investigate the
                                                ed
176   convergence of connectivity profiles across sidedness measures. Significance was determined

177   with the hypergeometric cumulative density function(Rosenberg et al., 2016), which returns the
                                          pt


178   probability of drawing up to x of K possible items in n drawings without replacement from an M-
                                   ce



179   item population. In other words, this approach calculates the probability of finding the number of
                            Ac




180   overlapping from two independent analyses by chance. Given many possible edges (> 35,000),

181   finding overlapping edges when randomly sampling two sets of ∼500 edges is rare. This was
                    ro




182   implemented in Matlab as: p = 1-hygecdf(x, M, K, n), where x equals the number of overlapping
             eu




183   edges, K equals the number of connections in the HCP-D, n equals the number of connections
      eN




184   in the HCP-A, and M equals the total number of edges in the matrix (35,778).


185


186   As thresholding results based on significance can modulate observed group differences(Taylor

187   et al., 2023), we also examined the effect sizes for brain-handedness, brain-footedness, and

188   brain-eyedness associations for the HCP-D and the HCP-A datasets without thresholding for



                                                                                                       11
189   significance. We quantified the effect size using Cohen’s D. To investigate if edges strongly

190   associated with handedness were strongly associated with footedness or eyedness, we

191   correlated effect sizes for all 35,778 edges. We used Z-tests to compare these correlations

192   across groups.

273   Discussion

274   We investigated how lateral preferences—extending beyond handedness preferences—

275   associate with the brain's functional organization across two distinct cohorts and generations

276   using data from the HCP-Development and HCP-Aging datasets. Behavioral associations

277   between handedness, footedness, and eyedness showed weaker correlations in the younger




                                                                                t
                                                                              ip
278   cohort (HCP-D) than in the older cohort (HCP-A). Footedness and handedness associations




                                                                            cr
279   strengthened in the older cohorts, while eyedness exhibited no associations between the other




                                                                      us
280   two measures. For both cohorts, significant associations between handedness and connectivity

281   were observed. Notable patterns include increased connectivity for left-handedness in the




                                                               an
282   posterior temporal areas and right-handedness in cerebellar regions. In contrast, significant

283
                                                         M
      associations between footedness and connectivity were observed only in the HCP-A. These
                                                ed
284   connectivity patterns were similar to the handedness results. No significant associations

285   between eyedness and connectivity were observed for either group. Finally, we compared the
                                          pt

286   effect size between brain-handedness and brain-footedness associations. A greater difference
                                   ce



287   was found in the HCP-D. The two cohorts primarily differed in edge distribution in the prefrontal
                            Ac




288   lobe, temporal lobe, and cerebellum. Overall, in adults, brain-handedness and brain-footedness

289   associations were similar. However, in children to adolescents, brain-handedness and brain-
                    ro




290   footedness associations diverge, suggesting a developmental shift. Characterizing sidedness
             eu




291   associations with whole-brain connectomes provide a starting point for benchmarking normative
      eN




292   variations.


293


294   Across analyses, the cerebellum and prefrontal cortex consistently showed differential

295   connectivity patterns associated with handedness and footedness. The cerebellum is known to

296   develop substantially postnatally (Friede, 1973), and both the cerebellum and the prefrontal

297   cortex are heavily influenced by environmental factors (Rossi et al., 1997; National Research

                                                                                                       17
298   Council et al., 2000; Angelucci et al., 2009; Hodel, 2018). Previous studies have highlighted the

299   cerebellum involvement in identifying left- and right-handed groups (Tejavibulya et al., 2022)

300   and fine motor development (Kusano et al., 2021; Ashida et al., 2022; Saadon-Grosman et al.,

301   2022). Notable these regions extend beyond regions classically associated with laterality

302   preferences, like language(Dunaif-Hattis, 1984; Knecht et al., 2000; Wiberg et al., 2019), motor




                                                                                    t
303   (Dassonville et al., 1997; Solodkin et al., 2001; Klöppel et al., 2010), and somatosensory areas




                                                                                  ip
304   (Sörös et al., 1999; Jung et al., 2008).




                                                                                cr
                                                                         us
305




                                                                  an
306   Most individuals have two hands, feet, and eyes, allowing laterality preferences to occur

307   naturally. Even so, whether we use our left or right side differently for a particular activity

308                                                        M
      depends on many factors (Coren, 1993). We can simultaneously use our hands for different
                                                  ed
309   purposes, contributing to how strongly we prefer our left or right hands for a specific activity

310   (Provins and Magliaro, 1993). In other words, an individual who writes with their right hand may
                                            pt


311   not necessarily throw with their right hand. Similarly, most humans have uneven
                                     ce



312   eyesight(Ehrenstein et al., 2005; Dupierrix et al., 2014). Purely studying handedness provides a
                             Ac




313   biased perspective of laterality preferences, as proportions of right-handedness are far more

314   polarizing than other measures of laterality(Germono, 1965; Tran et al., 2014). These variations
                     ro




315   in how an individual balances left- or right-handed tendencies across different measures are
             eu




316   often overlooked in handedness research despite studies indicating notable differences(Good et
      eN




317   al., 2001; Sun and Walsh, 2006; Gut et al., 2007). Previous studies have shown how and when

318   handedness is measured greatly affects neuroscience studies on brain-handedness

319   associations(Tejavibulya et al., 2024). Thus, genuinely capturing how lateral preferences shape

320   the brain's functional organization requires assessments beyond handedness. Though our

321   findings are only based on one measure of body laterality, further research using other laterality

322   measures will be needed to confirm our results.


                                                                                                         18
323


324   Despite recent advancements, left-handed individuals remain excluded mainly from human

325   neuroimaging studies. Research has yet to determine effective methods for controlling these

326   differences in brain function and when these differences matter to the question. Nevertheless,

327   this large-scale exclusion is counter to broader inclusive best practices in neuroimaging (Ricard




                                                                                   t
                                                                                 ip
328   et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024). Studies like ours that characterize the associations between




                                                                               cr
329   handedness, footedness, eyedness, and other laterality preferences and brain function are the




                                                                         us
330   first step in better involving left-handed individuals in neuroimaging studies.




                                                                 an
331


332

333
                                                           M
      We showed that footedness connectivity profiles converge onto that of handedness for the older

      but not the younger cohort. Overall, an overlooked aspect of studies on lateral preferences in
                                                  ed
334   the brain is the role of developmental trajectories and critical periods (Gabbard and Iteya, 1996;
                                           pt

335   Goodway et al., 2019).The development trajectories of laterality preferences occur on distinct
                                    ce


336   timelines for different body parts (Fig. 3). Speculatively, these results may offer insights into

337   potential comorbidities between handedness and psychiatric conditions (Logue et al., 2015;
                             Ac




338   Mallet et al., 2022; Mundorf et al., 2024). Adolescence is a sensitive and critical period in brain
                     ro




339   maturation, during which the risk for mental illness is significantly heightened (Chambers et al.,
             eu




340   2003; Casey and Jones, 2010; Nixon and McClain, 2010; Casey et al., 2011; Reynolds et al.,

341   2019). Similarly, our results suggest that it may also be a critical period for laterality preferences
      eN




342   and their associations in the brain. Further investigation into the associations between laterality

343   preferences, mental health, and brain development may be warranted.


344


345   Our study has several notable strengths. We investigated how different measures of body

346   laterality, beyond handedness, differentially affect the brain's functional organization. We also


                                                                                                          19
347   used large cohorts with harmonized imaging protocols and sidedness measures. Nevertheless,

348   our study has several limitations. One limitation of our work is the precision of measures of

349   footedness and eyedness. In contrast to handedness, which was summed over several

350   questions, they were reduced to a single preference question. In particular, for eyedness,

351   participants were asked which eye individuals prefer to remain open if one were to look through




                                                                                  t
352   a telescope. While it was the standard measure in two human connectome projects, it may be a




                                                                                ip
353   poor measure of eyedness. Other measures of eyedness will likely give strong associations with




                                                                              cr
354   the connectome. Thus, while we failed to show a significant eyedness association with




                                                                       us
355   functional connectivity, we cannot conclude for certain that brain-eyedness associations do not




                                                                an
356   exist. While the brain-footedness differences between cohorts suggests a development change,

357

358
                                                           M
      we lack longitudinal data over childhood to fully elucidate potential critical periods. Many studies

      over different generations are likely needed to map out generational effects fully. Longitudinal
                                                  ed
359   datasets (such as the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development Study (Casey et al., 2018)) may
                                           pt

360   represent a starting point for further investigations.
                                    ce



361
                             Ac




362   Lateral preferences associated with the brain’s functional organization. However, studies

363   associating brain connectivity to these predominant preferences have often been oversimplified
                     ro




364   to handedness. Although approximately 90% of the population is right-handed, other lateral
             eu




365   preferences—like footedness and eyedness—show much lower and more varied distributions.
      eN




366   Our study provides a broader view of lateral preferences and functional connectivity, offering a

367   more nuanced view. Characterizing sidedness associations with whole-brain connectomes

368   provide a starting point for understanding how and when handedness, footedness, and

369   eyedness need to be accounted for in fMRI analyses.
