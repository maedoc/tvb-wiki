Abstract
Epilepsy, characterized by abnormal neuronal hyperexcitability and disrupted

                                                            S
ion homeostasis, increasingly appears to involve complex neuron–glia
                                                          S
                                                         E
interactions. This study develops a three-compartment computational model

                                                       R
encompassing neurons, astrocytes, and the extracellular space to investigate

                                                      P
the effects of optogenetic stimulation (OS) of astrocytes expressing


                                               IN
channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2) and optogenetic inhibition (OI) of neurons
expressing Archaerhodopsin-T (ArchT). The model integrates dynamic sodium,

                               L E
potassium, and calcium concentrations with explicit Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and Kir4.1

                             C
channel mechanisms to simulate ionic regulation under normal and epileptic

                          T I
conditions. Simulations reveal that astrocytic OS markedly reduces seizure


                       A R
severity by enhancing Na⁺ influx and activating the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, thereby
accelerating extracellular K⁺ clearance and dampening neuronal excitability.
The anticonvulsant effect persists even when astrocytic calcium signaling or
Kir4.1 channel function is impaired, underscoring the dominant role of Na⁺/K⁺-
ATPase activity. Combined astrocytic stimulation and neuronal inhibition
further decrease extracellular potassium and neuronal spiking, indicating a
synergistic effect. Pre-seizure astrocyte activation proves substantially more
effective than post-onset stimulation. These findings highlight astrocytes as
active modulators of brain excitability and identify Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase–driven ion
regulation as a key mechanism underlying seizure control, offering a
mechanistic basis for glia-targeted optogenetic therapies in drug-resistant
epilepsy.
Keywords: Epilepsy, Optogenetics, Channel Rhodopsin-2, Sodium-Potassium
Pump, Mathematical Modeling of Neurons and Astrocytes

1 Introduction
Recent advances in optogenetics have transformed our ability to manipulate
and study neural circuits with high temporal and cell-type specificity [1].
Initially developed as a tool for precise control of neuronal activity,
optogenetics has rapidly expanded to include non-neuronal cells, most
notably astrocytes, whose contributions to neural computation and
dysfunction are increasingly recognized [2]. This shift is especially relevant
in the context of epilepsy, a disorder traditionally viewed through a neuron-
centric lens but now understood to involve complex neuron-glia interactions
[3]. The ability to stimulate channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2)-expressing astrocytes
with blue light and inhibit Archaerhodopsin-T (ArchT)-expressing neurons
using yellow light, not only provides insight into astrocytic and neuronal
functions but also opens up new therapeutic avenues for seizure control [4-
6]. Further investigation of the effects of astrocytic optogenetic stimulation
(OS) via ChR2 and neuronal optogenetic inhibition (OI) via ArchT is essential
to elucidate their combined potential in modulating seizure dynamics through

                                                 S
ionic regulation [2, 5, 6].


                                              ES
Unlike pharmacological treatments or electrical neuromodulation techniques


                                           PR
such as vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) or deep brain stimulation (DBS),
optogenetics offers cell-type-specific, reversible, and spatially precise

                                     IN
modulation of brain activity [6]. When applied to astrocytes, OS avoids direct
excitation of neurons, thereby minimizing the risk of triggering seizures—a

                          L E
major concern in neuronal optogenetic paradigms [2]. Instead, astrocytic

                       IC
stimulation induces slower, sustained regulatory effects on the local neural


                    RT
environment by enhancing mechanisms such as ion buffering,
gliotransmission, and Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase activity [5-7]. These functions are
                  A
essential for network stability and make astrocytic OS an attractive strategy
for modulating excitability without exacerbating hyperactivity.
There is an urgent need for improved interventions in drug-resistant epilepsy,
which accounts for approximately 30% of cases and is often refractory to
current treatment modalities [3]. Conventional antiepileptic drugs target ion
channels or synaptic transmission, typically aiming to reduce excitability or
enhance inhibition. However, they lack spatial precision and often affect
multiple cell types, leading to adverse cognitive and systemic side effects.
Electrical stimulation therapies face similar issues, with limited spatial
resolution and potential interference with normal brain function [5, 6]. In
contrast, OS of astrocytes operates through indirect but potent mechanisms
that confer longer-lasting and more stable control of neural networks,
without perturbing normal signaling patterns [2].
Epileptic seizures arise from pathological synchronization of neural
populations and are driven by disruptions in ion homeostasis, particularly
                         ACCEPTED
                           ARTICLEMANUSCRIPT
                                   IN PRESS



involving potassium (K⁺), sodium (Na⁺), and calcium (Ca²⁺) [7]. Traditionally,
the role of astrocytes in epilepsy has been underappreciated. Yet, growing
evidence suggests that these glial cells are not passive bystanders but active
modulators of extracellular ion concentrations and neurotransmitter
clearance [2]. Astrocytes buffer excess K⁺, take up glutamate, and maintain
resting membrane potentials through their control of the sodium-potassium
pump (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase). During seizures, the ability of astrocytes to clear
extracellular K⁺ and restore ionic gradients becomes crucial. If this pump
function is impaired, positive feedback loops of hyperexcitability can develop,
exacerbating seizure activity [7, 8].
Specifically, the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump exchanges three intracellular Na⁺ ions
for two extracellular K⁺ ions, a process essential not only for maintaining
electrochemical gradients but also for counteracting the depolarizing effects
of K⁺ accumulation during intense neural activity [9]. Dysfunction in this
mechanism—whether due to genetic mutation, metabolic stress, or
insufficient astrocytic activation—can result in elevated extracellular K⁺,


                                                 S S
intracellular Na⁺ buildup, and increased neuronal excitability, all of which
promote seizure propagation. Recent studies demonstrate that OS of

                                              R E
astrocytes enhances Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase activity, improving K⁺ uptake and

                                             P
rebalancing ionic homeostasis even in the absence of Ca²⁺ signaling pathways


                                       IN
[6].


                             E
In this context, ChR2-mediated OS offers a valuable intervention point. By

                           L
activating light-sensitive ion channels in astrocytes, it is possible to trigger
                         C
                        I
membrane depolarization and stimulate downstream ionic transport


                     RT
mechanisms. Unlike neuronal OI, which leads to millisecond-scale
depolarization and action potential firing, astrocytic OS produces slower,
                   A
cumulative effects over seconds to minutes—timescales more compatible
with homeostatic regulation than rapid signaling. This temporal dynamic
makes astrocytic OS particularly suited for suppressing seizures, where
sustained ion regulation is more critical than transient excitation or inhibition
[2].
Despite the increasing number of experimental studies demonstrating the
therapeutic potential of astrocytic OS, translating this potential into effective
protocols is hampered by the system's complexity. The slow, homeostatic
timescales of astrocytic effects interact non-linearly with fast neuronal
dynamics during seizures, making it difficult to empirically predict optimal
stimulation parameters. Consequently, computational models have yet to
fully incorporate these findings, and the field lacks mechanistic mathematical
frameworks that simulate how OS of astrocytes or OI of neurons impacts ion
dynamics and seizure susceptibility. Mathematical modeling plays a crucial
role in neuroengineering by enabling in silico exploration of dynamic,
                         ACCEPTED
                           ARTICLEMANUSCRIPT
                                   IN PRESS



nonlinear systems that are difficult to manipulate experimentally. Through
simulation, it becomes possible to isolate parameters, test hypotheses, and
predict outcomes of experimental interventions—all critical for the rational
design of neuromodulation therapies.
Several earlier models have contributed foundational insights into neuron-
astrocyte interactions. Nadkarni and Jung introduced a simple network
featuring a pyramidal neuron, interneuron, and astrocyte, showing that
astrocytic glutamate release can enhance neuronal excitability and promote
seizure-like activity when inhibitory tone is saturated [10]. Cressman et al.
developed a single-compartment neuron model that integrated dynamic
extracellular K⁺ and intracellular Na⁺ levels, revealing how small
perturbations in ion concentration can lead to seizure onset [11]. Sibille et al.
expanded these models to include astroglial Kir4.1 channels, emphasizing
their role in rapid K⁺ clearance and the importance of astrocyte function in
maintaining excitability thresholds [12].
More recently, Li et al. incorporated stochastic intracellular Ca²⁺ signaling

                                                 S S
into astrocytic models, showing that variability in IP₃-mediated pathways can


                                              R E
lead to depolarization block and seizure discharges [13]. Du et al. explored
how temperature-sensitive dysfunction of Kir4.1 channels contributes to
                                             P
febrile seizures, underscoring the vulnerability of astrocytic ion transport to

                                       IN
environmental stressors [14]. Rui et al. developed a neuron-astrocyte model

                             E
showing that low Kir4.1 channel conductance in astrocytes induces

                           L
spontaneous periodic epileptic activity (SPEA), while higher conductance
                         C
                        I
causes bursting and tonic discharges; abnormal voltage-gated calcium


                     RT
channel also triggers SPEA and spontaneous periodic bursting event.
Changes in extracellular potassium levels influence seizure-like discharge
                   A
transitions, offering insights into seizure mechanisms [15]. These studies
collectively highlight the multifactorial role of astrocytes in epileptic
dynamics—but none to date have explicitly modeled the effects of OS on these
regulatory pathways.
Despite remarkable advances, several knowledge gaps persist in the field.
There remains limited computational exploration of the effects of astrocyte
stimulation and neuronal inhibition via optogenetics in seizure control
scenarios. The timing and synchronization of optogenetic interventions have
not been systematically investigated in a model encompassing simultaneous
ionic, membrane, and pump-level variables. Many existing mathematical
models rely on oversimplified representations of ionic fluxes or ignore
essential cross-talk between neuron and astrocyte compartments.
Addressing these gaps, the present work develops a comprehensive
mathematical model of neuron–astrocyte interactions that tracks intra- and
                        ACCEPTED
                          ARTICLEMANUSCRIPT
                                  IN PRESS



extracellular concentrations of sodium, potassium, and calcium ions,
explicitly models membrane voltages and pump dynamics, and incorporates
mechanistically accurate optogenetic interventions using ChR2 (for astrocyte
stimulation) and ArchT (for neuronal inhibition). The model is designed to:
   Simulate normal and epileptic states by tuning physiological and
    pathophysiological parameter sets.
   Validate system behavior and output patterns against published
    experimental data.
   Systematically investigate the role of key astrocytic ion transport
    mechanisms and membrane pumps under basal and epileptic
    conditions.
   Assess the impact of temporal OS strategies on seizure reduction.
   Evaluate the relative importance of all parameters by global sensitivity
    analysis, facilitating targeted experimental and translational research.


                                               S S
By integrating cutting-edge biological insights with mathematically robust

                                              E
modeling, this study aims to advance understanding of seizure dynamics and
                                            R
                                           P
provide a framework for rational development and testing of novel glia-
targeted epilepsy therapies.

2.1 Simulation of normal and epileptic states
                       I
In our computational framework, the induction of epileptiform states is
                     T
                    R
mediated by the astrocyte-to-neuron excitatory current (Iastro ), which is

                  A
governed by the intracellular calcium concentration within astrocytes. This
current models the calcium-dependent release of excitatory gliotransmitters,
such as glutamate. Increasing the magnitude of Iastro elevates neuronal
excitability, leading to the emergence of seizure-like dynamical patterns,
including synchronized population oscillations and paroxysmal bursting
discharges.
To incorporate the intrinsic variability and pathological fluctuations
characteristic of epileptic networks, we introduced stochastic noise into the
neuronal dynamics. Following established methodologies [13], a Gaussian
white noise term with tunable amplitude was added to Equation (13) via the
dimensionless parameter DS. As the noise amplitude increases, the system
undergoes a transition from baseline physiological activity to sustained
epileptiform states, thereby simulating the role of dysregulated astrocytic
calcium signaling in ictogenesis [16, 17].

                                                        ARTICLEMANUSCRIPT
                                                                IN PRESS



While epilepsy is a network-level phenomenon, it is underpinned by
consistent cellular-level electrophysiological signatures during ictal events:
      High-frequency bursting (>10–50 Hz) and sustained tonic firing (30–
       100+ Hz).
      Pathological accumulation of extracellular potassium ([K⁺]ₑ), which
       creates a positive feedback loop driving further neuronal
       depolarization.
      Post-ictal hyperpolarization, mediated                               by   pump   activation   and
       potassium clearance mechanisms.
To calibrate the DS parameter such that the model's output reflects
experimentally observed ictal activity, we conducted a literature survey of
neuronal firing rates during seizures. These rates are phase-dependent,
ranging from pre-ictal bursting (10–50 Hz) to tonic-phase sustained firing
(30–100+ Hz) and clonic-phase rhythmic bursts (5–20 Hz). Reported values

                                                                            S
include ~20 Hz [10], 30–50 Hz [15], 20–150 Hz [18], and ~12 Hz [19].


                                                                         E
Our parameter sweep (Figure 1) confirmed that a DS value of 1.4 produces a
                                                                          S
                                                                       R
mean neuronal firing frequency of approximately 30 Hz, concomitant with an

                                                                      P
elevated extracellular [K⁺] concentration (Figure 4), consistent with the tonic


                                                                 IN
phase of a seizure. Therefore, we set DS = 1.4 to simulate the epileptic
condition and DS = 0 to represent the baseline, non-epileptic state in all
subsequent simulations.

2.2 Assessing the model's behavior
We begin by evaluating the consistency of the model's behavior with
established animal models. Figure 2a presents a comparison of the model
outputs with experimental data from [6]. The results demonstrate a
satisfactory agreement between the model outputs and the empirical data,
                         ACCEPTED
                           ARTICLEMANUSCRIPT
                                   IN PRESS



indicating that the model successfully replicates the behavior of the biological
system to a great extent.
Also, to investigate whether astrocytic calcium signaling is required for the
anticonvulsant effects of OS of astrocytes, a simulation was conducted in
which astrocytic calcium signaling was blocked during OS (IChr2Ca was set
to zero). Zhao, Sun et al. [6] used a pharmacological blocker to inhibit
astrocytic calcium signaling during OS. They concluded that while such
signaling is involved in seizure onset, it is not required for the anticonvulsant
effects resulting from OS of astrocytes. Our computational model aligns with
this conclusion, as the results in Figure 2a demonstrate that the presence or
absence of calcium only marginally alters the outcome.
Figure 2b-c examines the model's behavior over time following the
application of either astrocyte stimulation via ChR2 or neuronal inhibition via
ArchT. As can be seen, with ChR2 stimulation of the astrocyte, the seizure
intensity (ASE80-400 index) is drastically reduced, and the neuron exhibits

                                                   S
normal behavior. Furthermore, even after the stimulation is terminated at t


                                                ES
= 40 s, it remains in a regular sparse spiking state and does not return to the
epileptic seizure state. In contrast, when the neuron is inhibited by ArchT, its

                                             PR
spiking activity decreases but is not completely suppressed. Moreover, upon
the cessation of the ArchT optical stimulation, the neuron returns to the
epileptic seizure state.

Figure 4 shows the key changes in our model during normal and epileptic
states. Many behaviors reported in experimental studies are observed in this
figure. As expected, epileptic tissue has higher extracellular K⁺ levels (KO ).
This makes neurons fire more easily and keeps seizures going [20]. The high
KO comes from neurons losing K⁺ (KN ) during fast, repeated bursts, plus
                                                     ACCEPTED
                                                       ARTICLEMANUSCRIPT
                                                               IN PRESS



astrocytes failing to clear it away. Studies show that inside neurons, [K⁺] (KN )
can drop to 115–125 mM during strong seizures or synced brain activity. This
drop happens because K⁺ leaks out too fast through channels, while the pump
cannot bring it back in time [19].
During seizure-like activity, [Na⁺] inside neurons (NaN ) rises [21]. At the same
time, [Na⁺] outside neurons (NaO ) falls a little (by 4–7 mM) [21]. In epileptic
brains, astrocytes have less Kir4.1 channels, so they cannot buffer K⁺ well
[22]. To fix the high Na⁺ inside, neuronal Na⁺/K⁺ pumps work much harder
during seizures [23]. In response, astrocytes enhance their pump activity to
regulate the elevated extracellular K⁺ concentration. But in long-term
epilepsy, these pumps weaken, making K⁺ clearance worse and seizures
stronger.

2.3 Investigating the role of sodium-potassium pumps

When ChR2 is activated by light, it depolarizes astrocytic membrane and
raises intracellular sodium levels, which then triggers the astrocytic Na⁺/K⁺-
ATPase pump. Blocking this pump with drugs like ouabain completely stops
the seizure suppression caused by astrocytic OS, proving the pump's key role
[6].

To test this in our model, we removed the pump current entirely (Figure 5a).
As shown, even with OS in astrocytes, seizure intensity stays high without the
pump. This confirms that sodium-potassium pumps are essential in our model
for reducing seizures via ChR2 stimulation in astrocytes. The figure also
compares our model results to experimental data of [6], showing that the
model accurately matches real-world behavior.

For a closer look, Figure 5b tracks the ASE80-400 index over time during
astrocytic ChR2 stimulation under pump-present and pump-free conditions.
Clearly, without the pump, ChR2 stimulation in astrocytes has almost no
effect on stopping seizures.

2.4 The Effect of OS Timing on Seizure Control
The timing of OS aimed at astrocytes plays a crucial role in the therapeutic
control of epileptic seizures. Due to the rapid and reversible nature of
optogenetic tools, especially ChR2, researchers can precisely manipulate
astrocytic and neuronal signaling on a timescale that matches the fast
                         ACCEPTED
                           ARTICLEMANUSCRIPT
                                   IN PRESS



dynamics of seizure initiation and propagation. Delivering OS before an
epileptic event may stabilize the extracellular ionic environment, potentially
preventing the occurrence of seizure activity or reducing its severity.
Conversely, activating astrocytes after seizure onset provides an opportunity
for acute seizure interruption, effectively testing closed-loop interventions—
a clinically relevant paradigm analogous to responsive neurostimulation
devices in use for human epilepsy [25].
Recent studies suggest that the efficacy and outcome of optogenetic
interventions depend heavily on whether the stimulation is applied
prophylactically (before) or reactively (after) relative to the epileptic event.
This temporal specificity is not merely a technical advantage, but is essential
to optimizing neuroprotective effects, minimizing network disruptions, and
tailoring treatments to individual seizure profiles. Consequently,
investigating how the timing of OS influences seizure reduction in
mathematical and experimental models is foundational for translating these
methods into precise, patient-responsive therapies [26].

2.5 What is the role of Kir4.1 channel?
Figure 9 compares simulations of model under two conditions: full (100%)
versus halved (50%) Kir4.1 channel activity. At 50% Kir4.1 activity, KO rises
                                               ACCEPTED
                                                 ARTICLEMANUSCRIPT
                                                         IN PRESS



less and clears faster following seizure initiation than with 100% Kir4.1
activity. At 100% Kir4.1 activity, KO remains elevated for longer and stabilizes
at a higher post-seizure value than in the reduced Kir4.1 case. This is
counterintuitive but aligns with some computational and experimental
findings: excessive K⁺ uptake by astrocytes (via high Kir4.1) can “trap”
potassium intracellularly, resulting in reduced extracellular clearance via
other pathways (e.g., spatial buffering, Na+/K+-ATPase (NKA), or vascular
removal), and slower recovery to baseline [12, 27].
With 50% Kir4.1 activity, VN is less depolarized and recovers closer to
baseline after the seizure event. With 100% Kir4.1 activity, the neuron
depolarizes more extensively and remains depolarized longer. This suggests
that maximal Kir4.1 activity paradoxically sustains depolarizing influences,
perhaps by elevating or retaining excess intracellular K⁺ (in astrocytes),
thereby affecting ionic driving forces and membrane currents [15].

2.7 Global sensitivity analysis
Next, using the method described in [28], a sensitivity analysis of the model
was performed. The primary outcome of interest for the sensitivity analysis
                         ACCEPTED

                                   IN PRESS



is ASE80-400, and 500 simulations with different sets of parameter values were
run. The parameter values were randomly chosen from 0.8 to 1.2 of the values
in Table 2. Since there are two distinct pathways for stimulating astrocytes
(ChR2) and neurons (ArchT), the analysis has been performed once with
ChR2 stimulation in astrocytes and once with ArchT inhibition in neurons

at the model equations, it is evident that all of these parameters are in some

shared parameters between the two panels (such as d5, a2, and k3) are also
related to astrocyte calcium.

of neurons firing in response to stimuli, effectively lowering the threshold for
initiating action potentials. This increases the risk of synchronous bursting—
the activity characteristic of seizures [29].

3 Discussion
In this study, a three-compartment model was developed that describes the
dynamics of ions between neurons, astrocytes, and the extracellular space.
Also, the application of OS to astrocytes and OI to neurons was simulated
according to in vitro studies, and the role of astrocytes and sodium-potassium
pumps was investigated.
This study employs a reductionist, single neuron-astrocyte modeling
approach to investigate cellular ion homeostasis during the ictal state, a well-
established methodology in epilepsy biophysics (e.g., [11, 15, 30-32]). The
model does not simulate seizure generation (ictogenesis) at the network level.
Instead, it uses seizure-like electrophysiological input as a controlled,
phenomenological proxy to analyze the biophysical consequences of
sustained depolarization, high-frequency firing, and pathological ion shifts on
                         ACCEPTED
                           ARTICLEMANUSCRIPT
                                   IN PRESS



channel, pump, and astrocyte function. This design allows for isolation of the
core ion regulatory mechanisms that individual cells experience during a
seizure, complementing population-level studies of epilepsy.
The results show that OS of astrocytes expressing ChR2 can effectively
reduce the severity of neocortical seizures. This anticonvulsant effect is
related to the astrocyte Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, which prevents firing in pyramidal
neurons during seizures. OS of astrocytes also leads to sodium influx through
ChR2, which activates astrocyte Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. This increased activity helps
to remove excess extracellular potassium and reduces neuronal excitability.
Therefore, changes in astrocyte sodium and potassium concentrations are a
key therapeutic mechanism of OS of astrocyte.
Stimulation of astrocytes via ChR2 significantly reduced seizures (Figure 2b,
Figure 3b). This result suggests that astrocytes not only play a supporting
role in the brain environment, but also can act as active regulators in
response to neuronal activity. This finding could help develop new therapies
for epilepsy and other neurological disorders based on astrocyte stimulation.

                                                 S S
The role of astrocytes in modulating neuronal activity has attracted


                                              R E
increasing attention in recent years. For example, the study [33] showed that
OS of astrocytes can be used to modulate neuronal activity.
                                             P
                                       IN
Stimulation of astrocytes is more effective in reducing seizures than
inhibition of pyramidal neurons (Figure 2, Figure 3). This finding helps to

                           L E
better understand the interactions between neurons and astrocytes and to

                         C
clarify the role of astrocytes in regulating the electrical activity of the brain

                      T I
as a key factor in the control of seizures. Studies [34], [35] suggest that

                     R
targeting astrocyte functions could provide a therapeutic strategy for the

                   A
treatment of epilepsy. While inhibition of pyramidal neurons can reduce
seizure activity, it may not be as effective as stimulation of astrocytes.
Astrocytic calcium signaling is not essential for the anticonvulsant effects of
astrocyte stimulation (Figure 2a). This suggests that astrocytes use other
mechanisms to regulate neuronal activity and reduce seizures. This could
help future research to identify these mechanisms and how they work. Shah
et al. [36] have shown that changes in calcium dynamics can contribute to
neuroinflammatory processes and neuronal excitability, but do not directly
affect seizure control mechanisms. Studies have also shown that astrocyte
activation can enhance seizure activity through calcium-mediated glial
glutamate release [37], [38].
The results of this study point to the importance of the astrocytic sodium-
potassium pump in OS (Figure 5). This pump directly plays a role in
controlling the electrical activity of the brain by maintaining ion balance and
regulating the membrane potential of astrocytes. This finding suggests that
                         ACCEPTED
                           ARTICLEMANUSCRIPT
                                   IN PRESS



the sodium-potassium pump is effective not only in regulating the ion balance
in astrocytes but also in reducing seizure activity. As a result, targeting this
pump could be considered as a new therapeutic strategy for controlling
seizures. Studies [39], [40] show that activation of the sodium-potassium
pump leads to a decrease in extracellular potassium, which subsequently
prevents the firing of pyramidal neurons during seizures.
In addition, Figure 7 compares seizure severity (ASE₈₀–₄₀₀) under different
conditions where the astrocytic Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase effect on specific ionic
pathways was selectively removed. The results of this figure align closely with
established neurophysiology:
      The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase activity in astrocytes is vital for maintaining low
       extracellular K⁺ during neuronal activity, ensuring neuronal
       repolarization and preventing hyperexcitability.
      When astrocytic K⁺ uptake via the pump is compromised, extracellular
       [K⁺] rises, leading to sustained depolarization and seizure-like


                                                 S S
       discharges [41, 42]. Similarly, extracellular Na⁺ regulation supports
       the Na⁺ gradient required for astrocytic glutamate transporters, which

                                              R E
       limit excitatory neurotransmission [43, 44]. Therefore, the model’s

                                             P
       heightened seizure severity upon removing pump effects on Ko or NaA


                                      IN
       matches experimental and computational findings demonstrating that
       astrocytic pump dysfunction exacerbates epileptiform activity [45].

                           L E
The results of this study also showed that OS of astrocytes before the onset

                        IC
of a seizure is much more effective than applying stimulation after the seizure


                     RT
begins. These patterns align with established neurobiological behavior. If
astrocytes are depolarized in advance, they preemptively increase metabolic
                   A
readiness (via Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase priming and elevated transporter activity),
mitigating ionic accumulation and desynchronizing hyperexcitatory waves
[46]. Once seizures begin, massive Na⁺/K+ dysregulation and glutamate
buildup reduce the capacity of astrocytes to restore homeostasis, consistent
with the delayed stabilization in the model [47].
The simulation results in Figure 9 show that reduced function of Kir4.1
channels enhances the inhibitory effect of astrocyte OS on reducing seizures.
Conflicting evidence exists regarding the contribution of astrocytic Kir4.1
channels versus Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase to seizure suppression, especially when
astrocytes are optogenetically stimulated. Some studies and computational
models report that both a reduction and an increase in Kir4.1 activity can
trigger seizure-like activity, but with distinct dynamics. Specifically, one
modeling study found that increasing Kir4.1 conductance leads to high-
frequency neuronal firing, while decreasing Kir4.1 induces abnormal firing at
                        ACCEPTED
                          ARTICLEMANUSCRIPT
                                  IN PRESS



lower frequency, highlighting a nonlinear, dose-sensitive relationship
between Kir4.1 and excitability [15].
Conversely, recent experimental evidence suggests that the seizure-
attenuating effects of optogenetic astrocyte stimulation are critically
dependent on astrocytic Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase activity, with Kir4.1 playing only a
permissive or secondary role. For example, the authors of [6] showed that
optogenetically activating astrocytic ChR2 suppressed cortical seizures
through upregulating the Na⁺-K⁺ pump, and that genetic or pharmacological
interference with Kir4.1 did not abolish the protective effect. This supports a
paradigm in which astrocytic K⁺ uptake and redistribution (traditionally
attributed to Kir4.1) is auxiliary to the robust, activity-driven pumping
capacity of Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase for controlling hyperexcitability during seizures.
Importantly, loss-of-function mutations or knockdown of Kir4.1 channels are
well-established to facilitate epileptogenesis, both in human syndromes and
rodent models. However, the anti-seizure potential of OS appears to persist
even when Kir4.1 is impaired, as long as NKA function is intact [48, 49].


                                                S S
Our results in Figure 10 indicate that the combined stimulation of astrocytes


                                             R E
(via ChR2) and inhibition of neurons (via ArchT) is more effective than
individual application. Ledri et al. demonstrated that optogenetic activation
                                            P
of specific neuronal populations reduces seizure-like events and that

                                      IN
astrocytic modulation via ChR2 influences extracellular K+ dynamics, directly

                            E
affecting excitability and seizure propagation [50]. Similarly, studies have

                          L
shown that ArchT-mediated neuronal inhibition decreases neuronal spiking
                        C
                       I
and network excitability during seizures, while ChR2 stimulation in


                    RT
astrocytes facilitates K+ clearance [51], consistent with our model’s
observation of decreased extracellular potassium (Ko) and reduced neuron
                  A
spiking under the combined stimulation condition.
Similar studies emphasize the role of astrocytes in modulating neuronal
activity. For example, research has shown that optogenetic manipulation of
astrocytes can affect seizure dynamics and neuronal excitability through
various mechanisms. A study by Meyer [52] has shown that astrocytic calcium
signaling can affect neuronal excitability and synaptic transmission.
However, the findings of this paper suggest that sodium-potassium pump
activity may play a more important role in this context. The strengths of this
paper lie in its innovative approach to investigating astrocyte functions and
the use of optogenetics to precisely temporally control cellular activity. This
approach provides a clearer understanding of the dynamic interactions
between glial cells and neurons during pathological states such as seizures.
Nonetheless, a potential drawback is the narrow emphasis on particular
signaling pathways. Investigating additional mechanisms might offer a more
thorough understanding of the roles of astrocytes in neuronal excitability.

                           ARTICLEMANUSCRIPT
                                   IN PRESS



This study has inherent limitations. The model is not designed to simulate
ictogenesis—the network-level processes of seizure initiation and
propagation. It cannot address the roles of population dynamics, inhibitory
interneuron dysfunction, or spatial spread in epilepsy. Its findings are
explicitly contextualized within the cellular-scale phenomena of the ictal state
and do not explain emergent network-level seizure dynamics.
In this study, we focused on the interaction between a neuron and an
astrocyte while excluding the effects of chemical synaptic connections in
neurons and gap junction channels in astrocytes. Subsequently, a model that
includes N pairs of neuron-astrocyte circuits along with random connections
among neurons, as well as connections among astrocytes can be presented.
This approach is necessary because epileptic seizures are a population
phenomenon that cannot be accurately simulated using a two-cell model.
This model ignored GABAergic interneurons, which are critically important
in epilepsy. Furthermore, the pyramidal neuron was represented as a single
compartment; future iterations could model the soma and dendrites

                                                 S S
separately, incorporate detailed ionic currents, and add interneurons.


                                              R E
The geometric structure of dendritic and axonal structures, the spatial

                                             P
distribution of channels and transmitters, and the presence of immobilized


                                      IN
anions that are critically needed to maintain electrical and osmotic balance
were ignored. Also, the astrocyte in this model includes calcium, IP3, sodium,

                           L E
potassium, and the sodium-potassium pump; while nitric oxide can also affect

                         C
astrocytes and, consequently, epilepsy.

                      T I
Taken together, this study suggests that astrocytes play a critical role in

                   A R
regulating neuronal activity and controlling seizures. The findings of this
study can serve as a basis for future research in the development of novel
and targeted therapies (such as optogenetics) in neurological disorders,
especially epilepsy, and deepen our understanding of the complex
interactions between neurons and astrocytes.

4.3 An index for Seizure
                 A
Experimental studies typically assess seizure severity through behavioral
scoring. In our model, severity is quantified using the average power spectral
density (PSD) of VN in the 80–400 Hz band (denoted as ASE80-400), a validated
metric for seizure detection when behavioral data are unavailable [56, 57].
High-frequency power (gamma to fast ripples) correlates strongly with ictal
states and severity, including status epilepticus [58]. This band captures
pathophysiological markers (ripples: 80–250 Hz; fast ripples: >250 Hz) linked
to seizure onset and progression. PSD-based methods are widely adopted in
both experimental and computational epilepsy research.
