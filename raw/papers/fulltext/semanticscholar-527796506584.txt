Abstract
Purpose: Cerebellar functional connectivity changes have been reported in Alzheimer’s disease (AD), but
a comprehensive framework integrating these findings is lacking. This retrospective study investigates
the cerebello-thalamo-cortical (CTC) circuit in AD, using functional gradient analysis to elucidate deficits
and potential biomarkers.

Method:246 participants from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) (58 AD, 103 mild
cognitive impairment [MCI], 85 normal cognitions [CN]) underwent 3T MRI. The CTC circuitry was
constructed using T1 weighted imaging and resting-state functional MRI. Voxel-based gradient values
were analyzed globally, in subfields, and cerebellar functional networks. Cognitive scores (MMSE, MoCA,
ADAS-Cog) were assessed at each ADNI site. Statistical analyses included ANCOVA, post hoc analyses,
Pearson correlations, LASSO regression, logistic regression, and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests. False
discovery rate (FDR) corrections were used, setting the statistical significance threshold was set at P <
0.05.

Results: AD and MCI individuals exhibited increased CTC connectivity compared to CN (all P < 0.05).
Average CTC connectivity did not correlate with cognitive scores (P > 0.05), but specific CTC edges were
correlated. LASSO regression identified 20 discriminative edges, achieving high accuracy in AD-CN
classification (AUC = 0.92 training, AUC = 0.80 test). Thalamic and cerebellar gradient distributions
differed significantly across groups (all P < 0.05), with specific regions showing distinct gradient scores.
Five cerebellar functional networks exhibited decreased gradient scores.

Conclusions: This study provides preliminary evidence supporting CTC hyperconnectivity as a potential
diagnostic biomarker for AD. It suggests the involvement of the thalamus and cerebellum in cognitive
impairment in individuals with AD.


Introduction
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is widely recognized as the leading cause of dementia, a syndrome marked by
cognitive decline and concomitant behavioral and personality alterations [1-3]. While the cerebellum is
the final brain region to become involved in the pathologic progression of AD, as evidenced by amyloid-β
peptide (Aβ) neuropathological deposit [4, 5], functional MRI studies have indicated alterations in
cerebellar activity among AD patients, influencing not only motor coordination but also cognitive
functions [6, 7]. The cerebellum’s involvement in processing various cognitive functions such as working
memory [8],emotion [9], language [10], and other executive functions [11] is likely attributable to its
extensive connections with cortical regions [12]. Additionally, alterations in functional connectivity (FC)
are evident in the preclinical stages of AD [13-15]. The thalamus is a subcortical structure closely linked
to the cerebellum, and their interactions play crucial roles in cognitive functions. Connected by a neural
fibers network known as the cerebello-thalamic pathway, information processed by the cerebellum is
relayed to the cerebral cortex from the thalamus. The accumulation of Aβ plaques and Tau tangles can


                                                  Page 3/23
impact the thalamus, potentially resulting in functional changes [16, 17]. Disruptions in the FC between
the thalamus and other brain regions involved in memory and cognition have been reported in AD [18].

Nevertheless, the patterns of AD-related changes in brain function are not entirely uniform across
studies. This variability may, in part, arise from the fact that each study examined specific brain systems
based on task-specific features, thereby lacking a unified paradigm framework as an imaging biomarker
for the AD spectrum. The anatomical resemblance between atrophy patterns in neurodegenerative
conditions and intrinsic connectivity networks in healthy individuals offered the initial empirical proof in
living human subjects that neurodegenerative diseases structured breakdowns of large-scale networks,
with neuropathology spreading via trans-synaptic connections [19, 20]. From a neurobiological
standpoint, recent research has demonstrated that the brain possesses an intrinsic and state-
independent functional architecture. Functional networks across different paradigms are primarily
shaped by this “standard” architecture, with paradigm-specific characteristics playing a secondary role
[21-23]. The cerebello-thalamo-cortical (CTC) circuitry represents a complex neural network that
integrates both motor and cognitive functions. Although traditionally linked with motor coordination,
recent research has broadened our comprehension of its involvement in higher cognitive processes [24,
25]. Numerous investigations have revealed that the CTC circuitry likely constitutes an intrinsic and
state-independent neural characteristic relevant to various neuropsychiatric conditions, encompassing
Parkinson’s disease [26], essential tremor [27], Tourette’s syndrome [28], schizophrenia [29], obsessive-
compulsive disorder [30], and autism spectrum disorders [31]. While AD predominantly impacts cognitive
function and memory, certain symptoms may overlap with those seen in neuropsychiatric disorders.
Consequently, investigating alterations in the CTC circuitry in AD is imperative for advancing our
comprehension of the disease, creating diagnostic and therapeutic resources, and enhancing patient
care and outcomes.

In addition to examining large-scale networks, we present a novel gradient-based approach for the
nonlinear decomposition of high-dimensional resting-state FC [32]. Diverging from regional analysis, this
method enables a thorough identification of the functional hierarchy of the thalamus and cerebellum by
representing brain connections in a continuous low-dimensional space [33, 34]. Gradients provide a
holistic view by capturing global connectivity patterns, facilitating the identification of thalamic and
cerebellar connectivity hubs—regions crucial for integrating information across the entire brain.

To achieve these objectives, we collected 246 participants encompassing a diverse cohort, including AD
patients, individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), along with normal cognition (CN) subjects.
Through an analysis of the CTC circuitry within this cohort, we aimed to ascertain whether dysfunction
within the CTC circuitry is evident in the AD spectrum. Furthermore, we delved into the pattern of
alterations in the principal connectome gradient within the thalamus and cerebellum, two pivotal brain
regions in the CTC circuitry.


Patients and Methods

                                                   Page 4/23
Participants

We included 246 participants from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) phase 3
(ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT02854033), selecting them based on the availability of T1-weighted imaging
(T1WI) and resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI). Among these participants, there were 58 individuals
diagnosed with AD, 103 with MCI, and 85 individuals with CN who were matched in terms of age and sex.
Additionally, all included participants underwent comprehensive neuropsychological assessments,
encompassing the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA),
and the Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Scale Cognitive subscale (ADAS-Cog). All assessments were
conducted during the same study visit, and a summary of demographic characteristics is provided in
Table 1. Ethical approval was obtained by the investigators at each participating ADNI site, and all
participants provided written informed consent.

Data acquisition

In ADNI, all MRI data were acquired using 3T scanners and followed standardized scanning protocols
(detailed parameters can be found at https://adni.loni.usc.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/ADNI3-
MRI-protocols.pdf). T1WI were acquired using a 3D T1-weighted MPRAGE sequence, with an isotropic
voxel size of 1 mm and a repetition time (TR) of 2300 ms. Rs-fMRI consisted of 200 volumes per subject,
acquired using a 3D EPI sequence with an isotropic voxel size of 3.4 mm, and a TR/echo time/flip angle
= 3000/30/90°.

Rs-fMRI preprocessing

Before processing the rs-fMRI, a recon-all needed to be executed on the T1WI using FreeSurfer software
(version 6.0, https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/) to segment the cortical structures. This segmentation
served as a template for the subsequent registration of rs-fMRI.

The Computational Brain Imaging Group (https://github.com/ThomasYeoLab/CBIG) toolbox was used to
preprocess the rs-fMRI data, which involved the following steps: slice time correction, motion correction
utilizing framewise displacement (FDrms) and voxel-wise differentiated signal variance (DVARS) [35, 36],
and removing frames based on thresholds (FDrms >0.2 or DVARS >50), spatial distortion correction,
nuisance regression, temporal interpolation of censored frames, bandpass filtering in the frequency
range of 0.009–0.08 Hz, projection onto a standard surface (fsaverage 5), and smoothing with a 6 mm
kernel size.

CTC circuitry construction

After preprocessing, we extracted the mean time series from each of the 270 nodes in the extended
Power Atlas, following the procedures detailed in previous studies [29, 37]. Next, we constructed a 270 ×
270 pairwise whole-brain connectivity matrix for each participant by computing the Pearson correlation
coefficient between the extracted time series. From these connectivity matrices, we extracted the


                                                  Page 5/23
connectivity strength of the previously reported CTC circuitry, which comprised 84 edges, predominantly
located in the thalamus and cerebellum [24, 29]. A detailed list of these edges can be found in Table S1.

Gradient mapping of thalamus and cerebellum

The thalamo-cortical and cerebello-cortical FC matrices were constructed by applying Pearson’s
correlation to the time courses of each voxel. The preprocessed images were voxel-normalized to
dimensions of 2×2×2 mm3. Tian’s subcortical atlas defined the spatial extent of the thalamus, and the
Anatomical Automatic Labeling (AAL) atlas was used for the cerebellum. These yielded a thalamus
comprising 2509 voxels at 2 mm cubic resolution and a cerebellum comprising 24234 voxels, also at 2
mm cubic resolution. Cortical gray matter was defined using Schaefer’s parcellation. A correlation matrix
for each participant was constructed using these templates to characterize the thalamic and cerebellar
functional connectome.

Principal gradients, explaining primary variability in the spatial distribution of thalamic and cerebellar
voxel-wise functional connectivity, were calculated using the BrainSpace Toolbox
(http://github.com/MICA-MNI/BrainSpace) [32]. Six main parameters, including (1) sparsity (90), (2)
kernel (normalized angle), (3) approach (diffusion embedding), (4) alpha parameter (0.5), (5) alignment
(Procrustes analysis), and (6) number of components (10), were inputted into the toolbox. We computed
a normalized angle based on the aforementioned thalamic and cerebellar functional connectome, which
scales the angle between each pair of voxels according to their similarity. The resulting normalized angle
matrix was then input into the diffusion map embedding algorithm. In comparison to other non-linear
manifold learning techniques, this algorithm demonstrates relative insensitivity to noise and
computational efficiency. Notably, the algorithm is governed by a single parameter α, determining the
influence of sampling point density on the manifold (α = 0, maximal influence; α = 1, no influence). In the
embedding space, strongly interconnected brain regions are defined by either numerous connections or
a few very strong connections, whereas regions with limited and/or weak interconnectivity are more
distant. To account for the randomness in the direction of yielding gradients, we employed an average
connectivity matrix derived from all patients and controls to generate a group-level gradient component
template.

Extraction of principal gradient scores at global-, subfield- and functional-level

The default setting generated the initial ten gradient components. Considering that the principal gradient
is believed to encapsulate the most explained variance and has been extensively applied in previous
studies, our primary focused was on the measures within the first gradient component [34, 38]. We
generated spatial patterns of group-averaged principal gradient maps for the first gradient component to
delineate the organizational differences in thalamic and cerebellar hierarchies. Additionally, thalamo- and
cerebello-level gradient scores for each group were characterized. The gradient scores delineated the
scales of the group’s spatial distribution, with each voxel anchored in an abstract space based on its
gradient score.

                                                   Page 6/23
Subsequent analyses at the subfield level aimed to identify the alterations in thalamic and cerebellar
subfields. Tian’s atlas for the thalamus, which bilaterally segments the anterior and posterior thalamus
regions, and the AAL atlas, parcellating the cerebellum into 26 neuroanatomical regions, were employed.

Recognizing the cerebellum’s growing significance in cognitive activities, and in alignment with the
recently proposed Nettekoven2023 atlas of cerebellar functional partitioning [39]. The cerebellum was
segmented into four domains and sixteen functional regions: motor (eye, mouth, hand & upper, foot &
lower), action (spatial simulation, action observation, motor imagery, social actions), demand (spatial
WM, retrieval, verbal WM, spatial/N-back), and sociolinguistic (linguistic, social, rest, self-projection).

Statistical analysis

Group comparison of CTC circuitry, estimation of classification accuracy, and correlations between
altered links and clinical variables. To ascertain the presence of abnormal CTC connectivity within the AD
spectrum, we initially conducted an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA). Mean CTC connectivity across 84
edges was used as the dependent variable, while the independent variable was the participant’s group
(comprising AD, MCI, and CN), with age and sex as covariates. In cases of significant differences, post
hoc analyses employing the least significant difference (LSD) method were conducted to pinpoint the
specific groups that exhibited significant distinctions.

Subsequently, we aimed to determine whether the connectivity within the CTC circuitry could effectively
differentiate between AD patients and individuals with CN at the individual level. For this purpose, we
trained a Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression model using the 84
connectivity measures extracted from the AD-CN datasets. The LASSO regression is a L1-norm
regularization technique that introduces a shrinkage penalty term (λ) to prevent model overfitting,
compelling some predictor coefficients to shrink to zero [40]. In this procedure, 70% of the subjects from
the AD-CN datasets were randomly assigned to the training cohort, while the remaining 30% constituted
the test cohort. Initially, all 84 predictors incorporated into the model underwent adjustment for sex and
age using a linear model within the training cohort. The tuning parameter λ was optimized through a
repeated 5-fold cross-validation (CV) method, and subsequently applied to predict the remaining
subjects within the cycles. We selected the smallest mean-squared error to determine the coefficients of
the predictors. Predictors with non-zero coefficients were considered important features and were
retained. Subsequently, we constructed a logistic regression model within the training set using the
selected predictors. This procedure ultimately generated predicted probabilities of illness for each
individual within the training cohort, from which we calculated the classification accuracy, negative
predictive value, positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity. We assessed the generalizability of
the classification performance using the test cohort.

Additionally, Pearson correlations were conducted to explore associations between the mean CTC
connectivity, each edge within the CTC circuitry, and scores on MMSE, MoCA, and ADAS-Cog.



                                                   Page 7/23
Group comparisons of functional connectome gradients. To characterize the hierarchical organization of
the thalamus and cerebellum concerning CN, MCI, and AD, we compared the global-level gradient
distributions of these structures between group-averaged maps using two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov
(K-S) tests. We examined cumulative distributions, and initial comparisons of global scores in CN and AD
aimed to identify illness effects in patients. Subsequent analyses assessed the preclinical stage impact
by comparing CN and AD to MCI, respectively. Following this, subfield- and functional-level gradients
were separately extracted for each subject in an ANCOVA, with age and sex as covariates. Post hoc
analyses employing the LSD method, were then conducted to pinpoint the specific groups exhibiting
significant differences.

To correct for multiple comparisons, we used False discovery rate (FDR) corrections, setting the
statistical significance threshold was set at P < 0.05 with FDR correction.


Results
CTC connectivity in Alzheimer’s disease spectrum

Group comparison of CTC circuitry. As anticipated, the ANCOVA revealed significant differences among
the three groups of subjects (F = 4.36, P = 0.01). Subsequent post hoc analysis revealed that patients
with AD exhibited significantly higher mean connectivity within the CTC network in comparison to
individuals with CN (P = 0.03, Fig 1a). Moreover, the phenomenon of CTC hyperconnectivity was not only
limited to the AD group; it was also evident in the MCI group in comparison to CN (P = 0.01, Fig 1a).
Whereas there was no significant difference in the mean CTC connectivity between the AD and MCI
groups (P = 0.89).

Classification accuracy of CTC connectivity for Alzheimer’s disease. In the AD-CN dataset, a total of 20
edges within the CTC circuitry exhibited non-zero coefficients in CV LASSO regression. Consequently,
these 20 edges were chosen as the final features for further analysis. These selected edges
predominantly centered around the cerebellum and thalamus, encompassing connections between
these two structures and the DMN, the sensorimotor cortex, visual and auditory cortex. Further details
regarding these 20 edges are provided in Fig 1c and Supplementary Table S2. The AUC for the
classification ROC in both training and test cohorts was 0.92 (95%CI: 0.87-0.97) and 0.80 (95%CI: 0.66-
0.94), respectively (Fig 1b). In the training and test cohorts, the negative predictive values were 0.85 and
0.76, the positive predictive values were 0.90 and 0.75, the sensitivities were 0.90 and 0.82, and the
specificities were 0.85 and 0.68. This suggests that trained classification model exhibited robust
generalizability.

Correlations between altered links and clinical variables. Notably, there were no discernible correlations
between the average CTC connectivity and scores on the MMSE (R = -0.06, P = 0.39), MoCA (R = 0.02, P
= 0.81), or ADAS-Cog (R = 0.01, P = 0.96). This observation supports the notion that CTC
hyperconnectivity represents a characteristic functional anomaly with the AD spectrum, independent of
symptom severity measures. Nevertheless, several edges within the CTC circuitry exhibited correlations
                                                  Page 8/23
with MMSE scores. These included connections between the right middle temporal gyrus and the left
thalamus (R = 0.13, PFDR = 0.046), the left precuneus and the right thalamus (R = -0.12, PFDR = 0.06), and
the right medial superior frontal gyrus and the left cerebellum (R = 0.15, PFDR = 0.02, Fig 2a).
Furthermore, the MoCA scores exhibited correlations with specific edges, involving the bilateral middle
temporal gyrus and the left thalamus (R = -0.14 for the left/0.12 for the right, PFDR = 0.02 for the
left/0.045 for the right, respectively, Fig 2b). Additionally, the ADAS-Cog scores displayed correlations
with a specific edge, involving the right angular gyrus and the left cerebellum (R = -0.12, PFDR = 0.049, Fig
2c). These identified brain regions were situated within the default mode network (DMN), thalamus and
cerebellum. This suggests that the mental status of individuals within AD spectrum is intricately linked to
altered functional activity within this specific sub-scale network.

Principal functional gradient alterations in AD, MCI, and CN

Global-level gradient distribution of thalamus and cerebellum. AD patient displayed a broader range of
global principal thalamic and cerebellar gradient scores compared to CN (all K-S stat = 0.16, all P <
0.001, Fig 3c). Both the highest and lowest gradient scores expanded in AD patients compared to CN,
revealing excessive differentiation of CTC circuitry in the pathophysiology of AD. Fig 3a and 3b depict the
spatial distribution of gradients in the thalamus and cerebellum for CN, MCI, and AD, respectively.
Additionally, the gradient distribution of the MCI differed from both CN and AD in both the thalamus (K-S
stat = 0.07/0.17 between MCI and CN, MCI and AD, respectively, all P < 0.05) and cerebellum (K-S stat =
0.06/0.11 between MCI and CN, MCI and AD, respectively, all P < 0.05).

Subfield-level gradient of thalamus and cerebellum. Within neuroanatomical systems, AD exhibited
higher gradient scores in the left anterior thalamus than MCI (PFDR = 0.04). Additionally, when compared
to both CN and MCI, AD exhibited higher gradient scores in the left inferior cerebellum (PFDR = 0.04). The
gradient scores in the vermis of the cerebellum were lower in MCI than CN (PFDR = 0.046). These
discrepancies in subfields primarily contributed to the global excessive differentiation (Fig 4a).

Cerebellar functional network gradient. Five out of the 16 cerebellar functional networks exhibited
significant differences, including mouth, hander & upper, motor imagery, retrieval, and spatial/N-back.
Among them, mouth gradient scores in the AD group were significantly lower than those of CN (PFDR =
0.03). Compared to CN, both the AD and MCI groups demonstrated lower hand & upper gradient scores
(PFDR = 0.046 for AD and CN, PFDR = 0.003 for MCI and CN). The motor imagery gradient scores in AD
were lower than those in both MCI and CN (PFDR = 0.02 for AD and MCI, PFDR = 0.04 for AD and CN). The
retrieval gradient scores in both AD and MCI were lower compared with CN (all PFDR = 0.02).The
spatial/N-back gradient scores in both the AD and MCI groups were lower than those in CN group (PFDR =
0.003, for AD and CN, PFDR = 0.01, for MCI and CN). Fig 4b illustrates the gradient distribution of the
cerebellar functional networks.


Discussion
                                                  Page 9/23
While prior research has concentrated on the hypothesis of multiple target regions within the neocortex,
thalamus, and cerebellum, the absence of direct evidence amalgamating these discrete observations
into a unified framework persists. Our study indicates that CTC hyperconnectivity represents a “trait”
alteration that can be consistently detected in both AD and MCI patients. These findings suggest that a
state-independent functional neural signature precedes the development of AD and can reasonably
differentiate individuals with AD from those without it, with good generalizability. Utilizing the innovative
functional connectome gradient algorithm, our investigation additionally revealed a wider gradient
distribution in the thalamus and cerebellum of individuals with AD. Furthermore, at the subfield level, our
observations indicated elevated gradient scores in the left anterior thalamus for individuals with AD,
while the gradient scores of the left inferior and vermis cerebellum exhibited a positive shift. Additionally,
within cerebellar functional networks, gradient scores in regions associated with tongue, hand and upper
movements, motor imagery, retrieval ability, spatial tasks and working memory exhibited a collective
decrease.

One of the key findings in this study is the presence of CTC hyperconnectivity in patients with AD
spectrum. This hyperconnectivity within the CTC network is largely in line with the “glutamate
dysfunction” hypothesis of AD [41]. Recent research suggests that Aβ triggers elevated cytosolic
calcium levels, potentially contributing to mitochondrial calcium dyshomeostasis and subsequent
neuronal damage, particularly by activating N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors (NMDARs) in the early
stages of AD [42, 43]. In particular, Aβ engages α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, triggering the
release of astrocytic glutamate. This released glutamate subsequently activates extrasynaptic NMDARs,
resulting nitric oxide production, Tau phosphorylation, caspase-3 activation, and ultimately the disruption
of synaptic plasticity, long-term potentiation, and synaptic loss [44]. Among these mechanisms,
glutamate, the primary excitatory neurotransmitter in the brain, can enhance the likelihood of
postsynaptic neuron excitation, consequently contributing to exaggerated functional connectivity in
patients [45]. Meanwhile, it has been demonstrated that soluble Aβ oligomers can diminish neuronal
glutamate reuptake by influencing glutamate transporters [46], thereby maintaining neurons in an
excitatory state. Furthermore, while AD has been impairment to the impairment of cholinergic terminals,
followed by glutamatergic terminals, it ultimately results in gamma-aminobutyric acidergic (GABAergic)
terminal dysfunction [47]. GABA serves as the principal inhibitory neurotransmitter in the brain.
Downregulation of the function of cortical GABAergic interneurons can result in diminished recurrent
inhibition of pyramidal glutamatergic neurons, consequently contributing to increased functional
connectivity [45]. Consequently, the hyperconnectivity observed in the CTC circuitry in our study may
signify the presence of an Aβ-mediated hyper-glutamatergic state and GABAergic neuronal dysfunction,
which precedes the onset of AD and persists throughout the full disease state.

An alternative interpretation of these findings is linked to the “cognitive dysmetria” theory, which pertains
to the heightened cognitive effort required in AD patients. Several studies have highlighted the
cerebellum’s role as the central hub for “error detection and correction” within the brain. It receives,
integrates, and processes error-related information regarding movements and thoughts from the
cerebral cortex, offering adaptive feedback through the CTC circuitry [48-50]. Consequently, the
                                                  Page 10/23
hyperconnectivity of the CTC circuitry likely reflects a compensatory mechanism triggered by an excess
of erroneous inputs from the upstream cerebral cortex. This implies that individuals with AD spectrum
may need to exert greater cognitive effort to rectify errors and align their behaviors and thoughts.

Meanwhile, at the global level, the expanded gradient range signified a more diverse connectivity pattern
in the thalamus and cerebellum, suggesting a disturbance in functional integration during AD. This could
arise from abnormal connectivity patterns, potentially enhancing the heterogeneity of thalamo-cortical
and cerebello-cortical pathways. This observation aligns with the findings in the large-scale network,
indicating a dissociation and imbalance between excitatory and inhibitory functions in AD patients. In
our investigation, AD patients exhibited higher gradient scores in the thalamic subfield. We posit that this
elevation does not signify an enhanced capacity for integrating functions but rather stems from the
thalamus receiving redundant information input from the cerebellum.

In addition, this study also uncovered that CTC hyperconnectivity can serve as an individualized predictor
for AD classification. It is worth noting that many past studies relied on purely data-driven analyses of
participants in a single state. As a result, clinical confounders and state-related variables could
potentially confound the interpretation of these predictors, making it challenging to discern whether they
truly represent the underlying features of AD pathogenesis. Distinguishing whether these predictors were
a result of state-dependent disease changes or merely incidental phenomena would pose a significant
challenge. In contrast, this study employed a priori biomarkers. Subsequently, we explored the clinical
significance of these predictors for disease classification, ensuring that they were biologically
meaningful in the context of AD. This approach resulted in a high classification accuracy for AD from CN
individuals.

LASSO-regression feature selection unveiled that the most discriminative connections within the CTC
circuitry included links between the cerebellum, thalamus, DMN, sensory cortices, and the visual-auditory
cortex. These findings align with prior research that has indicated the potential for high classification
accuracy in structural or functional imaging of these regions. They underscore that these connectivity
alterations represent a promising and reliable candidate for a useful signature in the context of AD [6, 7,
14, 15, 20, 51-55]. Notably, several nodes were situated in the DMN, whose impaired connectivity had
been consistently identified as a robust biomarker of cognitive disorder. A substantial body of research
has highlighted the DMN’s susceptibility to various AD pathophysiological processes, observed not only
in AD patients but also in asymptomatic mutation carriers and cognitively normal individuals with
genetic risk for AD [56-62]. Our findings of dysfunction between the cerebellum, thalamus, and DMN
within the AD spectrum contributes additional evidence to support the crucial role of the DMN. In fact,
these connections may be linked to sensory gating deficits and impairments in cognitive control related
to error and conflict monitoring, resulting in perceptual disturbances and “cognitive dysmetria” in
patients [63, 64]. For the cerebellum, our finding of reduced gradient scores in cerebellum involved in
memory, executive, and spatial tasks adds further evidence of cerebellar involvement in cognitive
impairment in AD patients.


                                                 Page 11/23
We would like to note some limitations of this study. First, our study encompassed individuals with MCI
and AD. However, the study design was cross-sectional, and longitudinal follow-up data were absent.
Consequently, the observations are influenced to some extent by subject heterogeneity. Future
longitudinal studies are anticipated to enhance the current findings. Second, the sample sizes for AD
cohort were relatively small in our study. Therefore, we regard these results as preliminary and advise
cautious interpretation for clinical application. Third, due to the limited sample sizes, we employed linear
models in the study to maximize the utilization of available data and boost statistical power. However, it
is important to recognize that symptoms or disease progression may not follow a linear pattern in
practice. Therefore, in future studies with larger participant numbers, the implementation of nonlinear
models may further improve the diagnostic capabilities of the CTC circuitry for the AD spectrum
disorders. Finally, the current choice of the CTC circuitry atlas for the AD spectrum is grounded in
previous research. However, we anticipate that future studies, involving larger samples sizes, will refine
and enhance the atlas, ultimately improving its classification accuracy.


Conclusion
Using a diverse cohort representing the Alzheimer’s continuum, our study has showcased the clinical
significance of CTC hyperconnectivity as a state-independent neural trait for the prediction and
characterization of AD within the context of diagnostic classification. This holds the potential to
contribute to the advancement of a more unified framework for comprehending the neuropathological
aspects of AD. Moreover, our investigation introduces novel evidence and biomarkers supporting the
involvement of the thalamus and cerebellum in cognitive impairment in individuals with Alzheimer’s
disease.
