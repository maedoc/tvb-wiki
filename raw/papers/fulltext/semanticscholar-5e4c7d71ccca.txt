29    1       Introduction

30    Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disease characterized by cognitive decline,
31    loss of memory, and impaired reasoning skills (Safiri et al., 2024). The pathological hallmarks of AD are
32    widely recognized to include the accumulation of amyloid-β and tau peptide (Y. Chen & Yu, 2023; M. P.
33    Murphy & LeVine, 2010). An estimated 6.5 million Americans are currently living with AD, imposing
34    substantial economic burdens that amount to hundreds of billions of dollars annually. Existing treatments,
35    such as acetylcholinesterase inhibitors, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory compounds, sex steroid hormones,
36    and antioxidants (Allain et al., 2007), demonstrate limited efficacy in altering the progression of AD and
37    mild cognitive impairment (MCI). Moreover, AD symptoms typically manifest many years after the initial
38    onset of pathological changes, meaning that therapeutic interventions often occur too late to be fully
39    effective (Thangwaritorn et al., 2024). Consequently, there has been significant research interest in
40    developing effective interventions during early-life stages, before clinical symptoms manifest (Gandy et
41    al., 2017; Seifan et al., 2015). Early interventions well before the onset of noticeable cognitive symptoms
42    can be essential for altering disease outcomes timely.

43    The stages of AD progression include the preclinical, prodromal, and patent dementia periods (Vermunt
44    et al., 2019). The preclinical AD stage can begin as early as midlife and span decades without overt
45    symptoms, despite accumulating pathological features such as amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles
46    in the brain (Thal et al., 2013). The prodromal stage involves MCI, where measurable deficits are present
47    but daily independence is maintained (Albert et al., 2011), followed by dementia, characterized by severe
48    impairments in cognition and everyday functioning. The long, symptom-free preclinical stage (averaging
49    10 years) poses a major challenge to early detection. Most studies attempting to capture this stage must

50    enroll large cohorts of cognitively normal individuals, of whom only about 10% will eventually develop AD
51    (Bennett et al., 2018; Dhana et al., 2023; Petersen et al., 2010). This makes such research inefficient
52    and resource-intensive. As a result, there is growing interest in using biomarkers to more precisely identify
53    individuals at risk during these early stages (Millar et al., 2022; Risacher & Saykin, 2013).

54    Among these biomarkers, neuroimaging including functional MRI (fMRI) has shown great promise to
55    reveal altered activation patterns in memory-related brain regions among individuals at risk (Noh et al.,
56    2023). Functional connectivity (FC), an fMRI-based imaging feature derived to characterize network-level
57    temporal synchronization of activity, has emerged as a particularly powerful neuromarker. FC consistently
58    reveals network-level disruptions during the transition from cognitively normal aging to MCI (Penalba-
59    Sánchez et al., 2022), and has demonstrated strong predictive accuracy in identifying individuals likely to
60    progress from prodromal AD to dementia (Noh et al., 2023). Beyond AD, FC serves as a robust individual
61    fingerprint, capturing both normative traits such as age (Hu et al., 2019), sex (İçer et al., 2020), race
62    (Orlichenko et al., 2023), and cognitive ability (Qu et al., 2024), as well as deviations seen in disorders like
63    ADHD (Castellanos & Aoki, 2016), schizophrenia (Collin et al., 2020), and ASD (Sung & Park, 2024).
64    Given the sensitivity of FC to both normal variation and disease states, and its quantifiable progression
65    across AD stages, we propose using FC as a bridge to link early-life behaviors and physiological traits to
66    later AD risk. Critically, most current AD studies focus on older adults who are already on the disease
67    trajectory (Bennett et al., 2018; Petersen et al., 2010), limiting our understanding of how modifiable
68    early-life factors shape long-term vulnerability.

69    In this study, we leverage FC as an intermediate neurobiological representation to bridge the potential
70    causal pathway from early-life behaviors and physiological measurements to late-life AD risk. By rigorously
71    modeling how modifiable factors shape FC and how FC influences AD vulnerability across studies over
72    lifespan, we aim to elucidate causal mechanisms by which early interventions may alter long-term disease
73    outcomes. As illustrated in Figure 1, we develop a behavior- and lifestyle factor-conditioned variational
74    autoencoder (VAE) to model how modifiable factors influence FC and, in turn, AD risk. In the current
75    literature, such kind of analytical objective has mainly achieved by Mendelian randomization (MR) as
76    discussed in Section 2, which tends to be constrained by the availability of strong genetic instrumental
77    variables for diverse behaviors or lifestyle factors (Korologou-Linden et al., 2022; Sanderson et al., 2022),
78    known as the weak IV problem (Andrews et al., 2019). This limitation has hindered efforts to establish
79    causal links between early-life exposures and late-life clinical outcomes in general. In contrast, behaviors
80    and lifestyle factors are strongly correlated with FC endophenotype, and a growing body of evidence
81    supports the robust prediction of FC for AD. Our approach integrates these insights by modeling how
82    behavioral interventions affect FC and subsequently influence AD risk. Specifically, we train a VAE to
83    generate FC conditioned on behavioral inputs, then use an AD prediction model to quantify changes in

100    2       Related Work

101    Integrating early-life behaviors with disease risks that manifest only in old age presents a significant
102    challenge, though it has been explored in prior work. One widely used method is MR, which uses genetic
103    variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to proxy for exposures, offering an alternative to randomized
104    controlled trials (Sanderson et al., 2022). MR has been used in numerous applications, including AD
105    (Korologou-Linden et al., 2022; Su et al., 2023), schizophrenia (Veeneman et al., 2022), and cancer
106    (Wade et al., 2022). For example, (European Alzheimer’s & Dementia Biobank Mendelian Randomization
107    (EADB-MR) Collaboration et al., 2023) employed MR to assess the influence of 12 modifiable lifestyle
108    factors such as lipid levels, cholesterol, alcohol consumption, and smoking on AD risk. Most of these
109    factors did not show statistically significant associations with AD risk, except for High Density Lipoprotein
110    and ApoE genotype. We attribute this, in part, to the weak instrument problem: it is often difficult
111    to identify genetic variants that are strongly associated with complex behaviors like smoking (Andrews
112    et al., 2019). Additionally, issues such as pleiotropy and violations of the gene-environment equivalence
113    assumption further limit the reliability of MR for certain lifestyle exposures (Sanderson et al., 2022;
114    VanderWeele et al., 2014). In contrast, our current study focuses only on behavioral and lifestyle factors
115    that show significant correlations with brain FC (see Supplementary Materials). Behaviors that do not
116    explain variance in FC are effectively neutralized during inference, with their influence on downstream
117    outcomes averaging to near zero.

118    Another important line of related research involves mediation analysis, which typically decomposes the
119    impact from an exposure to an outcome into direct and indirect components via a mediator (Rijnhart et
120    al., 2021). Previous studies have used FC between specific brain regions as mediators for cognitive ability
121    in MCI (Huang et al., 2024). More recently, high-dimensional mediation analysis techniques, including
122    those based on Bayesian methods and deep learning, have enabled the use of entire FC networks as
123    mediators (Nath et al., 2023; Tian et al., 2024). Some studies have also examined how FC mediates
124    the relationship between genetic factors and disease outcomes (Dai & Zhang, 2024). However, a critical
125    limitation of classical mediation analysis in the context of our research is that it requires all three
126    components, i.e. exposure, mediator and outcome, to be measured within the same individual. This
127    requirement is impractical when studying how early-life behaviors impact late-life outcomes like AD, due
128    to the decades-long temporal gap and data availability constraints. In contrast, our method overcomes
129    this constraint by leveraging separately collected population datasets under different life stages, deriving
130    generative modeling to infer plausible mediating trajectories and estimate causal effects across lifespan-
131    separated variables.

132    A core part of our method is VAE. The VAE is a deep generative latent-variable model that learns a

133    probability distribution from which it can sample new data points (Kingma & Welling, 2019). VAEs

140    Finally, a substantial body of research exists on the integration of multimodal data, such as multi-omics

146    gregated dataset, which is fundamentally different from our objective of linking subjects from different
147    databases measured under various life stages “vertically” to mimic different information collection at a
148    subject-level. Our approach offers a novel method for integrating temporally and biologically disparate
149    data into a coherent, predictive framework.

       3.1 Variational Autoencoder for Intervention Analysis                                                                                       7


162    3.1       Variational Autoencoder for Intervention Analysis

163    An overview of our proposed use of a VAE for AD risk quantification is shown in Figure 2. The idea is to
164    condition a VAE on, in our case, non-modifiable/genetic factors, modifiable risk factors, and physiological
165    measurements in order to synthetically generate FC consistent with intervention on those factors. By
166    training a generative model, we can vary one particular risk factor while holding all others constant,
167    thereby disentangling the effects of each modifiable or non-modifiable risk factor.


168    3.1.1      Structure of the Risk Factor-Conditioned VAE

169    An autoencoder (AE) converts raw features into a lower-dimensional latent space via a learned encoder
170    function z = Eϕ (x), along with a decoder function to convert the latent features back into a reconstructed
171    version of the input x̂ = Dθ (z). The AE is trained to minimize the difference between the reconstruction
172    x̂ and original input x, where in our study x is the vectorized subject FC. By contrast, a VAE trains
173    the encoder function Eϕ (x) to produce latent features that approximate a known probability distribution
174    pθ (z), most often taken to be a standard multivariate Gaussian distribution pθ (z) = N (0, I) (Kingma
175    & Welling, 2014). This allows for artificially constructing latent samples zsamp from the approximated
176    distribution, followed by the conversion of those latent features to samples of the original distribution
177    pθ (x|z) by passing through the decoder function xsamp = Dθ (zsamp ). A VAE is trained by maximizing the
178    Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO). Maximizing the ELBO is equivalent to maximizing the reconstruction
179    probability logpθ (x|z) while minimizing the KL divergence between our empirical and target distributions,
180    most often the standard Gaussian distribution mentioned previously.

181    While prior research has explored conditioning VAE models on user-specified inputs (Razavi et al.,
182    2019; Sohn et al., 2015), and practical applications have demonstrated zero-shot generation capabil-
183    ities (Ramesh et al., 2021), these approaches typically lack explicit consideration of subject-specific
184    phenotypes. Similarly, VAEs have been employed to generate synthetic FC data for data augmentation
185    (J.-H. Kim et al., 2021), but often without integrating phenotype information. In contrast, our model
186    introduces a novel phenotype-aware conditioning mechanism, enabling the generation of synthetic FC
187    data that more accurately reflects individual variability and improves downstream analysis.

188    In this work, we include phenotypic features as input to the decoder function x̂ = Dθ (z, d), where z are
189    the latent features and d are the subject phenotypes. During training, we decorrelate the latent state
190    z = Eϕ (x) from phenotypes features d so that all of the FC signal that can be attributed to phenotypes
191    is based on user-provided input and not on the encoded latent features. To this end, we make several
192    modifications to the traditional VAE loss function.

251    MNI space of resting state fMRI scans was carried out using SPM12 (Friston, 2003). BOLD signal was
252    extracted using the 264 ROI Power atlas (Power et al., 2011), and the resulting timeseries were bandpass
253    filtered between 0.01 and 0.15 Hz or the highest frequency available in the data to eliminate scanner
254    drift, noise, heartbeat, and breathing signal. Pearson correlation between pairs of ROIs were taken to
255    create per-subject FC maps.

256    A total of 737 scans consisting of scans of subjects with current AD diagnosis or current cognitively normal
257    status were processed. Scans of subjects corresponding to mild cognitive impairment were excluded.
258    These scans were divided into 87 AD male, 68 AD female, 235 cognitively normal male, and 347
259    cognitively normal female scans. In order to avoid the confounding effect of sex on AD diagnosis present
260    in this dataset (relatively more AD males compared to AD females, and relatively more cognitively normal
261    AD females compared to males), a balanced dataset was created with randomly selected 68 subject scans
262    from each category, for a total of 272 scans. A linear logistic regression model was then trained using
263    the scikit-learn package (Pedregosa et al., 2011) to predict AD from the scans of these 272 subjects.
264    Subjects were then randomized and the learning process repeated for 20 bootstrap repetitions. Finally,
265    AD-predicting FC model weights from all logistic regression models were averaged to create a de-biased
266    model that was representative of the whole subset of the ADNI dataset used in this work.




267    3.3       UKB Dataset

268    The UKB is a large-scale biomedical database from over 500,000 participants, primarily collected during
269    mid-to-late adulthood. Resting state fMRI data as well as demographics, lifestyle factors, and physiologi-
270    cal measurements was collected for 40,623 UKB participants. A fraction of UKB subjects had both a first
271    and second fMRI scan, but only a single scan, the first, was used to train the VAE model. Correlation
272    between 124 fields, consisting of questionnaire fields, demographics, and physiological measurements,
273    was performed with resting state fMRI-derived FC. A set of 20 fields significantly correlated with FC
274    were selected for inclusion in the VAE model and further analysis. These fields and their summary
275    statistics are given in Supplementary Materials Table 6. A table of all correlations including field ID and

325    4.1       AD Risk Model

330    is a robust consequence of AD progression. Meanwhile, most functional connections related to the MEM

335    signal for AD prediction. Also notable is the FRNT network which is second behind the DMN in overall
336    AD risk by sum of connection weights. Supplementary Materials Table 8 shows the top ten negatively

349    4.2       Change in AD Risk Following VAE-based Intervention

350    In this section, we describe the changes in FC-mediated AD risk following intervention on non-modifiable/genetic,
351    modifiable lifestyle, and physiological measurement fields in both the UKB and HCP datasets.



352    4.2.1      Earlier-life Risk Factors Leveraging UKB Dataset


353    Figure 4 illustrates how modifiable factors and physiological measures affect AD risk via FC-mediated
354    modeling in the UKB dataset. Tobacco use has one of the strongest effects, with current smoking
355    increasing AD-like FC patterns; past smoking has a smaller but still significant impact, consistent with
356    findings linking heavy smoking to dementia risk (Zhong et al., 2015). We find that UKB-provided
357    polygenic risk scores for AD (Collister et al., 2022) are associated with higher FC-mediated AD risk, as
358    expected.

359    Low physical activity also raises FC-mediated AD risk, in line with studies showing exercise protects
360    against AD through effects on amyloid β, inflammation, and blood flow (De la Rosa et al., 2020). Time
361    spent outdoors, in both summer and winter, appears strongly protective, whereas TV viewing shows little
362    effect. Longer sleep duration increases risk, supporting findings associating over nine hours of sleep with
363    doubled AD risk (Westwood et al., 2017). Better self-rated health also lowers FC-mediated risk.

364    Among physiological factors, higher pulse rate and diabetes diagnosis significantly increase AD risk, con-
365    sistent with links between poor glucose control and earlier dementia onset (Barbiellini Amidei et al., 2021;
366    Imahori et al., 2022; Nguyen et al., 2020; Stanciu et al., 2020). The asymmetry in diabetes intervention
367    effects likely reflects the low prevalence of diabetes in the dataset. Increased waist circumference raises
368    AD risk, despite lower weight often being observed in diagnosed AD patients (Hsu et al., 2016; S. Singh

379    4.2.2      Earlier-Life Factors Leveraging HCP Dataset

380    Figure 5 shows FC-mediated AD risk changes from interventions on genetic, modifiable, and physiological

383    Age has a larger impact in HCP than UKB, likely because the same absolute age adjustment represents
384    a greater proportional change for younger individuals. Decreasing age significantly lowers AD risk,
385    while increasing it has a smaller effect. Height and sex show similar AD risk effects as in UKB, with
386    shorter stature and female sex associated with higher risk—supporting genetic links between height-
387    related pathways and neurodegeneration (Crews et al., 2010; Jørgensen et al., 2020; Larsson et al.,
388    2017; Nelson et al., 2015). Race also influences AD risk: African American subjects show slightly higher
389    FC-mediated risk than Caucasians, comparable in magnitude to the male–female difference, consistent
390    with prior findings (Barnes & Bennett, 2014).

391    Among modifiable factors, tobacco use again strongly increases AD risk. The HCP dataset also includes
392    sleep quality (PSQI), revealing that better sleep is protective, though poor sleep has a less pronounced
393    negative effect. This aligns with prior studies linking sleep disruption to AD (Gaur et al., 2022). Alcohol
394    consumption shows minimal impact on AD risk, echoing mixed findings in the literature (Seemiller et al.,
395    2024). In both datasets, weight and BMI show a strong relationship with AD risk, supporting findings
396    that obesity at any age may elevate AD susceptibility (Gu et al., 2014; Whitmer et al., 2005). Blood
397    pressure again demonstrates a U-shaped effect, both high and low values increase AD risk, suggesting that
398    deviation from normal ranges may be harmful even in healthy populations. Hand grip strength remains

408    The replication of group differences using synthetic samples leads us to believe the decoder accurately
409    captures the effect of demographics or lifestyle factors on synthetically generated FC. Moreover, this
410    highlights the main advantage of the VAE model over simply looking at group differences, i.e. the ability
411    to intervene on one specific demographic/factor and keep all others the same. This has the effect of
412    disentangling demographics that are otherwise correlated, e.g. age and height, sex and height, sex and
413    weight, etc. The built-in disentangling effects of different demographics is one of the key benefits of
414    using the VAE model that is not otherwise possible by using existing group differences in a naive way,
415    due to the correlations between demographics/factors.

437    5       Discussion

438    This project presents a novel framework for exploring how interventions on various phenotypic factors
439    influence brain FC and, in turn, the risk of AD. By leveraging a phenotype-conditioned VAE, the method
440    enables counterfactual manipulation of both modifiable and non-modifiable traits to assess their impact
441    on FC patterns associated with AD. A model of FC changes characteristic of AD was first derived using the
442    ADNI dataset. This was followed by the development of a VAE that conditions FC on individual covariates,
443    allowing simulations of how altering specific traits would affect FC and AD risk. Key findings revealed
444    that factors such as height, weight/BMI, tobacco use, and age (notably in the HCP dataset) significantly
445    influenced FC-mediated AD risk. In the UKB dataset, higher polygenic risk scores were associated with

446    increased risk, and a surprising U-shaped relationship was observed between blood pressure and AD
447    risk across both the UKB and HCP datasets. Beyond AD, the approach provides a flexible tool for
448    investigating the impact of phenotypic interventions on FC in other neurological disorders, such as
449    schizophrenia or ADHD, by adapting the underlying risk model.

450    While our current study focuses on resting-state fMRI, the underlying framework is adaptable to other
451    imaging modalities. Structural MRI, for example, has been extensively used in AD diagnosis, particularly
452    in identifying atrophy patterns in later disease stages. Applying our model to sMRI or combining it with
453    fMRI could enable multimodal inference across disease stages. Future work may also explore task-based
454    fMRI, which has shown improved sensitivity in predicting cognitive traits and clinical outcomes compared
455    to resting-state data in some studies (Zhao et al., 2023). Evaluating both modalities within the same
456    cohort could clarify their respective contributions to early AD risk modeling and assess the consistency
457    of inferred risk trajectories.

458    Another natural extension of this work is to incorporate dynamic FC, which captures time-varying changes
459    in brain connectivity and may better reflect the brain’s adaptive processes than static FC. Dynamic
460    FC has shown promise in improving classification of neuropsychiatric conditions such as schizophrenia
461    (Rashid et al., 2016), and may enhance the sensitivity of AD risk modeling to transient or subtle network
462    disruptions. Investigating whether dynamic FC yields consistent or complementary findings compared
463    to static FC would help deepen mechanistic insights into how early-life factors shape long-term brain
464    function and disease susceptibility. These future directions will further validate and extend the versatility
465    of our approach across modalities and temporal dimensions.

466    Finally, although the present study performs a rigorous integration of early-life behaviors and risk factors
467    and late-life AD risk, it would be valuable to obtain a large ground truth dataset, similar to the UKB
468    but enrolling subjects at a younger age and following them until prodromal AD symptoms appear. Given
469    the necessarily long time and resources required for this study, it is probably only possible with the
470    cooperation of a national healthcare system. Even if unlikely in the near future, such a study would be
471    the gold standard for the investigation of early-life risk factors and progression of AD in later life.

504    Simulation Experiments


505    The ability of our counterfactual VAE model to accurately uncover the relationships between latent
506    factors, an intermediate phenotype resembling FC, and outcome risk was tested in simulation experiments
507    with a known ground truth. The simulation consisted of 6 latent factors, labeled a through f , which
508    affected both the intermediate phenotype (x, y) and outcome risk y. In order to match our real FC-based
509    analysis, two synthetic datasets, DX and DY , with DX consisting of 1000 samples and DY consisting of
510    100 samples, were created. Outcome risk y was known only for the DY samples, however both x and y
511    were the same functions of the latent factors. The synthetic data was instantiated as follows:



                                                  a, b, c, d, e, f ∼ N (0, 1)
                                                                  y = a + b + a ∗ b + c2 − d
                                                                                                                                                 (9)
                                                 a, b, c, d, e, f ∼ N (0, I)
                                                              x, y = aa + bb + cc + dd + ee,


512    where x and y have 1000 features (x, y ∈ R1000 ) and are functions of a, b, c, d, e, while the outcome y (a
513    scalar) is a non-linear function of a, b, c, d. FC principal components a, b, c, d, e, f ∈ R1000 are sampled
514    once, and re-used for every synthetic subject, while a new sample of a, b, c, d, e, f is created for every
515    subject. For the simulation experiments, we assume that a, b, c, f are observed latent factors while d, e
516    are unobserved. Therefore both x, y and y are functions of both observed and unobserved latent factors.

517    Our goal in the simulation experiments was to determine the relationship between the observed latent
518    factors a, b, c, f and outcome risk y. We performed this in two ways. First, as a baseline, we regressed y
519    on observed features a, b, c, f , to determine the approximate values of a, b, c, f for each sample based on
520    the relationship between a, b, c, f and a, b, c, f and y. Then, assuming outcome risk y was a second-order
521    polynomial in a, b, c, f , we regressed the outcome risk y on the coefficients determined in the previous
522    regression, thereby estimating the functional relationship between y and the latent factors a, b, c, f .
523    Regression was performed using the Ridge model of scikit-learn package using three different values of
524    the regularization parameter α Pedregosa et al., 2011. The initial regression coefficients corresponding
525    to a through f were estimated on the larger dataset DX , then evaluated for the dataset DY for which
526    outcome risk y was available.

527    After determining the coefficients using the baseline two-stage regression, we then attempted to determine
528    the coefficients of outcome risk based on latent factors using the intervention VAE model. We began
         medRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.09.16.25335925; this version posted September 18, 2025. The copyright holder for this
      preprint (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted medRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity.

                                           It is made available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license .

                                                                                                                                                  37


529    by fitting an MLP on DY to predict y from y. The MLP was a simple two-layer function with a size
530    30 hidden layer. The MLP was trained for 2000 epochs using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate
531    of 10−4 and L2 regularization of 10−5 . Then, we trained the intervention VAE on the dataset DX
532    conditioned upon observed latent factors a, b, c, f . Once the VAE model was trained, we intervened on
533    each of the latent factors in turn and determine the change in counterfactual outcome risk ŷ compared to
534    y before intervention. We then fit coefficients to each of the latent factors, assuming the true function is
535    a second-degree polynomial in the latent factors. The mean squared error of the coefficients determined
536    using VAE was then compared to the coefficients found using the two-stage regression approach outlined
537    above. The synthetic data was simulated ten times and coefficients were estimated for each simulation.



538    Simulation Results


539    The results of our simulation experiments are shown in Figure 10. We find that due to the ability of
540    the VAE to create a highly reliable model on the larger synthetic dataset DX , the ability to estimate
541    coefficients for y from DY was greatly enhanced. The VAE was able to correctly estimate those coef-
542    ficients (a, b, a ∗ b, c2 ) that were non-zero and set to zero those coefficients which were observed but
543    not important for outcome risk (f ). On the other hand, the two-stage regression we were comparing
544    against was highly dependent on the regularization hyperparameter used for the regression. Using a large
545    regularization hyperparameter α = 100, the two-stage regression was able to set f to be correctly equal
546    to zero, but the other coefficients were found to be too small, around or less than 0.5 instead of the
547    ground truth of one. Decreasing the regularization parameter allowed the coefficients of a, b, a ∗ b, c2
548    to reach their correct values, but with the downside that f was no longer exactly zero but had a dis-
549    tribution of values around zero. In any case, we found the variance of the coefficients estimated using
550    two-stage regression to be much larger than for the interventional VAE model. Mean squared error for
551    the coefficients for the VAE model and each two-stage regression are given in Table 10.
