# FSLNets

**FSLNets** (FSL Networks) is a toolbox within the [[fsl|FSL]] suite for whole-brain [[functional-connectivity]] analysis from resting-state [[fmri|fMRI]] data. Developed at the Oxford FMRIB group, it provides an end-to-end pipeline — from node definition and timeseries extraction through network modeling to group-level statistical inference — enabling researchers to characterize the brain's [[intrinsic-connectivity-networks|intrinsic functional architecture]]. The toolbox is one of the most widely adopted frameworks for data-driven network analysis in human neuroimaging and serves as a key empirical reference point for computational models of [[brain-dynamics]].

## Motivation and Context

The analysis of [[resting-state-fmri]] data poses a series of interconnected challenges: how to define the nodes of a brain network, how to estimate the edges connecting them, and how to test hypotheses about group differences or clinical associations in a statistically rigorous manner. Before FSLNets, researchers typically assembled ad-hoc pipelines combining separate tools for [[ica|independent component analysis]] (ICA), correlation estimation, and permutation testing — a workflow that was both labor-intensive and prone to methodological inconsistency across studies. FSLNets integrated these stages into a single, coherent framework, reducing the barriers to reproducible [[connectomics]] research and promoting standardization in the field.

The toolbox is designed around the observation that [[functional-connectivity]] can be meaningfully decomposed into a set of spatially distinct resting-state networks — such as the [[default-mode-network]], sensorimotor, and visual networks — whose temporal correlations reflect ongoing neural communication. By providing automated tools to extract these networks and quantify their inter-relationships at both individual and group levels, FSLNets bridges data-driven [[brain-parcellation]] with hypothesis-driven statistical inference. Unlike atlas-based approaches that impose predefined anatomical boundaries, FSLNets uses ICA to discover network nodes from the data itself, avoiding the potential mismatch between anatomical parcellations and functional organization.

## Key Features

### Dual-Regression Framework for Subject-Specific Networks

At the core of FSLNets is a **dual regression** procedure applied to group-level spatial ICA maps generated by [[fsl-melodic|MELODIC]]. The procedure operates in two sequential stages. In stage one, the set of group-level spatial maps is used as spatial regressors in a multiple regression against each subject's 4D fMRI data, yielding subject-specific timeseries for each independent component. In stage two, these subject-specific component timeseries are used as temporal regressors in a second multiple regression against the same 4D data, recovering subject-specific spatial maps.

This two-step approach solves a fundamental problem in group ICA: how to obtain individual-level representations from group-level decompositions without re-running ICA separately on each subject — which introduces component ordering and correspondence ambiguities. Because both regressions are simple [[linear]] models fit by ordinary least squares, dual regression is computationally efficient and numerically stable even for high-dimensional data. The resulting per-subject spatial maps and timeseries preserve the group-level component identities, enabling direct statistical comparison of network amplitudes, spatial extents, and temporal dynamics across individuals and groups.

### Flexible Network Edge Estimation

FSLNets supports three classes of network edge estimation from the extracted node timeseries:

1. **Full correlation** — the simple Pearson correlation between every pair of node timeseries, producing a symmetric [[connectome|connectivity]] matrix capturing both direct and indirect statistical dependencies. This is the most common choice in [[resting-state]] [[functional-connectivity]] studies due to its simplicity and robustness to limited data.
2. **Partial correlation** — the correlation between each pair of nodes after regressing out the influence of all other nodes, approximating direct functional interactions. This yields sparser, more interpretable networks but requires more timepoints than nodes — a condition frequently violated at fine [[parcellation]] resolutions.
3. **Regularized partial correlation** — a penalized estimation approach (using L1 or L2 regularization) that stabilizes the partial correlation estimate when the number of nodes approaches or exceeds the number of timepoints. This addresses a common practical limitation in high-resolution [[brain-parcellation]] schemes and is particularly relevant for analyses using dense cortical parcellations with hundreds of nodes.

### Group-Level Statistical Inference

FSLNets implements nonparametric permutation testing for group-level inference on network matrices, accounting for the multiple-comparison problem inherent in testing every edge of a dense connectome matrix. The framework supports both single-group (testing against zero or a null model) and two-group (case-control) designs, with familywise error correction via maximum-statistic permutation. This approach is closely related to the inference engine of [[fsl-randomise|FSL Randomise]] and complements tools like [[palm|PALM]]. The permutation framework makes no distributional assumptions about the [[connectivity]] measures, making it robust to the non-Gaussian distributions often observed in correlation-based edge weights.

### Visualization and Reporting

The toolbox generates publication-ready visualizations including weighted-spring-embedded node-edge plots, circular connectome ring diagrams, and matrix heatmaps. These outputs enable direct visual comparison of network structure across groups and facilitate communication of results. Similar visualization capabilities are available in the [[connectome-workbench]] and the [[brain-connectivity-toolbox]].

## Relationship to TVB

FSLNets serves as a critical bridge between empirical [[neuroimaging]] and computational modeling with [[tvb|The Virtual Brain]] (TVB). TVB simulations generate synthetic BOLD timeseries from a [[structural-connectivity]] matrix coupled with a [[neural-mass-model]]; FSLNets provides the complementary empirical pipeline — deriving [[functional-connectivity]] matrices from real resting-state fMRI data that can serve as ground-truth validation targets for TVB simulations.

A common integrative workflow proceeds as follows: FSLNets is used to compute group-average functional connectivity from a cohort such as the [[human-connectome-project]], typically extracting ICA-based network nodes and estimating full or partial correlation edges. These empirical FC matrices are then compared against FC matrices computed from TVB simulations, and model parameters — such as the global coupling strength or regional excitability parameters in a [[neural-mass-model]] — are tuned to maximize the similarity between simulated and empirical connectivity patterns. This model-fitting pipeline, which underpins the emerging field of [[personalized-brain-modeling]], depends on FSLNets to provide reliable, reproducible empirical targets.

Furthermore, the ICA-based [[brain-parcellation]] produced by FSLNets can directly inform the regional parcellation used in TVB's region-based modeling. When the same network nodes are used for both empirical FC estimation and simulated FC computation, comparisons between model output and empirical data are made on identical spatial footings, eliminating a common source of methodological inconsistency. The dual-regression procedure's ability to produce subject-specific network representations also enables single-subject model fitting, which is essential for personalized modeling applications in clinical contexts such as [[epilepsy-modeling]].

## Relationship to Other Approaches

FSLNets occupies a specific position within the landscape of functional connectivity analysis tools. Compared to [[gift|GIFT]], which implements group ICA via temporal concatenation and back-reconstruction, FSLNets' dual-regression approach has the advantage of directly producing subject-specific spatial maps without requiring component matching across subjects, at the cost of being more sensitive to the quality of the group-level ICA decomposition. Relative to seed-based correlation analysis — where connectivity is measured from a user-defined region of interest — FSLNets' ICA-driven node definition avoids the arbitrariness and potential bias of seed selection, though at the cost of interpretability: ICA components may mix functionally distinct signals into a single node.

In the context of whole-brain modeling, FSLNets-derived functional connectivity is distinct from [[effective-connectivity]] measures obtained through [[dynamic-causal-modeling]] (DCM). While FSLNets captures statistical dependencies (undirected functional connectivity), DCM estimates directed causal influences between regions using a generative model of neural dynamics coupled to a [[hemodynamic-response-function|hemodynamic forward model]]. The two approaches are complementary: FSLNets is typically used for exploratory characterization of network architecture and as a validation target, while DCM is used for hypothesis-driven inference about directed interactions and their modulation by experimental conditions.

## Limitations

Several methodological considerations constrain the interpretation of FSLNets results. The ICA decomposition assumes that resting-state networks are spatially independent, a premise that is approximately valid for large-scale networks such as the default-mode and visual networks but breaks down for networks with overlapping spatial distributions. The choice of ICA dimensionality — how many components to extract — is a critical free parameter: too few components merge distinct networks, while too many split genuine networks into artificial sub-components. FSLNets provides heuristic guidance based on the Laplace approximation to the model evidence, but the optimal dimensionality remains a subject of ongoing debate. Additionally, the dual-regression framework can introduce spatial distortions in subject-specific maps when the group-level ICA decomposition does not adequately represent individual variation in network topography, a concern that is partially mitigated by the use of regularized estimation approaches.
