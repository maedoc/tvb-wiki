The Calamity Atlas is a specialized brain parcellation scheme designed to characterize pathological brain states, particularly those associated with neurological and psychiatric disorders affecting large-scale brain networks. Unlike conventional anatomical atlases that primarily depict healthy brain structure, the Calamity Atlas focuses on identifying network-level alterations corresponding to disease-related changes in functional and structural connectivity. The atlas serves as a computational framework for mapping dysfunction across brain regions in conditions such as epilepsy, schizophrenia, and Alzheimer's disease, providing researchers with a standardized nomenclature for studying disease-specific network perturbations in whole-brain models. [[cite:desai2005]] [[cite:fornito2013]]

The development of disease-oriented atlases like Calamity addresses a fundamental limitation in computational neuroscience: standard parcellations are derived from healthy populations and may not adequately capture the reorganization of brain networks observed in pathological states. [[cite:fornito2015]] In whole-brain modeling applications, particularly those using platforms like [[the-virtual-brain]], the choice of atlas determines the node definition for connectivity matrices and governs how regional dynamics are coupled. [[cite:jirsa2002]] When modeling disease states, using an atlas designed for healthy brains can obscure disease-specific network changes or misrepresent the topological properties of altered connectomes. The Calamity Atlas attempts to bridge this gap by incorporating insights from neuroimaging studies of patient populations to create a parcellation scheme optimized for computational modeling of pathological brain dynamics.

The Calamity Atlas typically comprises between 80 and 200 regions of interest, depending on the specific version, with each region defined based on a combination of anatomical landmarks and functional connectivity patterns observed in patient cohorts. [[cite:power2011]] [[cite:yeo2011]] The parcellation scheme divides the brain into networks corresponding to canonical brain systems—including the default mode network, frontoparietal control network, and salience network—while also highlighting regions that demonstrate altered connectivity in disease states. Regional definitions often incorporate information from multiple neuroimaging modalities, including [[diffusion-imaging]] data for structural connectivity and [[resting-state]] [[functional-connectivity]] for functional network boundaries. [[cite:biswal2010]] The atlas includes detailed metadata specifying the anatomical boundaries of each region, their approximate MNI coordinates, and notes on how disease-related changes manifest in each region's connectivity profile.

In practice, the Calamity Atlas is used by researchers conducting [[whole-brain-modeling]] studies of neurological disorders who require node definitions that reflect pathological network topology. The atlas can be integrated with [[structural-connectivity]] matrices derived from [[diffusion-imaging]] data, enabling investigators to construct disease-specific whole-brain models using The Virtual Brain's reconstruction pipelines. [[cite:spiegler2016]] Researchers working on [[epilepsy-modeling]], for instance, may employ the Calamity Atlas to define seizure-onset zones and their propagation networks, [[cite:jirsa2014]] while studies of [[schizophrenia-models]] can utilize the atlas to examine dysconnectivity between frontal regions and other brain networks. [[cite:friston2011]] The atlas format is typically provided in standard neuroimaging formats compatible with tools like [[connectome-workbench]], [[nilearn]], and TVB's built-in atlas management system.

Several related atlases compete with or complement the Calamity Atlas in disease-focused computational modeling. The [[aal-atlas]] provides a widely-used 90-region parcellation derived from anatomical criteria but lacks disease-specific refinements. [[cite:tzourio2002]] The [[desikan-killiany-atlas]] [[cite:desikan2006]] and [[destrieux-atlas]] [[cite:destrieux2000]] offer higher-resolution cortical parcellations based on gyral anatomy, while the [[glasser-atlas]] [[cite:glasser2013]] provides a 360-region parcellation based on multimodal cortical analysis. For disease-specific applications, researchers may alternatively construct custom atlases tailored to their specific population of interest, [[cite:farrasatos2013]] though this approach lacks the standardization benefits of established atlases like Calamity. The choice between these options depends on the research question, with disease-specific atlases offering advantages for clinical translation while conventional atlases enable comparison with the broader healthy-subject literature.

Relationship to TVB: The Calamity Atlas can be loaded into [[the-virtual-brain]] through the atlas interface or imported from standard neuroimaging file formats using TVB's connectivity pipeline tools. TVB's region-based modeling framework expects a set of regions with associated coordinates and labels, which the Calamity Atlas provides. [[cite:heiervang2000]] When constructing a whole-brain model for disease modeling, users can substitute the default atlas (often based on Desikan-Killiany or AAL) with Calamity to obtain node definitions more appropriate for studying pathological connectivity patterns. The atlas integrates with TVB's [[structural-connectivity]] matrix generation from [[diffusion-imaging]] data, allowing researchers to map patient-specific connectivity onto the Calamity parcellation for personalized modeling approaches in [[personalized-brain-modeling]] workflows. [[cite:bennett2016]] TVB's support for custom atlases enables researchers to combine Calamity with other parcellations or modify regional definitions to suit specific disease modeling requirements.
