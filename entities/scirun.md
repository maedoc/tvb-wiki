# SCIRun

## Overview

SCIRun (Scientific Computing and Imaging Institute Run) is a problem-solving environment (PSE) developed primarily at the University of Utah's Scientific Computing and Imaging Institute for forward and inverse modeling in bioelectric fields. It is an open-source software platform designed to simulate, analyze, and visualize electromagnetic fields generated by biological tissues in the human body. While often associated with electroencephalography (EEG) and magnetoencephalography (MEG) source localization problems, SCIRun's scope extends more broadly to general bioelectric field problems including cardiac modeling, neuromuscular simulation, and other applications where electric fields arise from physiological activity.

SCIRun provides an integrated environment for solving the ill-posed inverse problem of estimating neural or cardiac sources from measurements of electric potentials on body surfaces and magnetic fields outside the body. The software emerged from the bioengineering and neuroimaging research community's need for a modular, extensible framework that could handle the complete pipeline from geometric modeling of tissues to source reconstruction. Unlike monolithic commercial packages, SCIRun adopts a dataflow architecture where users construct computational networks by connecting modular components called "modules" and "packages," enabling flexible customization of analysis pipelines while maintaining scientific rigor.

## Key Features

SCIRun's architecture centers on a modular dataflow system where users build processing networks by visually connecting specialized computing modules. The software includes extensive capabilities for **forward modeling**, which computes the electromagnetic fields that would be produced by specified sources given a model of geometry and conductivity. This involves solving the Poisson equation for the electric potential using boundary element methods (BEM) or finite element methods (FEM), and computing the external magnetic field using Lead Field theory. The forward solver can handle realistic head models derived from structural MRI data, including compartmental models with different conductivity values for scalp, skull, cerebrospinal fluid, and brain tissue.

For **inverse modeling**, SCIRun implements various source localization algorithms including minimum norm estimates and weighted minimum norm solutions. Users can impose different constraints on the source reconstruction, such as smoothness priors or sparse solutions assuming a limited number of active sources. The software also supports cortically constrained inverse solutions where source estimation is restricted to the cortical sheet, taking advantage of anatomical information from FreeSurfer or similar segmentation tools.

A particularly notable feature is SCIRun's integration with 3D Slicer, which provides sophisticated visualization and interaction capabilities for three-dimensional anatomical models. Users can visualize source solutions overlaid on cortical surfaces, examine the geometry of head models, and interactively explore the relationship between hypothesized source locations and measured data. The software also includes the **BioMesh3D** system for generating tetrahedral volume meshes from segmented medical imaging data, enabling finite element forward computations in realistic geometries.

## Relationship to TVB

While [[the-virtual-brain]] (TVB) and SCIRun both address computational neuroscience questions related to [[brain-dynamics]], they serve complementary roles rather than overlapping functionalities. TVB is primarily a [[whole-brain|whole-brain modeling]] simulator that uses [[neural-mass-models]] to generate dynamic patterns of activity across large-scale brain networks, often driven by empirical [[functional-connectivity]] data. SCIRun, in contrast, focuses on the electromagnetic inverse problem—what can be inferred about current neural activity from EEG/MEG measurements.

However, the two platforms can be productively combined in multi-scale modeling workflows. SCIRun's source localization capabilities can be used to estimate the cortical current distributions underlying observed EEG/MEG data, which can then serve as initial conditions or boundary constraints for TVB simulations. Conversely, TVB forward modeling can generate predicted electromagnetic signatures from simulated network activity, which can be compared against empirical recordings through SCIRun's visualization tools. This integration is particularly valuable for studying the relationship between [[resting-state]] [[network-dynamics]] measured with EEG or MEG and the underlying large-scale brain [[connectivity]] structure that TVB models. The forward model capabilities in both systems share theoretical foundations in electromagnetic field theory, though they differ in implementation scale and computational approach.

## Historical Context and Development

SCIRun was developed at the University of Utah under the direction of Chris R. Johnson and later Rob MacLeod, beginning in the late 1990s. The software emerged from the strong tradition of bioelectric field research at Utah, which included pioneering work on boundary element methods for EEG and MEG forward problems. The original development was supported by the National Institutes of Health and the National Science Foundation, reflecting the software's significance to the biomedical computing community.

A significant milestone was the integration of inverse simulation capabilities that allowed researchers to not only solve forward problems but to systematically investigate the properties and limitations of inverse algorithms through controlled simulation studies. This "inverse crime" approach, while recognized as methodologically problematic for validating inverse methods in practice, nonetheless provided valuable insights into algorithmic behavior and numerical properties (Parker, 1977).

The development philosophy emphasized openness and extensibility, with SCIRun's modular architecture allowing researchers to contribute new algorithms and visualization methods. Several notable packages grew from or integrated with SCIRun, including tools for cardiac modeling—reflecting the broader bioelectric field applications beyond neuroimaging—and the integration with 3D Slicer for medical imaging visualization. The BioMesh3D system emerged specifically to address the challenge of generating high-quality tetrahedral meshes from segmented imaging data, a critical step for finite element forward computations in realistic geometries (Baum & Trew, 2014).

While SCIRun's active development has slowed in recent years as commercial and other open-source alternatives have matured, it remains an important educational tool and reference implementation for bioelectric field computation. The software's visual programming paradigm influenced subsequent tools in the field, and its documentation and algorithmic implementations continue to serve as resources for researchers learning forward and inverse methods.

## Related Software

SCIRun exists in an ecosystem of tools for electromagnetic source imaging. For forward and inverse EEG/MEG problems, alternatives include FieldTrip (a MATLAB toolbox popular in the EEG/MEG community), MNE-Python (a more recent Python-based platform), and commercial packages such as BESA or ASA. For anatomical segmentation and mesh generation, FreeSurfer, 3D Slicer, and [[mrtrix3]] serve related purposes. The comparison between SCIRun and these alternatives often centers on the trade-off between SCIRun's visual programming approach and the script-based workflow of MATLAB or Python alternatives.

For cardiac modeling specifically, which represents SCIRun's broader application domain, related tools include the Cardiac Atlas Project, CMISS, and EBECS. The boundary element and finite element methods implemented in SCIRun for bioelectric field problems share theoretical foundations with these cardiac modeling environments, though application-specific implementations differ in their handling of tissue heterogeneity and boundary conditions.
